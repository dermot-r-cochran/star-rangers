Ariel Pinya writes stories about the Star Rangers for people who will never meet one. They are sold where such things are sold: the kiosk racks at habitat docks and transit concourses, in short print runs that go out of print and come back, between the puzzle books and the almanacs. The series is called *Called In*, after the beat every story in it turns on, and there are, at the last count the Archive holds, twenty-six of them.

The record holds no residence for Pinya and no portrait, and she has declined both of the interviews the Archive knows to have been requested. What is on file is the work, a publisher's address that is a forwarding address, and one line from an author's note that has been quoted more often than anything in the stories: *I have never served and I have never been called in, and I write about it because the people who have will not.*

## What the Stories Get Right

A *Called In* story begins somewhere ordinary. A habitat corridor, a hydroponics deck, a grain store on a charter world, a school. Something in it is a little wrong and has been for a while, and the people who live with it have made their arrangements around the wrongness the way people do. A local detective is sent, finds a case that is not the shape of a case, and the case goes up until someone issues the invitation. Then a Ranger arrives.

What arrives is the thing Pinya gets right, and it is the reason the Corps reads the stories. The Ranger comes with an instrument, a page and a question, and not much else. The Ranger does not explain the wrongness. The Ranger measures it, names what can be named, writes down the rest as not yet established, and leaves the people with a better-lit version of the thing they already had. Nothing in a *Called In* story is ever solved in the sense a reader of detective fiction expects, and the ones that come closest are the ones Pinya's readers like least. Reviewers have called the endings unresolved. She has called them finished.

The Archive's own word for the shape is *marked*, and Pinya does not use it, which is one of several signs she has not read the doctrine and arrived at it by another road.

## What the Stories Get Wrong

Everything institutional. Pinya's Rangers hold naval ranks the Corps has never used: there is a Commander in every story and a Captain above her who is never seen. They carry a sidearm, which no Ranger carries, and across twenty-six stories not one of them has ever drawn it, which a Survey Corps reviewer called *the most accurate thing about them*. They swear an oath the Corps does not have, in words Pinya has kept the same in every book and which some recruits have been found to know by heart before their first posting. In the early stories they arrive uninvited, walking into a self-governing habitat as though a charter gave them the right, which it does not; by the eleventh story the invitation has become the hinge of every plot, and she has never said whether anyone wrote to the publisher in between.

The Archive files the stories in the [Codex](/star-rangers/codex/) for the same reason it files [Kate Ukrainka](/star-rangers/characters/kate-ukrainka/)'s: as one author's account, valid for its author and stated as such. Where a *Called In* story describes the Corps, the record is at [The Founding of the Star Rangers](/star-rangers/lore/formation-of-star-rangers/) and [The Command Hierarchy](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-command-hierarchy/), and the two disagree on every point of procedure. Where a story describes what it is to be called in to something that will not explain itself, the record has no quarrel with it.

## Who Reads Them

Rangers do. The stories are passed hand to hand on survey postings and turn up in the shared shelves of monitoring stations, and the Corps' own joke is that a new Deputy can be dated by which story she thinks is the good one. Nobody in the Corps has publicly claimed to have met the author, and the one Ranger the Archive knows to have written to the publisher received a reply from the forwarding address, typed, that thanked her for the correction and did not say which one.

## Character Notes

- Pinya appears in no chapter. The character exists in the record as an author exists in a library: by the work, and by what the Archive has been able to establish about it, which is little.
- The one quoted line of her author's note is the only sentence of Pinya's own on file outside the fiction.
