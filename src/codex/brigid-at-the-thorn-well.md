**AS THE ROAD TELLS IT**
**Taken down at Cill Aoife on the eve of her feast, in the house's book, by the one who keeps it. The telling is not the house's.**

---

She asked the house to say nothing of her that she had not said, and the house has not. The road was never asked.

They come on the eve of her feast, more of them each year and from further off, and they tell it to one another at the water while they wait their turn. There is nobody in the house now who saw her living. The last who did was a child in the well-house in the fever year, and we buried her old the winter before this one. So I have taken the telling down while there is still a house that knows where it parts from her, and I have marked the places under it. This is how it was told this year by a woman from beyond the river, who had it from her mother. I have not mended it.

### The Servant Girl

There was a servant girl at this well long ago, and Aoife was her name, and she had nothing. No father's house, no cow, no shoe that was made for her own foot. She carried water for the house above, and she sat with the sick that came, because sitting with them was work nobody else wanted and she was let do it.

Now you know who else was a servant. Brigid's mother was a bondwoman, and Brigid was reared at the churn, and gave away the butter as fast as she made it, and the crock was never short. So when Brigid goes the roads on the eve of a feast, and she does, it is not at the lord's door she turns in.

The girl had a way with the sick, and it was no cure. Mind that. She had no herb the next woman didn't have. What she had was that she could sit by a person and want nothing off them, not even that they would get better, and the sick know that in a person, and it lets them rest. The old people say a fever would ease in a room she sat in the way an argument stops when someone comes in who has no side in it.

### The Eve of the Feast

It was the eve of the feast, at the start of spring, and the girl was at the well by herself in the dark, minding it, the way she did every year.

And Brigid came. Who else comes to a well?

She did not come up the path. The girl looked, and the air over the water was a cloak being shaken out. You have heard what Brigid's cloak did at Kildare, when the king said she could have what ground it would cover, and it went on unfolding until it had covered the plain. It was that cloak. It came out over the well fold after fold, with no seam in it and no tear, and the clearing was too small for it, and it fitted in the clearing all the same.

And the woman that wore it was standing at the well. And she was standing in Kildare beside her fire, the same minute. And she was at every well in Ireland that has her name on it, and that is a great many wells. She was not three women or a hundred. She was the one woman, near enough to touch and away past the hills, and the girl saw the near and the far of her together, and it did her no harm to see it.

Brigid spoke to her. Not out loud. The girl said after that she could never tell which side of her the voice was on. And she told the girl three things about herself that the girl had told nobody. What they were I can't tell you. She never said. Anyone who tells you what they were is telling you what they would like to have been told themselves.

Then Brigid looked at her the way a woman looks at a loaf to see is it done. And she gave her nothing. That is the part they won't believe at the big wells. No bell, no staff, no corner of the cloak. She said, *You'll do. You were doing already.* And the cloak folded back the way it had come, and there was only the well and the thorn and the dark.

But she had left a coal of her fire under the water. You know Brigid's fire, that the women keep at Kildare, that is never let out and leaves no ash. The well was warm for three days. Put your hand in it now and it is as cold as any well. It was warm for three days, and it never was before, and it never has been since.

### What She Would Say About It

The sick that were washed in the warm water got well, more of them than you would expect. And the people said Brigid had healed the girl of whatever ailed her, and given her the healing to hand out. Why would she not? It is what you would say.

The girl would not have it. Any time it was said in front of her, to the end of her life, and she lived to be old, she said the one thing. *I was shown. I was not healed.* The old people had it from the people who heard her, and they all give it the same.

I don't know what she meant. I have asked at this well every year I have come, and nobody could tell me. But I will say this much. Brigid mended nothing in her because there was nothing in her to mend, only something to be seen, and it was seen. That is my own. Don't put it on her.

And she said the one who came was not like the carving. The stone over the shrine above; you will pass it going up. Well, how would it be like? A man cut that stone who never saw Brigid.

They came for her after, from the great houses, three times, to put her where she could be minded. She stayed a servant at the well. She is under the thorn. Wash your hands, and say her name and Brigid's, and go home.

---

### Where It Parts From Her

*In the hand that took it down.*

*The road says* Brigid. *She said* Muirenn, *whose eve it was and whose well this is, and Daire wrote it as she said it. The Sister is ours, and small, and a day's walk from this house nobody has heard of her. The road is long, and there is a well of Brigid's on every day of it. I do not think anyone chose to change the name. The people who knew the Sister's name stayed at home, and the people who came knew the other.*

*The road says the stone is a poor likeness of Brigid. It is a likeness of Muirenn, good or poor. She said that what came resembled nothing of it. The road has an answer for why. She had none, and I do not find that she looked for one.*

*The road says* servant, *and gives her Brigid's own beginnings to go with the word. Daire has* daughter of no house the record troubles itself to name, *and I add nothing to him. She carried water. So does everyone here.*

*The road gives the visitor a cloak, a fire and a coal. She gave none of them. She said the air folded* as *cloth folds. She did not say there was cloth.*

*The road says* You'll do. *She gave no words to what was said to her, to Daire or to anyone I can still ask.*

*The road says she was healed, then says she denied it, then washes its hands in the water. I have no quarrel with the washing. She asked that the well be kept for the sick.*

*One thing the road has kept that I did not expect of it.* I was shown. I was not healed. *It is word for word. The teller did not understand it, and said so, and did not mend it, and what she put beside it she called her own. Daire wrote the same of himself.*

*I have not corrected the telling, and it will not be read in the house. It is entered so that whoever comes after can tell her from what is said of her.*

*Scholarly annotation (M. Ó Fiaich, Union Cultural Archive, Tír na nÓg, 2819 UCSD): This leaf is the earliest witness to a second name for what Aoife met, and the name is not hers. Read against [Levrils: Dragons and Constraint Literacy](/star-rangers/lore/levrils/), the change is less strange than it looks. A Levril has no form a time-bound observer can receive, so the perceiver supplies one, and an account of a meeting tells a careful reader more about the witness than about what was met. Aoife supplied Muirenn, because the eve and the well were Muirenn's, and said as she named it that the fit was poor. The road, two generations on, supplied Brigid, because every well it knew was hers. If what came to the thorn well was a Levril, as the newer reading holds it may have been, then neither name was ever the datum, and a tradition that changes the name has changed only what the witnesses brought with them. The leaf's keeper could not have known to look for the other thing it shows. The three details the newer reading rests on came through the change of name, each in Brigid's dress: the air that folded as cloth folds and did not tear is a cloak with no seam; the presence at every distance at once is one woman at every well in Ireland; the well that ran warm for three days is a coal of the Kildare fire. Brigid's attributes fit those three better than Muirenn's ever did, which may be why the road's name held. The Union carried both names off Earth: Muirenn's eve is still Aoife's feast on every charter world, and the founders of [Drithane](/star-rangers/lore/planets/drithane/) named their star for Brigid of the hearth and the forge. The fit needs its caution. A costume that fits is evidence about the tailor. That a well-loved story kept its three strangest details shows the care of its tellers, and nothing about what stood in the clearing. The leaf widens the range of names. It does not narrow the range of what was named.*

*Archive note: this leaf is a pilgrims' telling, valid for its tellers, and the hand that entered it says where it leaves the* [Life](/star-rangers/codex/life-of-saint-aoife/)*. The record's one contemporary account is Brother Daire's, and in it Aoife names what she met Muirenn, the well's own patroness, and says in the same breath that it resembled nothing of Muirenn's carving. Nobody in the* Life *names Brigid, and nothing in it calls Aoife a servant. The leaf carries no year. Its keeper's statement that the last of the house to have seen Aoife living was lately buried places it about two generations after her death in 1241 AD (1441 UCSD), near the turn of the fourteenth century, and the Archive fixes it no closer. The cloak, the coal and the words given to the visitor are the teller's. The record files no miracle at the well, in this telling or in any other, and registers the three days' warmth as the* Life *reports it and no further. The annotation's reading is the Union scholars', offered as a possibility and filed as one. The Archive's position is unchanged: what Aoife met at the thorn well, and whether it held any Cascade standing at all, has never been settled, and a second name for it settles nothing. The Ceridwen Archive shelves the leaf after* [What Was Carried](/star-rangers/codex/what-was-carried/)*, whose author asked the later hand not to supply what the morning withheld. This later hand supplied nothing, and marked what the road had.*
