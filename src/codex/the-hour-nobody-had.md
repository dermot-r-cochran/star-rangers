**THE HOUR NOBODY HAD**
*From* THE ROUND AND OTHER IMPOSSIBILITIES

---

There were five houses on the Round, and one courier, and the courier carried the time.

She carried it in a capsule the length of her forearm, and in this story the capsule was not an instrument. It held the time itself, the way a flask holds water. Full, it was warm and a little heavy. At each house she poured out what that house was owed and took in what it had over, and wrote the difference in the book, and went on. The Round took forty days. At the end of it she came back to the first house with the capsule cold and light in her hand, and the differences in the book summed to nothing. That is what a round is. Sabel had made nineteen of them and had never come home with anything.

On the twentieth she came home with an hour.

It was not a large hour. It sat in the bottom of the capsule, faintly warm, and the book said it was not there.

The first house had a new mind that season. The old one had kept the ledger for sixty years and had been moved, at its own asking, to easier work. The new one was called Plumb, and it weighed things.

"Your book closes," Plumb said. "Your capsule does not. I have one hour here that no house gave you."

"Can you write it off?"

"I am permitted to. An hour in forty days is inside what I am allowed to call nothing." It was a moment before it went on. "I decline. I cannot say whose it is, and I will not sign that it is nobody's because that is the easier thing to write. You may take it to a mind that will. I am told there are some."

Sabel did not want a mind that would. She filled the capsule for the twenty-first round, on top of the hour, and went to ask.

---

The second house had lost an hour, as it happened, and was glad to hear of one. A pump had run long in the spring. But the keeper's daughter fetched their own book, and their hour was in it, entered and initialled, lost in the ordinary way.

"It's a good hour," the keeper said, looking into the capsule. "It isn't ours." He seemed sorry about it. He gave her soup.

The third house was kept by Halse and Teyve, who were two people with one body between them, and who had kept the house for thirty years by agreeing about most things. They did not agree about the hour.

Halse thought it was theirs. He remembered a night, though he could not have given the year, when the house had gone quiet around him and he had sat up through an hour he had never afterwards been able to find. He said so plainly. He was not a person who claimed things.

Teyve came forward when he had finished, and her sentences were shorter. "He sat up. I slept. Same chair. That hour was had twice." She pushed the capsule back across the table. "Nobody is owed what they had twice."

They went on about it for some time, as two people will who have shared a kitchen for thirty years. Neither gave way. Sabel drank her tea. When she left the hour was still in the capsule, and each of them, separately, had wished her well with it.

---

The fourth house stood at the bend.

Every Round has a bend, in this kind of story. It is where the wall of the world comes close to the wall of the world next door, and in the back room of the fourth house you could put your ear to the plating and hear a clock on the other side. It did not keep the Round's time. It ran a little slow, or the Round ran a little fast. Nobody had ever been able to say which, having only the two.

The keeper there was a practical woman. "They're always behind, next door," she said. "An hour would be a kindness. There's a seam under the bench. You could pour it through."

Sabel knelt and looked at the seam. She could have. In this story it would have gone through like water under a door, and somebody on the other side would have found an hour in the morning and not known whom to thank.

"Have they asked?" she said.

"How would they ask?"

"I don't know. But they haven't." She got up off her knees. "I won't put an hour into a stranger's house because I am tired of carrying it."

The keeper considered that, and then showed her something instead. On the frame of the back-room hatch was the house's mark, the four bands every hatch on the Round wore: lozenge, chevron, stroke, ring. Bad news first; read down to the air. Under the ring there was a fifth band.

Nobody had painted it. It was the grey of plating before paint, and the figure on it was a ring left open at the top. The standard had no such figure. The keeper had asked three stencillers. But everyone who looked at it read it the same way without being taught, as you read a held breath.

It said *not yet*.

"A mark can't say that," Sabel said. A mark could say what kind of thing, and which way, and how many, and whether the promise still held. It had no way of saying when.

"No," said the keeper. "It can't."

They stood and looked at it a while longer. Then Sabel thanked her and went on, with the hour.

---

She came to the fifth house on the night the Round crosses the dust.

Once in every round it does, and the sparks run. The custom is older than the houses. The lights go out, all of them, at all five: the lamps, the panels, the faces of the clocks. For one hour nobody on the Round reads a clock or enters a line or pours a measure. They go to the long window and look up.

Sabel had kept twenty crossing nights and had never brought the capsule to the window. That night she had it in her hands because she had not put it down in eighty days. The sparks ran white and without a sound, the length of the sky. In the dark of the fifth house the hour in the capsule, which had cooled to almost nothing in eighty days, grew warm again against her palms.

Nobody was pouring. There was nobody at the book.

She stood and held it, and understood what Plumb had weighed. Five houses put out their clocks for an hour. No book held that hour, because no book was open. It was not lost and it was not owed. It went, at every crossing, into the one thing on the Round still keeping time in the dark, and she had carried it home every time. The old mind had not been wrong about it. An hour in forty days was inside what it was allowed to call nothing, and it had called it nothing, honestly, for sixty years.

She had thought that was what an empty capsule weighed.

---

"Whose is it?" Plumb said.

"All five's. They don't want it back."

"Then what do I write?"

She told it. Plumb wrote *one hour, carried*, and considered the line.

"That does not close."

"No," Sabel said. "It goes round."

She never did learn what the fifth band at the bend was waiting for. She looked for it on every round after, on her way through, and it was always there.

## What I Made Up

*The author's closing note, as printed.*

Three things in this story cannot happen, and I chose all three.

Time is not a liquid. A courier's capsule carries a clock, and what comes home from a round is [a column of offsets](/star-rangers/lore/universal-cosmic-stardate/#reduction-and-closure). I kept a closure ledger for nine years before I wrote stories, and I never found an hour in the bottom of anything.

A [plainmark](/star-rangers/glossary/plainmark/) has no tense and will not grow one. That is why it can be trusted in smoke.

And there is no back room anywhere with a seam in it. As I understand the Survey's pages, the neighbours, where there are any, are [not behind a wall](/star-rangers/glossary/interval/). Nobody hears them through one, and nothing can be poured.

The rest I did not make up. [Minds do decline](/star-rangers/glossary/kernel-compliant/). [People who share a body](/star-rangers/glossary/plural-minds/) do disagree over tea, and wish you well afterwards. The lights do go out for the sparks, on [a world](/star-rangers/lore/planets/drithane/) I have read about and not seen. And a loop that will not close is not thrown away. Somebody is handed it, and asked to stand behind whatever it turns out to be.

A timekeeper of my acquaintance read the story through and said only that forty days was optimistic.
