### Receiving desk's note

*After re-contact the Archive received, among the Celtic Union's public print, a collection of going-to-sleep songs from the households of one of the Currach Fleet's six hulls. Each song is printed with a note. The note to this one is signed by [Gráinne Ní Chathail](/star-rangers/characters/grainne-ni-chathail/), whom the Archive had until now held only as a credit in the Union register's public summary. The collection is in Union Irish. The rendering of her note is this desk's. The song, and the verb forms the note is about, are left as printed and glossed once each; the desk has corrected nothing, which the note asks of anyone who handles it. Her heading stands over it in both languages. The note at the end is ours.*

## An Briathar a Fágadh

*The Verb That Was Left*

I am asked to write these notes because I am supposed to be good at saying why a form is what it is. For this song I can say why. It does me no particular credit, so I will be brief and exact.

The song is a *suantraí*, a going-to-sleep song, from a run of households along one gallery of our hull. They kept Irish at table and at bedtime through the whole crossing, on purpose, when most of the hull had let the working speech take the day. This is the song as they sing it.

> *Chuaigh an ghrian a luí,*\
> *chuaigh an t-éan a luí,*\
> *chuaigh an lá thart, a stór,*\
> *is ní dheachaigh mise.*
>
> The sun went to its bed,\
> the bird went to its bed,\
> the day went by, my dear,\
> and I did not go.

One voice sings it. There is no instrument, and nobody remembers there ever being one. It is sung low and unhurried, and each line falls at its end. The four lines go through twice, and the second time the last word, *mise*, is held until the breath is done. Nothing is added. If the child is asleep by the second *luí*, the song stops there. It is a tool.

Now the verb. It is *téigh*, go. Every child in our schools can give you its past: *théigh*, went; *níor théigh*, did not go. It is built as *phléigh* is built from *pléigh*, as the past of any verb of its shape is built, and I have taught it so to every class I have stood in front of.

The song does not have that. It has *chuaigh*, went, and *ní dheachaigh*, did not go. Those are the old forms. They are in every grammar we carried, in the table of verbs that would not behave, with *rachaidh*, will go, beside them. Nobody disputes the table. But a table is not a mouth. A verb keeps an odd past by being heard ten thousand times before a child is seven, and on the hulls the children heard Irish in the evening and the working speech all day. A child who has not heard a form often enough builds it by the rule, and builds it correctly, and is not wrong. By landfall *chuaigh* was a thing people could read, and *théigh* was what they said, in these households too. When we wrote the language down again we wrote what the rule gave and the people had, and I would make the same choice tomorrow.

Except that in this one song the old verb was heard every night of a child's life, by every child, for as long as the crossing took. That is ten thousand times. So here it stayed, by the same law that lost it everywhere else, and the people who kept it could not have told you what they were keeping.

I asked the woman who sang it for me whether *chuaigh* was *théigh*. She said that it was. Then she said that in the song it was *chuaigh*, and looked at me as you would look at someone who had asked whether the air might be better as a different air.

A schoolbook could print it regular. I tried it. *Théigh an ghrian a luí* sings perfectly well, and so do the next two lines. The fourth does not: *is níor théigh mise* comes up a syllable short, and the air has to be bent to cover it. I noticed that I was relieved, and I do not trust the relief. If the fourth line had sung, I should still have left it.

The rule I am given credit for, more credit than is just, says what to do at a gap: fill it with the rule, and not with the exception we imagine once stood there. I hold to that. But it is a rule about gaps, and this is not one. A gap is where nothing came through. Here something came through, one verb in one song, in the mouths of people who were not trying to save a verb. They were trying to get a child to sleep. My rule says nothing about what to do with a thing like that, and I have decided that its saying nothing is an instruction.

So it is printed as sung. I have asked that it not be marked as an error, and not as a treasure either. The footnote reads *chuaigh: théigh, the old form*, and that is all it reads. A child who asks why can be told that this is how it came.

I have entered it in the register as inheritance, by ear, one gallery's. It is the smallest entry I have made, and the only one in which I had nothing to decide.

One thing I did not see until I had the song on paper. For the whole of the crossing the sun in it went nowhere. There was no sun, and nothing aboard went to its bed but people. We have landed now. There is a sun here, and it goes down every evening where a child can watch it go, and the first verb the children of that gallery had for what it did was the old one. I had *théigh* ready for them. They did not need it.

I began by saying I could tell you why the form is what it is. It is because it was sung. I will teach *théigh* in the morning, as I should.

### Receiving desk's note, continued

*Filed Cultural Record, valid for its author. What the Archive holds in its own voice, on the [Celtic Union](/star-rangers/lore/celtic-union-of-planets/) page, the note also holds: that across the transit generations the spoken Irish thinned to the households that kept it on purpose, while the written language survived almost entire; that the Union rebuilt the spoken language after landfall and has never pretended it is the old one; that it is regular where the old language was irregular; and that which forms are inheritance and which are reconstruction is a question the Union's own register answers. The Archive keeps no second copy of the register and has not checked the entry she describes. Everything else is hers: that this verb's old past had gone out of speech by landfall, in the keeping households too; why it went, and why the song held it; the gallery, the singer and the footnote; and her reading of the rule the register credits her with. The register's public summary gives that rule as a rule for gaps. That it is silent on what is not a gap is her account of it, and the Archive files it as that.*

**The Irish, as printed, for a reader without it.** *Téigh* is the verb "go", cited as Irish cites a verb, by its command form. *Chuaigh*, "went", and *ní dheachaigh*, "did not go", are the old language's irregular past, and *rachaidh*, "will go", its irregular future. *Théigh* and *níor théigh* are the regular past the Union's Irish builds from *téigh*, on the pattern of *pléigh*, "discuss", and *phléigh*, "discussed". The note gives no regular future and the desk has supplied none. *Suantraí* is a going-to-sleep song; *a stór* is "my dear"; *mise* is "I", with weight on it. The heading, *An Briathar a Fágadh*, is her own and means what the title says, in both of its senses: left behind, and left alone.

*The collection names no hull, being one hull's own book, and the record does not place her on a named hull or world. The note carries no date. The air is printed in the collection in a notation of the Union's; the desk has not reproduced it and holds no recording.*
