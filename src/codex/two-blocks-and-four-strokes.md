### Receiving desk's note

*In 2825 UCSD a pressure-boundary surveyor of the [Orbital Habitats Compact](/star-rangers/lore/orbital-habitats-compact/)'s schedule office walked one residential ring of a member habitat, reading frames. On that ring most household doors carry a painted count on the moving leaf. The surveyor's return has one entry on them, which the desk gives whole:* "Leaves carry painted counts, by local custom. Not on frames. Not stencilled. No panel affected. The schedule does not provide for these. No finding. Painter's terms attached." *What was attached is below: the sheet the ring's sign-painter, [Nkem Fairlie](/star-rangers/characters/nkem-fairlie/), keeps posted at her paint store, with the lines she had added lower down in her own hand. The return is public and the Compact's record copied it to the Archive. The surveyor asked that the ring not be named outside the return, on the ground that a custom the schedule does not provide for is not improved by visitors, and the desk has agreed. The title is the desk's. The note at the end is ours.*

---

**LEAF COUNTS**

**What I paint and what I don't. Read it before you ask. I have had the conversation.**

**What it is.** Frame marks are the ring's. I do those in the morning with the stencils and the warden's sheet, and this is not about them.

This is about the count on your door. On this ring a household is whoever sleeps behind one pressure door, and the household's count goes on the leaf. It is not [plainmark](/star-rangers/lore/plainmark/). The schedule has no such mark, and where it speaks of a leaf at all it is to say that no panel goes on one. I hold the Compact's ticket for frame work, and I am telling you as a ticket-holder: what I put on your leaf is paint on a door. It promises nothing to anybody. Nobody coming down this corridor on a bad day should read it, and they are taught not to. They read the frame, and the ring's roll says who is behind it. If I ever hear that a count of mine was used to decide a door was empty, I will scrape every leaf on this ring myself.

Other rings do not do this. I am told the next ring in thinks we are showing off. I have not been asked to paint there.

**Why it looks like the frame's.** The ring settled, before my time, that if a door was going to say a number it would say it the way the frames do, so that everyone on the corridor could read it, whatever they speak at home. I know of nine languages on my corridor. The count is the one thing on any door there that all of us read.

So inside the band I paint in the schedule's hand, though not by its leave. Bone-white ground. Black figures. A block is five and a stroke is one, blocks first from the left, in the stencil's proportions. By brush. The stencils do not go near a leaf.

**The band is plain.** Nothing goes in it but the count. No pattern in a block. No foot on a stroke. An apprentice of mine once painted a household's blocks as small houses, with roofs. She repainted the band that afternoon with the household watching, and they were very kind to her about it.

**Outside the band is yours.** Any colours and any pattern your household calls its own, with these exceptions, which I will not argue. No black with ochre anywhere on a leaf, in any shape. No white points on blue. No rings, no lozenges and no chevrons: not as flowers, not as a child's sun. Those are the frames', and they mean air, danger and the way out. I will not put anything that looks like them on a thing that swings. And a hand's width of bare leaf all round the band, so that nobody takes your border for part of your number.

**When I paint.** A stroke goes on with the household there and at least one door of neighbours. People bring food, but it is not a party. It is witness. A count nobody saw painted is a number somebody put on a door. I paint what the people standing there tell me is so. If they do not agree, I go and have tea until they do.

**The fifth.** When a fifth comes, four strokes come off and a block goes on. It is the best job there is. I take the whole band back to white and paint it new with everyone watching. Whoever was the fifth is called the block on that corridor for some years afterwards. I was one.

**What I don't paint.** In no order.

*Not yet.* A child who is expected is not a stroke. Somebody arriving next month is not a stroke. I am asked every time to come early, because the day itself will be busy. The count says who sleeps there tonight. I will come on the day. I always have.

*Not any more.* When somebody leaves a household I take the band back to white and paint it whole, one fewer. I do not paint one stroke out. A painted-out stroke shows as a patch for years. A patch says *was*, and the count has no *was*. It has no *why* either. Gone to another ring and died read the same on a door: fewer.

When it is a death I am asked to leave the stroke a while. I am asked nearly every time, and I would think less of a household that did not ask. I do not leave it. People keep their dead somewhere. Every household on this ring has a place for that, and I have been shown most of them. It is not the door. Neighbours do not come to that repaint unless the household asks them. I do it early, before the corridor is about, and there is no charge.

*Not nobody.* There is no figure for none. When the last one goes I paint the band out to the leaf's own colour. An empty door on this ring is a plain door.

*Not half.* A child who sleeps at two doors is counted at the one they are behind tonight. I had one who went week and week about for most of a year. I repainted two doors every rest day and charged for the first. The schedule office would tell you that both doors were wrong at the edges of every week. It is a leaf. I am not the schedule office.

*Not who.* It cannot say a name, and I do not letter. Put a name in your border if you want one there. The border is yours.

*Not one stroke struck.* The bar is the frames' way of saying a promise has been withdrawn. It goes across a whole band, and it is theirs. I have been asked for a stroke with a small bar through it, for somebody away a long time. No.

*Not guests.* Who sleeps there is for the household to say and the neighbours to hear. I hold the brush.

*Added lower on the sheet, in the same hand:*

The door at the turn of my corridor has stood at two blocks and four strokes for six years. No door on this ring has held one number so long in my time, and it is the largest household I paint. This month their oldest is not expected to see the month out, and their youngest is expecting. I was asked about both on one afternoon by one person. Could the stroke stay a while, when the time came. Could the block go on early. No, and no. She said she knew. She said she had wanted to have asked.

So I may paint that door twice inside a week. If I do, it will go to thirteen and back, or to three blocks and back, in whichever order things happen. Three blocks is the best job there is and I will do it properly even if it is for four days. At the end of it the leaf will say two blocks and four strokes, as it does today. It will not be the same fourteen, and the paint has no way of telling you so. Anybody passing will see that the band is fresh and know I have been. That is all the door will say about that week, and all of it will be true.

### Receiving desk's note, continued

*Filed Cultural Record, valid for its author. It is one ring's custom, by her word and the surveyor's. It is not the Compact's, not the habitat's, and not a use of the plainmark. The Compact's marking schedule provides for no count of persons, no mark on a moving leaf, and nothing a household may add. The surveyor's "No finding" is a finding about frames and panels, which were in order. The desk does not read it as leave, and the author does not either: she says in terms that the schedule has no such mark.*

*What the Archive holds in its own voice, on the [plainmark entry](/star-rangers/lore/plainmark/), the sheet rests on and does not go past: that a count band is black strokes and blocks on a bone-white ground, a stroke one and a block five, blocks first from the left, so that fourteen is two blocks and four strokes; that there is no zero, and where there is nothing to count the plainmark says nothing; that it has no tenses and cannot name; that a diagonal bar voids a whole band; that the figure set is closed; that a panel is stencilled, sits on the fixed frame and never on the moving leaf; that the schedule fixes proportions; that black with ochre, and white chevrons on slate blue, are the grounds of hazard, refuge and the way; that whoever holds a pressure boundary keeps its marks current, so that fresh paint is a small checkable claim; and that surveyors read marks. The plainmark is drawn for a standing human's eye, and so is this. The sheet does not say whether any household on the ring includes people of another body, and the desk has not asked.*

*What the sheet holds from no source but its author: the custom; that on this ring a household is whoever sleeps behind one pressure door; the witness of neighbours and the naming of a fifth; each thing she declines; the apprentice, whom she does not name; and the door at the turn. A sheet of terms adds no sign to the plainmark and could not. It borrows two of the standard's figures onto a surface where the standard gives them no meaning, and keeps everything else of the standard's off that surface. Whether a stranger untrained in the schedule could take a leaf count for a panel, the desk cannot say. Her terms are written against it, and she says what she would do if it happened.*

*The return is dated and the added lines are not. The desk has not asked what the door at the turn says now. It would be told a number.*
