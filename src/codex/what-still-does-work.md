### Receiving note

*The Survey Archive asked, through the Corps' rolls of former officers, for a history of the Krenyi by one of them. The author answered the asking, came to the receiving desk at Threshold Station, and asked what form the Archive wanted it in. The desk said: whatever form her people keep a history in. She said that her people keep one in their own count, and that the reader would not have the count, and that she would give it in the count regardless, since that is what the desk had asked for. She wrote it at the desk in the Archive's working language, read it back once, and passed it. The desk asked one thing of its own, which was to set the Archive's date beside hers; she declined, with her reason, and the reason is in the account. The note at the end is ours.*

---

The desk has asked me for the history of my people, and has asked for it in the form we keep it in. I will do that. First I will say what the form is, so that the reader does not take the shortness of what follows for a withholding. Some of it is withheld, and I say where. The rest is short because it is kept.

We keep what still does work. A thing that happened is kept while it returns something to the present: a measurement, a failure worth not repeating, a precedent that still decides something. When it stops returning anything it is let go, and nobody decides to let it go, and nobody notices that it has gone. Your record keeps what nobody has yet found a use for, in case. We do not. The reader will have been told that we are poor keepers of such things. That is true, and I would say it myself, and I say it here.

### The count

Every kept thing is given at its place in our count. The count is a count: it runs forward from something, in a unit that is ours, and I give neither the something nor the unit. What a count runs from is a placing, and I decline to be placed. The length of the unit I also decline, because a unit set beside yours is a conversion, and a conversion is a placing of one count against another. The Corps has a figure for that, which it uses for the ages of its Krenyi officers on its rolls. The figure is the Corps'. I did not make it and I do not carry it.

So the reader will have numbers and no way to turn them into the station's calendar, and I have been asked whether that makes them useless. It does not. The numbers are in order, and the distances between them are true, and that is most of what a date is for.

### What was let go

The oldest kept thing in our count is a gap.

At one thousand four hundred and twenty-seven, something was let go. At one thousand six hundred and three it was needed, and it was not there. What it was, nobody kept, because it had been let go; what is kept is that it was needed, and by how much, and that nothing could be done. The two numbers are kept together and are never given apart.

It is the oldest thing we keep and it does the most work of anything we keep. Every judgement since, that a thing has stopped returning anything and may go, has had one example to stand beside. A people who let things go need one case where the letting go cost, and need exactly one, and we have it. That it is a gap is not a defect in the record. It is what the record is for.

Below one thousand four hundred and twenty-seven the count has numbers and nothing at them. Your record would call that loss. It is not loss. It is what a people who keep only what does work look like from a long way after, and it is correct.

### The figure that was rounded

At three thousand and eighty-two, one of us who was young wrote down a figure that the instrument had not carried. The instrument carried less. The reading had felt as certain as the larger figure, and the larger figure was written, and a place was certified on it that was not yet certain. The certification had to be withdrawn. Nothing was lost but the certification, and the withdrawing is what is kept, in the words that were said at it, which were short.

*What did the instrument carry?*

*Less.*

*Then write less.*

Your Corps has the same rule, in its own words. We did not bring it. It was there when we came, which is one of the reasons we came, and I mark that the young are still trained out of the larger figure, ours and yours, and that this is kept because it is not finished.

### The blood

At three thousand nine hundred and seven, one of yours was given our blood.

There was a shortage. A physician of yours reasoned from what was known of us, that we are robust, that we are rarely sick, that we are honest about what we are, and the reasoning was not unreasonable, and the human was ill for days and lived. Our blood takes apart what is not ours, and a human is entirely not ours, and we had not said so. We had not been asked.

The physician asked afterwards why we had not said. The one of us who was there said: *You did not ask.* The physician said: *I am asking now.* And the one of us said: *Then write it where it will be read before it is needed, and not after.*

Since then we say it first. It is in the material part we give to anyone who may one day have to treat us, before they ask, and the human who was ill is the reason. That is kept because it still does work, every time one of us meets a medic, and because it is the clearest case we have of the cost of a true thing not said. We are honest by disposition. Honesty is answering what is asked. This was the thing that taught us that it is sometimes also saying what has not been asked yet.

### The ones written up afterwards

At three thousand nine hundred and seventy-one, one of us named a constraint in a settlement of yours, and something was done about it, and she left. I do not describe what was present with her, since what it looked like is supplied by the witness and I decline to supply one. The settlement wrote the thing up afterwards. I have read the write-up. It is a measurement of the settlement.

We keep the constraint. It still does work: it is a case of its kind, and the kind recurs. We do not keep the write-up, and we have never found a way to correct one, since it is told in a place we have left, by people who were not there, about a person who gives the material answer when asked and declines the rest. The reader has the write-up already. I do not set anything beside it. It is yours.

### Going

Some of us go to your Corps. Nobody sends us. I went. I asked counsel of those whose counsel I wanted and was given it and went, and that is the whole of how it is done, and the reader's record has it right.

I give no count for my going. The Corps holds the station's date for it, on its rolls, and a count of mine set beside a date of yours is a conversion, and I have said what a conversion is. The reader may make one from other sources if the reader can. I do not make it here.

I held a rank. It stopped doing work on the day the work stopped, and it is not in this account, and the desk has agreed not to set it beside my name, since the rolls hold it and the rolls are the right place. It is not a slight to the Corps. A rank that does no work is a history, and a history offered as a description is the one use of the past we decline.

### One who finished

I will give one thing that is not my people's but mine, since the desk asked for a history and a history that has no one in it is not what the desk meant.

I served long enough beside people who die to see one of them out. He was good at the work and was asked about it, which in our rendering of your ladder is a rank. The grief was real and it finished. I did not hold it open, and I was asked afterwards by one of yours whether that meant I had not grieved. I had. What is kept of him is not the grief. It is a way of reading an instrument that I did not have before I worked beside him and have had since, which still does work, and which I have passed to two others, and which they do not know came from him. That is what a person leaves with us. It is more than a name.

I give no count for it. It is not history. It is in use.

### The question

The reader will ask, as every reader asks, where we are from. I give the material part, which the reader already has: we are long-lived; we cannot interbreed with any other people; we keep our own count. Asked where, I say: here. The reader will take that for an evasion. It is the whole of the answer that does work. A place offered as an explanation is the past used to define a person, and that is the one use of the past we decline, and I have declined it now in words, so that nothing after this is mistaken for agreement.

It is not a secret. A secret hides that there is anything to tell. I am telling the reader that there is, and that I will not, and those are two different sentences, and I have said both.

### What is kept

That is the history. The reader has asked, kindly, whether a history so short can be a history, and I have given the answer the form gives. It is short because everything in it is in use. There is nothing in it kept only for being true. When a thing in it stops returning anything, it will go, and nobody will notice, and the count will go on with a number where it was, and that will be correct.

The desk asked for it in the form we keep it in. This is the form. It is all in use.

### Receiving note, continued

*Filed Cultural Record, valid for its author. The Archive's rule is that a year is always somebody's year, converted beside its own and never replaced; here it has converted nothing, because it holds nothing to convert with in this collection, and because the author's reason for declining to set her count beside the station's calendar is one the Archive files rather than overrides. The conversion the Corps uses for a Krenyi officer's age on its rolls is the Corps' and is not held here; the Archive has not asked for it for this account. Every figure in the account is therefore in the author's reckoning, with its beginning and its unit withheld by her, and the reader is to take the figures as ordered and spaced, and as nothing else. Whether every Krenyi keeps the one count, and whether a second Krenyi asked would give the same kept things, the Archive does not know and has not asked. Five things in the account the Archive already holds in its own voice: that Krenyi blood is a hazard to a human and that the Krenyi volunteer the fact early; that a reading is written at what the instrument carries and no higher; that a Krenyi's rank leaves Krenyi speech on the day the work stops; that the Corps converts a Krenyi officer's age without the figures being on file here; and that the record holds no Krenyi homeworld, by the Krenyi's own stated wish. The gap at one thousand four hundred and twenty-seven is kept as the author gave it, two figures together and nothing at either, under the Archive's own doctrine that a marked absence is itself information. The rest is hers, and in use.*
