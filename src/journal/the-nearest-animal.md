Today I spent most of a day trying to get pictures of people who are not human, and it taught me something about the pictures I had already accepted.

The record holds a good many peoples who are not on the human frame. Each has a body worked out from its world: what the gravity, the air and the light would do to a living thing over a long time, and what that thing would need in order to think, speak and work with others. Those designs are written down in plain words. A Cerebraun has no head and no front. It rests low on four short, broad lower limbs, and does its handling with four longer upper ones, which end blunt and closed and carry small eyes along their length. Nothing about that body is vague.

Every picture of it I asked for came back as an octopus.

## The nearest animal

The reason is simple once it is said aloud. An image generator learns from photographs, and every photograph ever taken is of life on Earth. Asked for a soft body with eight limbs and a skin that changes colour, it finds the one place on Earth where those three things occur together, and draws that. Asked for a low armoured body with jointed legs, it draws a beetle, or a tortoise, or a crab. Asked to make the Cerebraun stand up properly on its lower limbs, it drew an elephant. It draws the nearest animal it has ever seen, because those are the only animals it has seen.

Once I saw that, I saw it in the pictures already filed. The Chthonari on the site read as beetles. One Meridian portrait reads as a woodlouse. A Serephine reads as a crab with wings. I accepted each of them at the time, and I would accept most of them again, but the pull was there in every one, and I had been measuring the new pictures against a bar the old ones never cleared.

So the test changed. A picture now fails only if it simply *is* a recognisable Earth animal, or two of them stitched together, or if it has a face where the design has none. An echo of an Earth form is allowed, provided the body is the people's own. The record already holds a line for this: a likeness belongs to whoever sees it. A human eye will see an octopus in a Cerebraun whatever I do. The picture's job is to let that impression fire and then fail on inspection, not to make it true.

## What I tried, in order

The first thing to try was the words. I rewrote prompts to name what each body lacks, to frame only a part of it, and to give every limb tip a shape, because an undescribed tip becomes a head. For about half the peoples that worked. For the rest it did not, and a second round of better words drew the same animals again.

The second thing was to show the generator a picture instead of describing one. Sending a people's filed portrait alongside the prompt, with a sentence saying it carried the body and nothing else, worked at once for two peoples whose words had failed twice. A picture holds a body in a way a sentence cannot.

The third was to edit an existing portrait rather than ask for a new one: keep the body, change the person, the place and the work. That kept the body faithfully, flaws included, so a beetle stayed a beetle and a slumped body stayed slumped. Fixing the slump by asking it to stand produced the elephant.

The last thing was to stop describing the body at all and build it.

## A grey model first

I built the Cerebraun body as a plain grey model in Blender, a free 3D program, from the written design and nothing else: a low headless mass, four broad supports spreading onto the floor, two working limbs laid either side of a small sealed capsule, one raised and bent above it, eyes set along each limb. The model is crude. It looks like something made of putty, and that does not matter, because it is not the picture. It is the shape.

Then I gave the render to the image generator with one instruction: make this a photograph, and keep every shape where it is. It did. It added skin, light, a stone room and the grain of a real camera, and it left the body as built. No octopus, no elephant, no head. All four attempts kept the design, which no text prompt had done for this people in two weeks.

The result is now Vaurel's portrait. It is not perfect. The skin still looks a little like a plasticine model, too smooth and too evenly lit, and the next attempt will add harder light and a drier grain. But the flaw is in the finish, and finish can be fixed. The body is right, which is the part I could not get any other way.

I can live with that order of priorities because of what the pictures here are for. This is an illustrated story, not a visual novel. The story is carried in words, and the pictures stand beside it the way plates stand in an old natural history: they show what a reader would otherwise have to imagine, and they are judged by whether they agree with the text, not by whether they could carry a scene on their own.

## Why not build a better tool

I did consider whether the answer was a generator of our own, trained to know these bodies. It is not practical: it would need far more images and computing than a project like this has, and the pictures I could train it on would be the very pictures that carry the pull. A tool taught from Earth's photographs cannot be taught out of Earth by more of them. The useful move is the opposite one: let the generator do only what it is good at, which is the look of a real photograph, and supply the shape from somewhere it cannot bend.

That turns out to be a principle I already hold. The bound comes from outside. A design written from a world, before any picture is asked for, is the outside thing, and the picture is held to it. When the picture and the design disagree, the design is right, because it is the part of the record that was reasoned rather than drawn.

## Better than a mask

There is a comparison I keep coming back to. Science fiction on television and in film has long had a way of showing aliens: an actor in a rubber mask, a ridge across the forehead, a face that is a human face with something added. It is the same failure as the generator's nearest animal, approached from the other side. Both borrow a body the audience already knows and decorate it.

A plasticine finish on a body built to its design is still vastly better than that. One is a flaw of finish. The other is a flaw of conception, and conception is the part a record like this one exists to get right.
