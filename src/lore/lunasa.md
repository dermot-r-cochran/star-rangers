The best-known forty seconds in the record are a comparison, and Lúnasa is the far end of it.

## What It Is

**Lúnasa** is a [Survey Corps](/star-rangers/lore/star-rangers-survey-corps/) station in Earth orbit. Among its duties, it keeps the reference time standard against which [Threshold Station](/star-rangers/lore/threshold-station/) checks its own. It is one of the many well-kept standards whose readings the Concord reconciles into [Universal Concord Standard Date](/star-rangers/lore/universal-cosmic-stardate/). It is not a master clock: the record keeps none. It is simply the standard Threshold is set against.

Its time comes down to the shore by link, and Dock Seven shows it. Of the two chronometers on the dock wall, the one above the pressure seal keeps Threshold's own time, and the station's watch log is kept by it. The one above the inner hatch is a repeater of Lúnasa's standard: *the station's and the other one*, in the words of the [first-night log](/star-rangers/seasons/s01/e01/s01e01c04/).

## The Ring

Lúnasa is one station in Earth's ring: thousands of stations in a single circle at geostationary height, above the equator, each keeping pace with the turning ground below it. From Ireland the ring is a beaded arc low in the southern sky, a line of separate points of light before dawn that does not move. Lúnasa is one of the points. A javelin's job ends at a parking orbit, so a passage to the ring is two journeys: up by [ascent javelin](/star-rangers/lore/ascent-javelins/), then out along the orbit to a station's docking spine. Lúnasa has one.

The ring is not a polity. Its stations are installations below the [habitat threshold](/star-rangers/lore/habitat-threshold/), each run by its operator, Lúnasa by the Survey Corps, and a shared slot-and-traffic office allots their positions and settles nothing else. It does not belong to the [Orbital Habitats Compact](/star-rangers/lore/orbital-habitats-compact/), whose civil chain and [Orbital Five-O](/star-rangers/characters/kai-larsen/) reach its five member habitats and no further. If a member habitat sits in the ring, that habitat alone is theirs; the record does not say whether any does.

That is also why so many Rangers are posted to the ring and work there. Below the threshold, the space around and inside a station is the Rangers' proper domain under their charter, and a habitat above it is a place they enter only by invitation. Near Earth, the ring is the largest stretch of orbit that is theirs to work in without asking.

## The Clock Room

Lúnasa's standard is an optical clock: a cloud of ytterbium atoms held in a vacuum chamber and read by laser. The light that cools the atoms is violet, and while the chamber loads, the atoms glow a faint violet behind its port. That is the one light in the room.

Beside the standard hangs a repeater of Threshold's station time, carried up by the same link that carries Lúnasa's time down. So the forty seconds can be read at either end, and an officer at Lúnasa sees the same two times a watch officer on Dock Seven sees. It is one comparison, shown on two walls.

## The Forty Seconds

A clock in orbit and a clock on the ground do not run alike, and the record expects them not to. Each clock's proper time is [reduced to a stated convention](/star-rangers/lore/universal-cosmic-stardate/) before any two are compared. Between Earth orbit and Earth's surface, the predicted correction comes to microseconds a day, and the light-time of the link to a fraction of a second. Both are applied, routinely, before Threshold's time and Lúnasa's are set side by side.

What remains after that is forty seconds. It does not grow and does not shrink, and it returns within forty-eight hours of every recalibration. The record files it the way its timekeeping files any residual that survives every predicted correction: not as bookkeeping slack, but as a reading. It is the best-documented Type II boundary reading on the books ([Boundary Zones](/star-rangers/lore/boundary-zones/)).

## Which End Disagrees

The forty seconds belong to the shore, not to the station in orbit. Lúnasa's standard reconciles with the rest of the ensemble as any good standard does. Threshold's own clock, checked on its own, is accurate too. It is only the comparison between this shore and the standard that comes out forty seconds apart.

The disagreement is also older than either station. The lighthouse keepers' clock corrections in the [Sceir Light](/star-rangers/seasons/s01/e01/s01e01c03/)'s register, read long after the fact, all run one way and grow across two centuries, measured against the standard time of their day, carried then by the Lights Board's signal. How those corrections relate to the forty seconds is not established. The disagreement has always been between this shore and whatever kept the standard. Lúnasa is only the present holder of the far end.

What produces the forty seconds is not established. The comparison is.

## What the Record Does Not Hold

Lúnasa's size and crew, its shape beyond the docking spine, its place along the ring, the year it entered service, and why it bears the Irish name of the old harvest month are not on file. Nor is the ring's age, its exact count of stations, or who built it.

## Provenance

The station's name first appeared in the film *[The Long White Trail](/star-rangers/journal/the-long-white-trail/)*, and it entered the record from there on 13 November 2026. On 19 November 2026 the ring, the docking spine, the repeater on Lúnasa's wall and the violet light of its clock followed, at Dermot's word. The film's narrator and her fifty years did not: they are reserved for a later thread. The film itself remains an impression, filed in the Journal.
