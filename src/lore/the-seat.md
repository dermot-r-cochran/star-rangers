A post outlasts the person standing at it. Any ship knows that. The households on this page are the ones who went on knowing it after there was no ship.

**The Seat** is a custom of certain human households, descended from a [generation ark](/star-rangers/lore/the-generation-ark-era/) and living now under Concord law, in which what a family hands down is a name and a place and never a person. The households are [plural](/star-rangers/glossary/plural-minds/) by upbringing, generation after generation, and a seat is held by one member of a system, not by a body. The name the record uses is a rendering of theirs, and so is **standing-beside**, their word for how a seat comes to be held again.

## What a Seat Is

A household keeps a small number of seats. Each has a name, and the names are plain ones; the two the record has been given are rendered *Midwatch* and *the Door*. By the households' own account the seats began as watch posts aboard the ark, places that had to be stood through a crossing longer than any life aboard it, so that the post was the lasting thing and its keepers were the ones who changed. The record files that as their account. It is consistent with what the era's companies did elsewhere, [marking their hulls](/star-rangers/lore/plainmark/) for descendants they would never meet, and nothing on file contradicts it.

A seat is not a rank and carries no authority over anyone. It carries a name, a place at the household's table, and whatever its holders have undertaken in its name.

## Standing-Beside

A seat comes to be held again by cultivation, which the record already knows: one mind may deliberately grow another, and the one grown is a person. What the households add is company and time. A child who chooses to stands beside an elder relative whose system holds a seat. The child keeps that member's hours, works where that member works, and for some years is simply near. Somewhere in those years a member may come to be in the child's system who answers to the seat.

That member is new. The households say so before anyone asks, and the record agrees with them: nothing crosses from the elder body to the younger, no memory and no self, and the younger holder knows of the elder's life what any grandchild knows, which is what they saw and what they were told. The seat is old. Its holder is as old as they are.

Both are alive at once, and that is the custom's whole safeguard. For a time the seat is held twice, in two bodies, by two people who can sit at one table and disagree. Nobody in the household has ever had the chance to read the one as the other come back.

Two courtesies keep it so.

**The cup.** A child of these households is never asked who is there. A cup is set out, and whoever takes it is greeted, by the name they give, or by none if they give none. A child in whom nobody new ever comes takes the cup all their life and is greeted all their life, and a seat nobody answers to stands empty without reproach.

**The name said once.** While both are living, the younger does not use the seat's name in the elder's hearing until the elder has said it to them first. The elder says it once. After that it belongs to both of them, and the household counts the seat as held from that day.

## The Seat Before the Law

The Concord's rule is the standard one and the households live under it: a system is [jointly responsible](/star-rangers/glossary/plural-minds/#responsibility-before-the-law) for every act of the body its members share, and the responsibility is counted per body. It ends at the skin.

The households count some things differently. An undertaking given in a seat's name is held, in the family, to be the seat's, and to stand when the body that gave it is gone. The law says that nobody owes it. Both are true in the same kitchen. So a young system can find that one of its members has taken up, with the seat, a promise the others never made and would not have made, and that once the body acts on it the law will hold all of them to it together. What follows is an argument, of the kind any people sharing a life have, among persons who are all real and who want different things. It is nothing else, and the record files no other reading of it.

From outside it has been misread in two ways. A registrar who finds one name entered in two living systems of one family sees a duplicate. A registrar who finds the younger entry outliving the elder sees a claim of succession. The households see neither, and have said so patiently on the forms provided: two people held one seat, and now one does. No estate, standing or credit moves with it. A commendation earned in the elder body belongs to the elder system and stops there, and the younger holder is usually the first in the room to say it.

## What the Record Does Not Carry

As of this edition the record has not established which ark the households descend from, or how its company came to live under Concord law. Nor does it yet say whether a member who has never fronted can hold a seat; what becomes of a standing-beside when the elder dies before the name is said; how many households keep the custom; or the seats' names in the households' own speech, which are theirs.
