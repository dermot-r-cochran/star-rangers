A post outlasts the person standing at it. The Casimor watch has kept one since before the Kingdom's count of monarchs begins, and has had that long to work out what follows.

**The Seat** is the custom by which the watch-post on Krilzat, in the [Kingdom of the Five Islands](/star-rangers/lore/planets/kingdom-of-the-five-islands/), is handed keeper to keeper. What the watch hands down is a name and a place and never a person. The keepers' family is [plural](/star-rangers/glossary/plural-minds/) by upbringing, generation after generation, and the seat is held by one member of a system, not by a body. The words here are the Archive's renderings: *the Seat*, and **standing-beside**, for how the seat comes to be held again. The seat's name in the watch's own speech is not carried.

## Hereditary, and on Service

The Kingdom's page says two things of the watch-post that look as if they cannot both be so. It is hereditary, the one hereditary command in the Kingdom that still means something on the ground. And it is held on service rather than blood, filled the way the Sergeant tier has always been filled: somebody does the work, and their children learn it.

The Seat is how both are true. A keeper's child usually follows the keeper, and nothing follows in the blood. What passes is learned, over years, by standing where the work is done, and the one who comes to hold the seat was not there to be born into it.

Three reigns back the Kingdom took the civilian offices away from the Old Houses, after hereditary landholders had mismanaged a run of storm seasons badly enough to cost Ostrel most of a winter's stores. The reform left the watch-post as it found it. The post was never a noble commission, and there was nothing in the way it passes for the reform to correct.

## Standing-Beside

The seat comes to be held again by cultivation, which the record already knows: one mind may deliberately grow another, and the one grown is a person. What the watch adds is company and time. A child of the post grows up beside the keeper. The child keeps the keeper's hours and works where the keeper works, and nothing is asked of them but the work. Whether to go on standing there, once they are of an age to say, is theirs. Somewhere in those years a member may come to be in the child's system who answers to the seat.

That member is new. The watch says so, and the record agrees with it: nothing crosses from the elder body to the younger, no memory and no self, and the younger keeper knows of the elder's life what any grandchild knows, which is what they saw and what they were told. The seat is old. Its holder is as old as they are.

Both are alive at once, and that is the custom's whole safeguard. For a time the seat is held twice, in two bodies, by two people who can sit at one table and disagree. Nobody at the post has ever had the chance to read the one as the other come back.

Two courtesies keep it so.

**The cup.** A child of the post is never asked who is there. A cup is set out, and whoever takes it is greeted, by the name they give, or by none if they give none. A child in whom nobody new ever comes takes the cup all their life and is greeted all their life. The post's guests are met the same way: the court's child who came up in the survey's third year was given bread before anyone asked her name, which was the court's business.

**The name said once.** While both are living, the younger does not use the seat's name in the elder's hearing until the elder has said it to them first. The elder says it once. After that it belongs to both of them, and the watch counts the seat as held from that day.

## The Seat and the Kingdom's Count

The Kingdom counts one keeper. The court's letters come to the gate addressed to the keeper, and say that the keeper decides, and the court has never needed to know more than that. The watch, for years at a time, has two who answer to the seat, and has always settled between them which one a letter has reached. Neither count has been put to the other. The Kingdom is practised at that: it has lived for longer than its historians can agree on with a chronicle and an Accounting that differ, and has not pressed those either.

Inside a keeper's system the seat can sit less easily. What has been undertaken in the seat's name is held, at the post, to be the seat's, and to stand when the body that undertook it is gone. A young system can find that one of its members has taken up, with the seat, a promise the others never made and would not have made, and they share one body and one day in which to keep it or not. What follows is an argument, of the kind any people sharing a life have, among persons who are all real and who want different things. It is nothing else, and the record files no other reading of it.

Whether the Kingdom's law holds the others in a keeper's system to what the keeper undertakes, the record has not established as of this edition; the Common Council holds the day-to-day law, and no case on the point is on file. The Concord's rule, which counts responsibility by the body, is the Concord's. The Kingdom holds no charter, and that rule does not run on Krilzat.

Keepers are honoured in the Kingdom above nearly every other commoner rank. The honour is the seat's and goes to whoever keeps it. What one keeper is honoured for having done is that keeper's and stops there, and the next keeper would be the first in the room to say so.

## What the Record Does Not Carry

As of this edition the record does not say whether any household in the Kingdom but the watch's keeps a seat. Nor does it yet say whether the post keeps more seats than the keeper's; whether anyone not born at the post has stood beside a keeper; whether a member who has never fronted can hold the seat; what becomes of a standing-beside when the elder dies before the name is said; or how old the custom is, the watch keeping no chronicle. The delegation has not been to Krilzat, and has filed no reading of its own.
