:::pov sildron-drey

The general asked the chair's patience for one more sentence, and Drey, on the Command's row, knew what it would be made of before she heard it.

The words were the general's, and Drey would not have chosen them. What stood under them was a staff paper filed at Solar Command in 2698 and set before every officer-track intake since: that the wars had been fought by institutions able to move faster than they could think, and that a command which could not be hurried had been built that way and was not failing that way. Twenty-one years on the syllabus. She had heard it summarised by cadets, by instructors, and, in eight years as Director of Operations, by committee secretaries in the covering notes to their findings. She had never heard it from a witness table.

The history was right. It had been right when she wrote it, and the general gave the figures as they stood, forty years and three billion.

After the history the paper went on to its conditions, the section that made it longer than its argument, and Drey found that she was waiting for them. They did not come. The general finished, and the sentence stood where she had put it.

The chamber went quiet. Drey had sat in rooms that agreed, and this was not one. Along the Command's row nobody moved.

She noted what there was to note. Nobody in the chamber was waiting for the conditions: not the chair, not the delegates, not the officers on either side of her. And the sentence did not want them. It was complete as given, and a room that had spent eleven weeks in the Command's own file had taken it as complete.

That afternoon she noted nothing further. The chair let the quiet stand and then adjourned, and Drey went back to her desk, because it was a working day and the Directorate's folio closed at the evening signal whatever had been said upstairs.

Two things were waiting there. One was the day's orders for signature. The other had been waiting a week: the clerk's correction copy of her own evidence, nine days of it, with a return slip due before the week was out.

Every witness was sent one. The slip had three lines. *Corrections to the record as taken. Requests to the chair on the characterisation of evidence. None.* With hers had come a note from the Command's counsel, respectfully drawn, proposing a request under the second line. The tables measured each case from a station's request to the authorised order. Inside that interval sat the Directorate's own part, receipt to referral, and in eight years that part had never run past three working days. Counsel suggested the hearing be asked to show the two separately.

It was true. Drey had checked it the day the note came, because it was a figure and it concerned her directorate, and no referral in her tenure had taken a fourth day. She was being offered her own record, and it was a good one.

She had known since then what she would enter. The week had been for the reading, not the decision, and she did the reading now, each figure in the transcript against its row in the tables. Eleven months to form the Eden committee. Fourteen months to its preliminary report. Six years to the follow-up findings. Halyard Station's supply authorisation, one hundred and twenty-two days. On one page a delegate had asked whether the *Patience First* classification she had signed in 2715 was correct, unauthorised and therefore outside the Command's obligation, and she had said that it was, and the clerk had taken it down as she said it. She read that page twice. On another they had asked about the monitoring officer at Eden, and she had given her two requests that the post be allowed to act without a further committee, and the two answers, that the change required committee review. She had told the hearing that both answers were correct. That was there too.

Nothing had been taken down wrongly. As for the second line: a station that had waited a hundred and twenty-two days had not waited three days and then a hundred and nineteen. It had waited once.

She initialled the third line and dated it. Counsel's note went into the file behind the slip, since it was a document and had been received.

Then she signed the day's orders.

::::: scene 2

:::pov solano

Halyard's council had sent her for something the station could use, and she had sent it two days ago. This morning she owed the hearing the other thing.

A delegation's formal testimony was read from the witness table, and Solano read hers the way the nine before her had: from the Directorate of Operations' own tables, by table and row. The Eden sequence first, because every delegation led with it. Eleven months, fourteen months, six years. Then her own station's line, which she'd given the chamber in round terms two days ago and gave now as the row had it: Halyard Station, primary supply route, request to authorised order, one hundred and twenty-two days.

The gallery was full again. It had not been full for the Director's nine days. Solano had sat through all nine, expecting the Command's senior staff officer to contest every figure, because a contest was a thing Halyard's council would have followed. There had been no contest. The Director had given each number as her tables gave it, agreed when a delegate read one back, and not once asked for one to be put more kindly — and there'd been nothing in it to send home, because a council on double shifts does not sit still to hear the other side agree.

So the gallery was full, and Solano knew what it had come for. She could see it from the table: faces on her, and nobody following the figures. The last count Halyard's feed desk had sent her was already a day old.

One person was following. On the Command's row the Director of Operations had the tables open flat, and when Solano named a row the Director was already on it. It was not checking. Solano had spent four years on a council learning what checking looked like, and what arranging looked like, and this was neither. It was somebody reading along.

Solano read the rest to the one reader she had. It changed nothing about what she'd released or why. When she gave the last figure the chair thanked the delegate, and the Director closed the tables.

:::pov sildron-drey

One listened to figures with the tables open. Drey had done so for nine delegations and did so for the tenth.

Most of them had rounded. The delegate from Halyard gave the days. She gave the table and the row before the figure, in the order a staff officer would have, and each time Drey reached the row the figure was the one read out. They were the Directorate's figures and they were right, and there was a small, exact satisfaction in hearing them carried correctly by someone who owed the Directorate nothing.

This was also the delegate who had released the general's sentence. The Command's communications office had circulated the list that morning: the stations carrying it, the nine seconds it had been cut to, the delegate's name against the first release. Along the row the word was *context*. Drey had been in the chamber. The recording was what the general had said, in open session, in answer to the chair, and she could find nothing in the release to correct.

She noticed the gallery. It had not come for nine days of these tables, and it had not come for them today. It was looking at the delegate, and the delegate was reading out what the hearing had been convened to find, to a room that already had the part it meant to keep.

When the session rose she went back to the Directorate. A request had come in that morning from a deep-range station, asking leave to set its own resupply interval without referral. She drafted the order herself, in the form she had been trained in and had since trained others in: the question, then what was known, then the recommendation, and the decision left where it belonged.

*Received, and correctly made. The change requested falls to the Standing Committee on Station Supply. Referred this day, with the Directorate's recommendation that it be granted. The originating station will be notified of the committee's finding.*

She read it through once for form and found nothing to correct. She signed it, and it went out with the evening signal, the same day it had come in.

The committee next sat in five weeks.
