:::pov jorun-madaki

The inbound hull had put down forty-one pallets at the second lock, and the manifest said forty-two.

Jorun Madaki counted them again before she wrote anything. She had kept Halyard's route book for nineteen years. In that time the manifest had been wrong more often than her count had, though never by so much that she could stop checking either. Forty-one. She walked the row a third time with the slate against her hip and found the forty-second behind a stanchion, where a loader had set it down to get at something else. Filters for the water plant. She entered the pallet, the lock and the hour.

The book was the station's own. The Command had never asked to see it, and for most of nineteen years that had suited her. In 2719 one line of it had gone to Mars anyway, by way of the Directorate's tables: *primary supply route, request to authorised order, one hundred and twenty-two days.* She had filed the request. She had counted the days as they went, one to a line, because a count nobody kept was a count somebody would later round.

Somebody had not rounded it. A delegate had read that row out in a hearing chamber as the row had it, table and row first and then the figure. Jorun had sent for the transcript when the session rose, for that page only. She had never mentioned it. The station had the nine seconds by then, and nobody on Halyard was asking after figures.

The convenor's clerk found her at the lock a little before the evening signal, with the sheet.

"Tonight's session," he said. "The seat."

She knew the sheet. The council sat fourteen, and for as long as she had been one of them a session's business had been walked round the station beforehand, and each member had initialled *content* at their place of work. The session itself took a quarter of an hour and was mostly the convenor reading out what had been agreed. It had been explained to her in her first year as a courtesy to people who worked. A council of shift-leads and plant hands could not sit four hours over a motion and then go on shift. That was true. She had not, in nineteen years, seen a motion fail in the room, and she had never been sure whether that was the same fact.

*That Petra Solano be appointed Halyard Station's delegate to the Oversight Council.* Thirteen sets of initials under it. A space for hers.

She read it the way she read anything that came to the lock, for what was on it and then for what was not. There was no term. There were no conditions. It was a manifest with no weights.

"Is there a term?"

"The convenor thought that could be settled after. She's only just in." The clerk nodded at the pallets. "Same hull."

Jorun thought the delegate had earned it. She thought so without difficulty; there was nobody else on the station who had read the charter, let alone argued it. She initialled the space. Then, because the slate was in her other hand and the habit was older than the council, she wrote one word in the margin beside her initials, small, with a query after it.

The clerk looked at it and did not say that the sheet had no margin for that.

::::: scene 2

:::pov solano

She had been on the station four hours, and the room was easy.

Solano knew the look of it before she had found a seat. Fourteen people along the council table, some still in plant overalls, sitting the way people sit through a reading. Nobody leaned forward. Nobody had notes. She had learned to recognise that ease in this room, years ago, and had then spent four years at a table on Mars where nothing was arranged and every clause took the morning, and she found she had lost the habit of mistaking it for agreement.

The delegation's copy of the charter was under her arm. She had meant to hand it to the convenor and go and sleep.

The convenor read the motion. He was a decent man who had run the air plant for twenty years and the council for nine, and he said some things about her that were kind and that she would have preferred he had not said in front of people coming off shift. Then he said, "Content, all fourteen," and looked up to close it.

"May I see the sheet?"

He passed it down. Fourteen sets of initials in fourteen hands. Beside one of them, small, a single word: *term?*

"There's no term on this," Solano said.

"We thought it could wait until you'd rested."

"The Accords give the station the appointing. For a term, and on conditions the station sets. I sat through the drafting of that sentence. It isn't decoration."

"Then we'll set them. Nobody here is going to quarrel with you over conditions, Petra." He said it warmly. He meant it. "Tell us what you can live with."

"That's the first reason I can't take it," she said. "The second is worse."

Nobody along the table had moved yet. She put the charter down in front of her and turned it to the page, which she could have found in the dark.

"The corps this makes has no jurisdiction on a planet or inside a habitat that governs itself. It comes in when it's asked and leaves when it's told. Those are the limits. This station's vote was the price of them, and I wrote the paragraph. Most of the words in it are mine." She kept her hand flat on the page. "The Council is the body that gets to ask the corps whether it kept to them. Some year a captain is going to stand somewhere he wasn't invited, for a good reason, and the Council will have to say what that paragraph means. If I'm in the seat I'll know what it means. I'll know what I meant. And I'll tell the room, and the room will take my word for it, because I was there."

"That sounds like a qualification," the convenor said.

"It's the old arrangement with a better conscience. A limit that only its author can read isn't one. It has to hold for somebody who comes to it cold. If it doesn't, I would rather this station found that out at the first session than the fortieth."

"Nobody else here has read it."

"No. That's what the seat wants."

He was quiet for a moment. He was not a man who took a refusal personally, which was most of why he had lasted nine years.

"Then give us a name," he said. "We'll walk it round tomorrow."

"I'm not going to do that either."

"Petra."

"If I name her you'll initial it. All fourteen. I watched this council do it for four years before it sent me anywhere." She looked along the table. There was no unkindness in the faces. There was a good deal of tiredness. "The hull goes inward in twelve days and the delegate has to be on it. You've a seat, a room and a quorum. I think you'll have to do it here."

"That's four hours," the convenor said. "These people are on at second signal."

"Yes," Solano said. "I know what it costs. I'm sorry for it."

She took the charter and went and sat at the side, on the bench the station used when it came to watch, which it very rarely did. For four years she had been the thing this council sent. She had never yet been in the room when it did not know what it would decide.

::::: scene 3

:::pov jorun-madaki

It took nearer five.

For the first of them nobody knew how to begin. The council had a form for reading out a decision and none for making one. The convenor, to his credit, said so, and then asked whether anyone wished to propose a name, in the voice of a man reading an unfamiliar gauge.

A plant hand proposed the convenor. He declined. He said he had spent nine years settling this council's answers before it met, that he had thought of it as sparing them, and that he was possibly the last person on Halyard who should be sent to a room whose whole business was to ask. He said it without heat. Jorun had not known he could see it, and thought better of him than she had that afternoon.

Then the dock lead from second lock said, "Madaki."

"No," Jorun said.

"She found the forty-second pallet today. I watched her walk the row three times."

There was some laughter. It was the first sound the room had made all evening that had not been arranged.

"I keep the book," Jorun said. "I can't keep it from a hull."

"Then say what you'd want," the dock lead said. "If it were somebody."

It was a fair question, and she had already written half the answer in a margin. She stood up to give the rest, because she was not standing for the seat and wanted that understood.

"Three years, and then it comes back to this room. Not *until she tires of it.* A report every quarter, written. What she asked, and what she was told. Both columns. Given here, in session, with the station on that bench if it cares to come." She did not look at the bench. "And she can be recalled. By a count. I mean hands, in the room, and a number entered. Not the sheet."

"For whoever it is," the convenor said.

"For the seat. It's the seat's conditions."

He added a fourth himself: that the delegate hold no other council office while she sat, since a person could not report to a body she was part of. Nobody disagreed. Then he asked for hands on the four together, and fourteen people who had initialled *content* for most of their adult lives looked at one another and raised them or did not.

Twelve to two.

The clerk had the minute form in front of him. It had a line for the motion and a line that read *content*, and nothing else. He looked at it for some time. In the end he wrote the two numbers in the margin, and Jorun, who could see the page from where she sat, did not say that the form had no margin for that.

There were three names by then: hers, the dock lead's, and a medic from the ring clinic who had not been asked and bore it well. The convenor called them one at a time. Jorun put her hand up for the dock lead.

Eight. Four. Two.

She had the seat by eight in fourteen. She sat with the figure and found that she was glad of the six. A count that came out fourteen to nothing was one she would have walked the row again for.

::::: scene 4

:::pov solano

Solano carried the charter across the room herself. There was no one else to do it and no form for it.

The woman who kept the route book was still in her seat, with a slate on the table in front of her. She looked like someone who had gone to a session to initial a sheet and been handed a posting. She was perhaps fifty. Solano had passed her on the ring a hundred times and knew her name and her lock and nothing else.

"This is the delegation's copy," Solano said. "It's the station's now. I'd read the preamble last."

"I read from the front."

"Then read from the front."

Madaki took it and squared it against the edge of the slate. She did not open it.

"You read our row," she said. "At the hearing. Table and row and then the days. A hundred and twenty-two. I had the page sent out."

Four years. Solano had been asked about the nine seconds in corridors, at locks, at a wedding. She had been asked on the hull coming home, by a rigger who wanted to know whether the general had looked at her.

"You're the first person on this station to mention it," she said.

"I'm not asking about it. I'm saying I counted them."

"I know you did. It was the only figure I had that nobody could round."

Madaki nodded, once, as if a tally had come out. Then she picked up the slate and the charter together and went out toward the locks, where she presumably had filters to sign for.

Solano stayed on the bench a little longer. The council had sent her to Mars with one instruction, to come back with something the station could use. She had come back with it twice now and both times it had been taken out of her hands, and the second time she had been the one to say so.

She did not know what Halyard would ask at the first session. She had not arranged it. She sat with that, and it was not comfortable, and she found she would not have traded it.

:::pov jorun-madaki

She read it that night at the supply office desk, after the filters, from the front.

She read it as she read a manifest. A manifest was a list of what somebody said was there, and the work was to walk the row. The preamble said what the corps was for. She let that go by; a thing's purpose was not a thing she could count. The limits she read slowly, the paragraph the delegate had kept her hand on, and found them plain. A person could stand at a lock with that paragraph and know whether a hull had been asked in.

Then the money. *No government appropriation. Data licensing. Operational fees tied to mapped fold routes. Voluntary outer-station contributions.*

She stopped there, with her finger on the line.

Halyard was an outer station. She knew what it had to spare in a year, to the pallet. She knew three stations along the same route that had less. *Voluntary* was a word she had seen on requisitions.

The route book would go to her second in twelve days. He was careful and quick and he rounded, and she would have to speak to him about it. She took down a new book from the shelf instead of thinking about that, a plain one, the kind she used for a route nobody had run yet, and ruled it in two columns. She headed one *Asked* and the other *Told*.

On the first line, in the first column, she wrote:

*Voluntary outer-station contributions. Who keeps the row? If one station gives and the next cannot, where is that written, and who in the corps may read it before a hull decides whose call to answer first?*

She read it through for what was on it and what was not. Then she dated it and left the second column empty, as it was.
