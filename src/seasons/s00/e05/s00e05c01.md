:::pov kirsi-lund

The sentence was given as a toast, which Kirsi had not known could be done with it.

It was the intake dinner, the tenth week, the long table in the cadets' mess with the new crest on the wall above it and a paler square of wall showing round the crest's edges where the old one had been larger. The senior officer present was a captain of engineers with thirty years in. He had stood to give the Command, as the form required, and had given it, and had then not sat down.

"You'll have noticed the crest is smaller than the one I was commissioned under," he said, "and the list of people we answer to is longer. You'll be told that's accountability. Some of it is. I'm not going to stand here and tell you that every finding against us was unearned."

He was not a loud man. The mess had gone quiet for him anyway.

"You'll also be told that a general of this Command disgraced it in nine seconds. I was on her staff for four years. She was the best engineering officer I served under, and she said in that chamber what every officer at this table has been taught, in open session, to people who did not want it said. She didn't trim it. I've not met many who wouldn't have." He lifted the glass. "*We tried democracy. The result was forty years of the Coherence Wars and three billion dead. I would rather be called a tyrant than repeat the experiment.* They called her one. — The General."

The mess stood. Kirsi stood with it. She was a cadet of ten weeks and did not think it was a room in which to find out what happened if she didn't.

She had been sixteen when the nine seconds ran. She remembered where: the long corridor outside the school hall in her district, a wall screen, the general's face and name and rank and nothing else. It had run for a week. Her mother had switched it off at home, each time, on the last word. Kirsi must have heard the sentence two hundred times before she was seventeen, and she had never once heard it said by someone who was for it.

It was the same sentence. That was what she kept returning to, with the glass in her hand. It had not been cut differently or said differently. The corridor had been certain what it meant and so was the mess, and she had now stood in both.

She drank, since that was the form, and sat when the captain sat.

Afterward, in the passage, a second-year told her which parts of the reading mattered. The intake had been issued it that morning: a staff paper in a grey cover, *On the Cost of Speed*, S. Drey, Solar Command, 2698. One page on its argument, due at first bell the day after next.

"One to three is the argument," the second-year said. "That's what the general was saying, with the footnotes put back. Four's conditions. Nobody examines four. Nobody's ever lost a mark on four."

Kirsi thanked her. It was the most useful thing anyone had told her all evening, and she wrote it inside the cover.

::::: scene 2

:::pov sildron-drey

The garden hall gave out seed trays on the first day of the quarter, and Drey had gone for hers.

It was the day's errand. She had learned, over three years, to plan a day as one plans a resupply, from what was actually in the store. There was a morning in it, most days. If the morning went on the walk to the hall and the queue and the walk to the car, then that was where it had gone, and the afternoon would be a chair. She did not resent the arithmetic. It was the first set of figures in her life that nobody else had to live under.

Two trays of dwarf beans, on her knee, in the seat nearest the door.

The car slowed before the fourth pressure door and stopped. The board over the door lit.

*HELD — SEAL INSPECTION, DOOR 4. ESTIMATE 9 MIN. INSPECTING: DISTRICT WORKS. HOLD REVIEWED BY: TRANSIT CONTROL. CANNOT WAIT? PRESS THE CALL PLATE. THE ATTENDANT WILL TAKE YOU THROUGH THE SERVICE LOCK.*

She read it through, as she read anything that had been drafted. She had been held at this door before and had read it each time. It was correctly formed. It gave the delay and its cause. It said who was imposing the delay and, on a separate line, who was checking them, and the two were not the same office. It told the person waiting what to do if the wait was more than they could bear, and whom they would reach.

She could not have written it. She knew that exactly, as she knew the trays' weight. Give her the door, the works schedule and a blank board, and she would have sat in front of the blank board. She could read one. That had come back, or had never gone.

Beside her a cadet in the new crest let out a breath at the board and opened a paper on her knee. It had a grey cover.

Drey did not need to see the title. They had not changed the cover in twenty-five years. It was the syllabus printing, the one set before every intake, and the cadet had it open near the end of the third section, where the history stopped.

After the history the paper went on to its conditions. There were three. That the delay be visible to those bearing it. That it be reviewable by someone other than the body imposing it. That the person waiting have a route to someone with the authority to shorten the wait. She had been proud of them. They were the reason the paper was longer than its argument.

She looked at the board over the door.

A works crew that had never been set the paper had put all three on one lit panel, in five lines, because a district of people trying to get home would not have stood for less. Nobody had needed to argue it.

She noted that, and held the trays level, and looked at her watch.

:::pov kirsi-lund

"Nine minutes," Kirsi said, to nobody.

The woman beside her did not take it up. She was sixty or so, in a plain civilian coat, with two seed trays on her knee and her back not touching the seat. Kirsi had grown up among people who worked for the Command and people who had, and could tell them on a car.

"Is that a real nine, do you know? Or a works nine."

"I have timed it on four occasions," the woman said. "It has come in under on each."

"You timed it."

"I had a watch and was not otherwise engaged."

Kirsi laughed before she could decide whether it had been a joke. The woman's face gave her no help.

She found her place in the paper and lost it again. Six minutes of a free afternoon. Her mother's table was three stops on, and the page was not going to write itself there either.

"Did you serve? You sit like the staff instructors."

"I did. I hold no commission now."

"Staff?"

"Operations."

"Then you were issued this." Kirsi tilted the cover.

"Every intake has been, since it was written."

"They say section four isn't examined. Were you ever examined on four?"

The woman took longer over that than she had over the nine minutes.

"No," she said. "I was never examined on it."

"So I can leave it."

"That is not what you asked me."

It was said without edge. Kirsi thought about it and found it was true. She tried again, the way they were being taught to put a thing to a senior. "Should I read it?"

"The question is whether to read the fourth section. What is known: it is the shortest of the four. The board gives you a little under six minutes. Nobody will ask you for it at first bell." The woman kept her eyes on the door. "I would read it. The decision is yours."

"That's a referral order."

"It is the only form I have."

So Kirsi read it, because the car was stopped and there was nothing else to do with a little under six minutes. It was four pages. It had three conditions in it, each a paragraph, each with its footnote. Visible to those bearing it. Reviewable by someone other than the body imposing it. A route to someone who could shorten the wait.

She looked up at the board. She had ridden under boards like it all her life and had never once read one to the end.

"It's the door," she said.

The woman said nothing. It had not been a question.

Kirsi went back to the footnotes, which was where the paper said how each condition would be met. *Visible*: the Directorate's own returns. *Reviewable*: by the standing committee concerned. *A route*: through the chain, to the reviewing officer. She read them twice.

"They're all inside. Every one of these goes to an office in the Command. Have I got that right?"

"You have read it correctly."

"Then who checked?" It was out before she had put it properly. "I mean, did anyone outside ever check that they held?"

"No."

Kirsi turned back a page, and then forward. The captain's glass, the mess standing. The corridor outside the school hall.

"They toasted the general's sentence last night. At the intake dinner." The woman did not answer, and Kirsi heard that she had done it again. "Were you there? At the hearing?"

"Yes."

"Was it like the clip?"

"The clip is what she said."

"The captain said she only told them what we're all taught." The woman waited. "Is that true?"

"Yes," the woman said. "That is also correct."

Kirsi sat with the paper open at section four. The general had said the first three sections in one sentence. The corridor had heard the sentence and the mess had heard the sentence, and nobody in either had been given these four pages. She had not been going to read them herself.

The door released. The board went dark. The car took up again as though it had never stopped.

"Seven minutes forty," the woman said, to her watch. "Under."

Kirsi got down at the third stop with her thumb still in the page. On the platform she looked at the cover again. *S. Drey, 2698.* A staff officer of her great-grandmother's time, she supposed; the people who wrote syllabus papers were generally dead. She wondered what Drey would have made of the door.

She wrote the page at her mother's table that evening. Three paragraphs on the argument, since the argument was what had been set. Then a fourth, short, giving the conditions. Under it she wrote one line more, because she could not see how to hand the page in without it.

*Query: by whom are the conditions tested?*

She expected to lose a mark for it. Nobody had ever lost a mark on four. She supposed somebody had to be first.

::::: scene 3

:::pov sildron-drey

She put the trays on the sill, where the dome light came longest, and sat down.

A dozen questions, near enough. She had not counted them, which was unlike her. She had found, in three years, that she could answer what she was asked. It was the other thing she could not do, which was to begin: to take a blank sheet and decide what went at the head of it. Asked, she could still do the work of an answer. She could put what was known in order and say where the decision lay. It cost what the walk to the garden hall cost, and she had paid both today, and the afternoon would be the chair. It was an errand's worth. It was not more than that.

The cadet had not asked her name. She had been glad of it on the car and was not sure, now, that glad was the word. Nobody had asked her whether the paper was right. She had been asked whether its last section was examined, and she had been able to say so truthfully from both ends of the question.

Maren had been three years her junior. She was three years dead. Drey had not heard the sentence given as a toast before, and she turned that over. She could find nothing in it to correct. It was what had been said. The officer who gave it had told a mess that the general said what they were all taught, and that was so, and Drey had written the teaching.

What she had not written was a door.

She thought about the board for some time. It had not been drafted by anyone who had read her. It had been drafted by somebody at District Works with a schedule of seals and a car full of people, and a second office down the line that would ask why if nine minutes became twenty. She would have liked to know who. She did not think she would write to find out. That would have been a beginning.

The trays wanted labelling. The garden hall asked for it, three lines on a card: what was sown, the day, and when to look.

She could do that. It was not a blank sheet. It had its three lines ruled.

*Dwarf bean. Sown this day. Look again in nine.*

She read it once for form and stood the card in the tray.
