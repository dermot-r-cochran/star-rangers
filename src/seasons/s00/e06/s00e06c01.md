:::pov mette-sarpong

The certificate blanks had come out from Mars with the old heading on them and a stamp.

There was an instruction with the stamp. Until new stock was printed, assessors of the former Authority were to strike through *Solar System Safety Standards Authority* at the head of each blank, in ink, and stamp *SAFETY CORPS* beneath it. The method of assessment, the instruction added, was unchanged.

Mette Sarpong had been an assessor for twenty-six years. She had certified pressure seals, life-support integration, and for the last nine the instruments that outer stations pointed at places where instruments misbehaved. She had been absorbed into a corps of the Star Rangers one season ago, by a charter she had not been asked about, and she had read the line in it that concerned her until she had it by heart: command might decide what her corps tested, and might not decide what it concluded. It was the Authority's own rule in somebody else's document. She did not yet know whether it would be kept. Nobody could know that from reading it.

She struck through twelve blanks and stamped them, and squared the stack. Then she opened the file.

The method was older than the Authority and older than the body before that, and it had four steps. Define what failure looks like. Test for it. Certify what does not fail that way. Keep the standard. She had never in twenty-six years been stopped at the first.

The board at Eden had five applications in front of it. Four were for crew on a fold-corridor transit, and they were ordinary: the people would be examined against a certified profile, and either they met it or they did not. The fifth was for their instructor. And there was no certified profile. Nobody had ever certified one. The transit had been flown out and back once, in 2712, by a cargo hauler with a crew of three. It had been flown out once more, two years later, by a ship that went on past it and whose record was not in her file. One of the three had applied.

So the profile came first, and the profile began with the log.

It was a good log. She read it through twice and then a third time against the analysis the charter cited, the chief scientist's, three hundred and forty pages of it. The hauler's drift-corridor telemetry had been run three times before the threshold. The threshold had come where the model put it. The margins were entered to more places than she would have asked for.

What she was looking for was the other entry, the one every profile she had ever certified was built around: the reading at which the crew would not have gone in.

It was not there. It was not in the log, the flight plan or the modification record. She looked for it under four headings and then stopped looking, because she had begun to see that it had not been left out. Nobody had written it.

There was one more paper in the file, a single leaf from the old Command's monitoring officer, dated the morning of the departure. *Manifest lists a transit profile inconsistent with any authorised operational envelope on file.*

She went down to the ring to see the file.

The monitoring post had come through the Accords with its counter intact. The officer behind it was young and exact, and he brought the binder to the counter without being asked twice. It had a tab: *Authorised operational envelopes — fold transit.* It had an index sheet, ruled, with the columns headed. It had been opened in 2712 and kept in perfect order since.

It had no entries.

"There's never been one lodged," he said. He seemed to feel he should apologise for the binder. "The note's accurate. It's been accurate each time."

"I don't doubt it," Sarpong said. "I wanted to see what it was accurate about."

She thanked him and went back up. The hauler had not flown outside the envelope. There had been no envelope. For eleven years the post had looked into that binder and written down, correctly, that it was empty, and gone on to the next thing.

That was not a failure she could certify against. But it told her where to put the first question.

::::: scene 2

:::pov kilbride

She had expected to be asked to fly something.

Kilbride had prepared for that. She had the corridor terms cold and the hauler's handling cold. In her jacket she had a folded sheet with six names on it, people she had watched in the simulator bay for three months and would have signed for. She had meant to hand it across at the end, when the flying was done, as the natural next item.

Instead there was a table with three assessors behind it and a woman in the middle with the 2712 log open flat, who looked up and said, "Captain Kilbride. I have one question about this, and then some smaller ones."

"Go on."

"At what reading would you have turned back?"

Kilbride opened her mouth to answer and found there was nothing in it.

It was a strange sensation. She had been asked about that morning by a recruiter, by officials of two Commands, by a great many people at a reception. Nobody had asked her that. She went back through it honestly, since she was in front of a board: the airlock, Renner's seals, Dez running the telemetry a third time. The model in her hands. She had read every figure on it. She had known what *right* looked like to four places.

"There wasn't one," she said.

The assessor did not write anything yet. "If the third telemetry pass had come back off the model."

"I'd have looked at how far off."

"And decided."

"Yes."

"Against what?"

"Against being there." It sounded worse aloud than it had been. It had not been recklessness. She tried to give the board the thing itself. "I knew the model. I'd overseen every hour on that hull. If something had read wrong I'd have known it was wrong, and I'd have weighed it. That's what a captain is for."

"I believe you would have," the assessor said. "I can't examine a trainee against it."

"No."

"Tell me what I'm missing."

It was a fair request, and Kilbride took her time over it.

"Nothing. You've got it." She looked at the log, her own hand, eleven years younger. "I didn't want a number. I'll say that plainly. A number's a door, and I've never trusted a decision that had one left in it. Once the corridor closes you can't reconsider, and I wanted to be someone who'd already finished deciding." She stopped. "That was right for me. I've spent three months trying to work out how to teach it, and that's the part I couldn't get onto a page. I had nothing to hand them for when to stop."

There was a man at the end of the table who was not on the board. Kilbride knew him: the Survey Corps' recruiting officer, who had once noted on the record how quickly she said yes. He had the look of someone watching a founding asset being weighed on an ordinary scale.

"With respect to the board," he said, "the corps has asked for the *Constant Margin* profile to be certified. The charter cites that transit. It's in the preamble. I'm not sure the corps can be in the position of flying on a record its own Safety Corps has failed."

"The corps asked the board to test it," the assessor said. "It's being tested."

"And if the finding is that the founding flight doesn't pass, the Defence Command will have that in every mess on Mars by the end of the quarter. *Unauthorised* was their word. You'd be handing it back to them countersigned."

He was not wrong. Kilbride could hear the sentence being said, and by whom.

"He's right about how it'll be used," she said. "And I still wouldn't send four people out on what I did."

The assessor looked at her for a moment, and then wrote for some time.

Kilbride remembered the sheet when the smaller questions were done. She took it out and unfolded it. "I've six people I'd put forward for the seats."

"The board doesn't take names from an instructor."

"I know them. I've watched them fly."

"That's why." The assessor said it without unkindness. "You'll answer for how they fly. If you also chose them, there'd be nobody left to ask whether they should. We examine whoever applies. Some of yours may apply."

Kilbride looked at the sheet. Three names had been enough for her once, and she had picked every one.

She folded it and put it back in her jacket.

::::: scene 3

:::pov mette-sarpong

The finding took her two days and came to a leaf and a half.

She wrote the profile first. *Eden corridor, to the confirmed transit point, and return.* The far limit was where the hauler had turned, since that was the last place anyone had a reading from. The board certified nothing past it, having nothing to test against, and said so in those words.

Then the entry that had not been there. It was hers to write and she did not enjoy it. A turn-back criterion wanted to come from a hundred transits, and she had the log of one hull, out and back. She took the model's own stated tolerance for the corridor's drift, and the three telemetry passes the hauler had logged, and set the line where the passes and the tolerance agreed: read three times before the threshold, and at this figure the hull does not go in. After the threshold there was nothing to write. The corridor closed. A standard could not put a door where the physics had none, and she noted that as a limit of the standard.

She added one requirement that was not about flying. Every order issued under the profile was to state its limits, and where they came from. She had spent a morning at a counter looking at a binder. She did not want the next assessor to have to go downstairs to find out what a transit had been measured against.

Then the two items the recruiting officer was waiting for.

*The transit of 2712, as flown: not certified.* She weighed the next sentence longer than any other on the leaf. *The board finds no fault in the flying. The transit carried no written criterion for turning back, and so nothing it could have failed against. The board cannot pass what could not have failed.*

*B. Kilbride: certified as instructor on the profile above. The 2712 log meets every limit this board has since written. The board notes that the limits were met without having been set.*

The recruiting officer read it standing. He read the second item twice.

"That isn't what the corps asked for."

"No."

"I could take it up the ladder."

"You could. They can tell me to test something else. They can tell me to test this again." She had the stamp in her hand and put it down, so as not to be holding it while she said the rest. "I don't know yet whether they can tell me what it says. I've read that they can't. This would be how we found out."

He looked at the leaf a while longer. He was a sincere man with a corps three months old and more mandates than officers, and she did not think less of him for wanting the founding flight to pass.

"It says there was no fault in the flying," he said.

"There wasn't."

"Then I'll carry it as written." He folded his copy. "I'd have liked the other finding. I'd not have liked how we came by it."

She entered that he had not asked again. It was not a thing the form had a line for. She put it in the board's own minute, since it was the first time anyone under the new crest had been in a position to ask, and one day somebody would want to know how it had gone.

The last copy went down to the ring.

The young officer took the profile at the counter and turned it over, and then opened the binder at the index sheet with its ruled columns. He entered the title, the certifying body and the date on the first line. His hand was very neat. He had plainly wondered for some time what an entry would look like.

"The body," he said. "The blank says Authority, struck through."

"Enter what the stamp says."

He did. Then he looked at his pad of note forms, the ones that read *inconsistent with*.

"I'll need a different note."

"I expect you will," Sarpong said.

:::pov kilbride

Her copy came up that evening. She read it in the simulator bay, with the lights down and nobody in the seats.

*Not certified.* She had known it would say that. She had more or less said it herself across the table. It was another matter to see it stamped.

She read the sentence under it, and then again. *The board cannot pass what could not have failed.*

Pitch had told her, that morning in 2712, that the model made the thing correct and not authorised, and that she had no data on what those weren't the same word for. Kilbride had said she understood the difference, and had believed she did. She had flown on *correct* and it had held, and for eleven years the holding had seemed to settle it.

She went to find the assessor before the board packed up. The woman was squaring blanks.

"So I'd have failed," Kilbride said.

"You couldn't have. That was the defect."

Kilbride considered that. It was not the kind of thing anybody had said to her at the reception.

"I'd still have gone."

"I know. I've read your log three times. I'd have wanted you to go with a line in it." The assessor fitted the stamp into its case. "You'd have ignored the line?"

"I'd have known where it was."

"That's all a standard is for."

The turn-back table was on the second leaf. Three passes, one figure, and under the figure the words *the hull does not go in*. Kilbride read it over until she could see what it was. The assessor had put a door into the thing she had flown because it had none. A stranger had cut it from her own three telemetry passes.

She did not know yet whether she could want one. She knew she would not take four people to the threshold without it, and that before long she would be asked to.

There were six names in her jacket. She took the sheet out, looked at it, and did not file it anywhere. Whoever the board sent her, she would check their seals herself.

Nobody had written a rule against that.
