:::pov voss

The request came up from the registry with the morning folio, third in the stack, under a routing slip that gave his name and no reason for it.

Voss knew the reason. His signature was on the Command's section of the charter, and the registry, which had been given no rule for paper from a body less than a year old, had sent it to the last officer known to have touched the subject.

It was one leaf. *Under the Schedule of Release to the Consolidation Accords, and under the founding research mandate of the Survey Corps, the Corps requests the inquiry record of the vessel* Patience First *and the instrument data lodged with it.* Then the items, numbered, seven of them. Then a name he had seen on a reception list and had not expected to see on a request: *B. Kilbride, for the mandate file, Eden Space Habitat.* She would collect in person, the leaf said, at the registry counter, that afternoon.

That afternoon. He read the date again to be sure he had it.

There was nothing in the leaf for a commodore to decide. The Accords had settled the file more than a year ago, by name, in a schedule he had sat through the drafting of: to the body the charter would create, on that body's request. The request was all that had been wanting, and here it was. The registry needed a countersignature. He gave it one.

Then he did what he had done at every desk he had held, which was to read the list against the thing it was a list of.

The seven items were the file. The committee's terms of reference, the classification and its signature, the Eden instrument record up to the corridor threshold, the inquiry's minutes. He knew them. He had first read them as a lieutenant, at a desk two floors down and one wing over, because he had asked for the desk that was nearest to them.

The log was not on the list.

It could not have been. Nobody who drew up the Schedule had known there was a log. It had never been part of the inquiry record. It was a correspondence office's own running book — what six families had written and said to the Command, and what the Command had said back — begun before his time at that desk, kept through it, and kept after it in hands he did not know. It had been forwarded to no one, because no one had asked. He had said so to himself often enough, over the years, that the sentence had stopped sounding like a complaint and had become a description.

He sent down for it. It came up in twenty minutes, which told him where it had been sitting.

It was thicker than he had left it. He did not read it through. He turned to the fourth visit, because he knew the page, and found his own hand of eight years ago: careful, slightly too large, the hand of an officer who had been told that a record was all he had the authority to keep and had taken the instruction literally. *Verbatim where possible.* He had underlined it. He did not remember underlining it.

He did not look for the last entry in her name. He had known for some years which page that was on.

The registry clerk's note, clipped inside the cover, was correctly made. *Unclassified. No retention hold. Not scheduled under the Accords. Status as Command record undetermined. Recommend referral to the Records Board.*

He looked up when the board next sat. It was nineteen days.

Voss sat with that for the length of time it deserved. He had spent four years at a table losing an argument about exceptions, and had come to think the losing was the best work of his service. He was not going to spend the first quiet season after it deciding that this one case was different because he happened to care about it. If the log was not his to release, then it was not, and nineteen days was the answer, and a pilot from Eden would go home with seven items.

So he read the regulation, which was short. An unclassified record of the Command, under no hold, could be supplied by any officer of flag rank to a body with standing to ask for it, and the supplying officer was to notify the board. Notify. The board's part came after.

He had wanted, at that desk, a rank that would let him close the gap a woman had described to him across a desk. He had the rank. It did not close anything. What it would do, he found, was let him hand a book across a counter on the day it was asked for, and write to a board afterward to say that he had.

It had not been asked for. He noticed that. The leaf said seven items.

He put the log under his arm and went down to the counter himself.

::::: scene 2

:::pov kilbride

The registry counter at Solar Command was the first place on Mars that had not asked her to wait.

Kilbride had come braced for it. She had dealt with this Command's predecessor for long enough, and what she knew about its counters was that a thing was never refused at one. It was received, and it went somewhere, and a note came back. She had a berth on the evening hull and had privately given the errand four days.

Instead there was a sealed case on the counter with seven tags on it, a release leaf with a commodore's countersignature already dry, and the commodore.

She knew him by sight. He had been across the reception hall on Signing Day, among the Defence Command's officers, a heavily built red-haired man who had stood through the speeches like somebody listening for errors. She had been told he was the one who had lost the jurisdiction argument. She had been told he had lost it well.

"Captain Kilbride," he said.

"Near enough. The corps is still deciding what the word weighs."

"The file is as listed. Seven items, sealed in the registry's presence, and the release is in order." He put his hand flat on a second thing, which was not in the case and had no tag: a bound book, thick, in the plain grey cover the old Command had used for anything it did not expect to show a visitor. "This is not on your list. I want to say that before I say anything else about it, because you're entitled to leave it where it is."

She waited. It was a habit from the corridor: when an instrument was about to tell you something, you did not talk over it.

"When the ship didn't come back, six families wrote to this Command. The Command had one sentence for them. There was an office whose work was to send it. That office kept a log, for its own use, of what the families wrote and said and what they were told in reply. Nine years of it. I kept part of it. It isn't evidence of what happened to the ship, Captain. Your analysts will find nothing in it. There is not a reading or a bearing anywhere in it."

"Then why is it on the counter?"

"Because your leaf says *the founding research mandate*, and the mandate says *the open questions*." He said it the way she had heard people quote a clause they had argued over one word at a time. "The inquiry record is this Command's account of why the questions weren't its to answer. That's the case. The questions themselves, as they were actually put, by the people who put them first — those are in here, and nowhere else. I can't tell you it's what you came for. I can tell you nobody has asked for it in nine years, and that I'd rather it were refused by someone who'd looked at it than kept by an office because no one knew to ask."

Kilbride looked at the book. She had come for the instrument record. She had been clear with herself about that on the way in: the mandate file at Eden had been opened in her name because the corps had, this season, more mandates than officers, and she had resolved to be the kind of file-holder who fetched data and left the conclusions to people certified to draw them.

"May I?"

He took his hand off it.

She did not start at the front. She let it open where it had been opened most, which is what a bound book will do if you let it, and it opened at a visit. *2715. Fourth family member received. In person.* A lieutenant's large careful hand. She read down the page standing at the counter with her berth four hours off.

*I'm not here about liability, Lieutenant. I know what that word means in your letters. I'm here because "unauthorised, therefore" is not an answer to the only question I actually asked, which was what happened to her.*

There was a name at the head of the entry. Okafor, A.

Wren had mentioned a sister. Once, in the instrument bay, in the way a person mentions the one relative who will not be impressed. Kilbride had not thought of it since. She had raised a glass on Signing Day to a name the charter did not print, and had thought, doing it, that she was the only person in that hall who would.

She read the last line of the entry, and then she read the lieutenant's initials under it, and then she looked up at the commodore, who was looking at the release leaf.

She did not say anything about the initials. He had not, and it was his to say.

"The charter has this in a committee's words," she said instead. "Four lines down from the assets. I've read that clause a good many times. I took it for the corps saying it hadn't stopped looking." She closed the book. "It reads differently beside this. It reads like a reply."

"It isn't one yet," Voss said. "A clause in a charter doesn't arrive anywhere. Somebody would have to send it."

"Yes."

"The addresses are in the log. Some will be nine years old."

"Then some of them will come back," Kilbride said, "and I'll know which."

She signed for the case on the registry's leaf, seven times, because the registry wanted seven. For the book there was no leaf; the Command had never drawn one up for a thing it had not expected to part with. She took one of the corps' own from her folio. It was a short form. The corps did not have many yet and had kept this one to four lines: what was handed over, by whom, to whom, and its state.

Under *state* the form offered three words, to be struck as applicable. *Received. Read. Answered.*

She struck two and left the first, dated it, and tore him his half.

:::pov voss

He took the half-leaf back upstairs and wrote to the Records Board before he did anything else.

He wrote it in the form he had been trained in. He had no other, and had found over four years of charter drafting that he did not want one; the form had never been the fault. The question, then what was known, then what had been done. *Supplied this day to the Survey Corps, on its request under the Schedule, items one to seven. Supplied with them, as an unclassified record under no hold, the running log of the former Casualty Correspondence Office, 2714 to 2723. The Corps' receipt is attached. Notified to the Board for its record.*

It took him six minutes. He read it once for form and sent it down.

The board would sit in nineteen days and would find the notice among its papers. It might find that a commodore had read the regulation correctly. It might find some ground on which he had not, and say so, and he would answer it. Either way the book would be at Eden, with someone who had read a page of it standing up.

That left the half-leaf.

The session book was in the second drawer. He had closed it the evening of the signing, with the last sitting entered, and had not expected to open it for anything but reference. Four years of who had spoken, to which clause, and what they had said. It had no heading under which a receipt belonged.

He opened it anyway, dated a line, and entered the receipt as he had entered everything else, verbatim, since it was short enough to manage: what had been handed over, by whom, to whom. Then the one word she had left standing, and after it the two she had struck through, with the strokes shown, because that was how the leaf read and he had never yet improved a record by tidying it.

*Received.* Then *Read*, and *Answered*, each with her stroke through it.

He had kept that log while the desk was his, and it had been kept after him, and across nine years it had gone out to no one and had nothing come back.

It had a line in reply now. One word of three.

He did not know where Adaeze Okafor was this evening. He thought, for the first time, that somebody had been given the means to find out, and that it was not him, and that this was correct.

He closed the book and signed the day's orders.
