::::: scene 1

:::pov pitch

The order reached her desk at ten past two, station time, which was not an hour she had ever been sent anything.

It was a single leaf under the Survey Corps' heading. *Training transit. Eden corridor, to the confirmed transit point, and return.* Hull, complement, instructor. Then two lines she read slowly, because she had spent a career reading orders and had not seen either of them on one.

*Envelope: corridor transit per the Pitch model.*

*Limits of the envelope, and their source: the model is confirmed for the corridor transit only; the terminus remains unconfirmed (Pitch, analysis, p. 190). This order does not extend past the confirmed point.*

And at the foot, under the duty officer's initials, a ruled space with two words beside it. *Checked by.*

There was a covering line in a hand she knew. *Undock is twenty to four. If it's wrong, say so before then. If it's right, sign it. — B.K.*

Pitch put the leaf down on the desk and looked at it from a little further away, as she did with a reading she did not yet believe.

Page one hundred and ninety. She had written that page before either ship flew and had not expected it to be read. It was where the model said what it was not. A committee had passed over it on the way to a footnote. An analyst with four years' wanting behind her had learned it well enough to quote and had filed it where confidence keeps such things. Now it was the limits line of an operational order, cited by page, and somebody had sent it up at ten past two in the morning to be told whether they had it right.

They did not have it right.

It was one word, and she saw it at once. She had been waiting a long time for someone to get near enough to make the mistake. *The terminus remains unconfirmed.* A thing is unconfirmed when it has been predicted and the prediction is still out. She had predicted nothing about the terminus. The model stopped before it. There was no claim of hers standing at the far end of that corridor waiting on evidence, and there never had been, and the difference was not a nicety. It was what she had tried to say to Wren Okafor across this desk, in different words.

She struck *remains unconfirmed* and wrote above it: *has not been measured. The model makes no statement about it.* She initialled the change, gave the time, and sent the leaf back down.

Then she waited, which she knew how to do.

It had taken the old Command fourteen months to cite her and it had never once written back. She had learned the length of that silence the way a person learns the length of a room in the dark. She made tea. She did not expect to finish it before anything happened, and she expected nothing to happen.

The leaf came back at nine minutes to three.

The limits line had been reset, clean, with her words in it and her initials carried into the margin as the source of the amendment. Nobody had softened it. Nobody had put a note beside it asking whether the distinction was material. The duty officer had initialled again. The ruled space at the foot was still empty.

*Checked by.*

She had built the thing to be checked. She had said so, to herself, at this desk, nine years ago, on a morning when it had just been believed in instead. She had not at any point supposed that checking would turn out to be a line on a form with a space for her name. She had thought it would be an argument.

She signed it. It went down. There were forty minutes left, and she found she had nothing to draft.

That was new. In 2712 she had stayed at this desk and begun a report in whichever tense turned out to be true. In 2714 she had stayed and begun another. She had told herself both times that a chief scientist at a docking ring was a liability, and it had been true both times, and both times she had known it was not the reason.

This morning there was no report to begin. The order had the model's limits in it already. The ring had a reason to expect her: her name was on the leaf.

She looked for the other reason, the one under the true one, and it was not there either.

She left the tea and went down.

::::: scene 2

:::pov kilbride

Four names on the manifest besides her own, and she had picked none of them.

That was the first thing the charter had taken off her, and she noticed it every time she read the list. On *Constant Margin* she had overseen every hour of modification under all three names because it was the only way she would sign her own. The corps had a certification board now. It had sent her four people who had passed it, and her signature went under theirs as instructor. That meant she was answerable for how they flew, and somebody else was answerable for whether they should.

She had thought she would mind. She found that she checked the aft seals herself anyway and that nobody on the board would have wanted her not to.

The Defence Command's officer was at the ring. The post had survived the Accords, as posts do, and the man in it was young and correct and had a note to file. Kilbride had signed two of his predecessors' notes in her time and could have recited them. He showed her this one because the form required that she see it.

*Manifest lists a transit profile consistent with the authorised operational envelope on file.*

"You'll want my signature," she said.

"It isn't required for a consistent profile, Captain. Only for an inconsistent one."

"Then you've nothing for me to sign."

"No." He seemed to feel this as a gap in the morning. He filed it anyway.

The corrected order was clipped inside the hatch where all four trainees would pass it. She had put it there on purpose. One of them, the navigator, a quick young woman who had the model's corridor terms by heart, stopped at the limits line and read the amendment and its initials.

"She changed a word."

"She changed what it claims," Kilbride said. "Read it again and tell me what we know about the far end."

The navigator read it again. "Nothing. It doesn't say it's dangerous."

"It doesn't say it's anything. That's the line you're flying to this morning, and you're turning round on it. Not because the order's timid. Because that's where the knowing stops, and you'll be the one carrying back whatever we add to it."

She had not planned to say that much. She had said a version of it once before, in an instrument bay, to someone who had asked whether the corridor got easier, and it had not been enough. Then there had been nothing behind the saying: no order, no limits line, no desk at Eden where the log would be read the day it came home. There had been one pilot's opinion. A person who has waited four years to go is entitled to weigh a pilot's opinion and set it down.

She did not think an order would have stopped Wren Okafor. She was not sure it should have. She thought only that this navigator would go out this morning knowing exactly where the edge of the page was, in the hand of the person who drew it, and that it was more than anyone had been in a position to give either ship before.

It came to her, checking the last seal, that she was about to take a hull into the corridor on a decision that had a door in it. *To the confirmed transit point, and return.* She had never found a way to want one of those. A decision had only ever been real to her once the way back had sealed.

The corridor would still seal. No charter had changed that. Nothing would outrun the hull this morning either, and for as long as they were in it they would be past anything Eden could say to them. What was different was at the other end, and it was small. Somebody would be at the ring when they came back, with the authority to read what they brought and act on it before the week was out. That was the door. It did not open onto a way to un-go. It opened onto somewhere to bring it.

She found she could want that. It surprised her less than it would have a year ago.

At a quarter past three she looked up from the seal and Dr. Pitch was standing at the head of the passage, in a grey jacket, with her hands in its pockets, like someone who had not been sure the place would be where the plans said.

Kilbride had undocked from this ring eleven years ago with that woman three decks away at a desk. She did not remark on it.

"It was wrong," Pitch said.

"I saw. It's right now."

"One word."

"It's the one my navigator stopped on." Kilbride closed the seal. "The book's on your desk, Doctor. Under the draft. I've written everything in that letter I'm in a position to write. I can fly your model. I can't say what it claims as exactly as the one person who never let it claim more, and they're owed it exactly. Fourth entry, if you only read one."

"I read it last night."

"Then you know who you're writing to."

Pitch looked past her at the hatch, and at the order clipped inside it, and at four people who had been at school when *Constant Margin* flew, getting on with their checks.

"I'll stay until it closes," she said. "If that isn't a liability."

"It's on the order," Kilbride said. "You're allowed."

::::: scene 3

:::pov pitch

The corridor took the hull at the threshold the model gave, and this time a board at the ring showed the margin as it was logged, and a copy went to her desk before she had walked back to it.

She set it aside to check in the morning. It would keep. That was new as well.

The book was where Kilbride had said. A grey cover, the old Command's, and on it a half-leaf of the corps' own with three words printed under *state*. Two had been struck at a counter on Mars. One of the two strokes had since been crossed through and initialled, *B.K.*, with a later date than the first. *Received. Read.* The third word was still struck.

Under the book was the draft.

Kilbride's part was short, and Pitch read it as she would have read a colleague's method. It said who was writing: the Survey Corps, which had not existed when the ship left. It said that under the corps' charter the question of what happened to *Patience First* was not closed and could not be closed by classification. It said what was in the file and that the file was now at Eden. It said that the writer had trained beside one of the six and had flown the corridor before them. It made no promise. It did not say *recovered* and it did not say *lost*. It gave a desk and a name, and said that a letter sent there would be opened by the person named.

Then a gap, ruled off, with a pencilled note. *What the model says. Yours.*

Pitch opened the lower drawer.

The report was there. She had not filed it. The inquiry had asked her for the instrument record and had not asked her for anything else, and a report needs someone to be sent to. She had begun it in the autumn of 2714, the morning the ship left, and she had kept it honest in the only way open to her, which was to keep every version. There were pages in the past tense, begun in the second year. There were pages in the conditional. There was one, from the week of the hearing, that she had started in the future and had not got to the end of the sentence.

She read the fourth entry in the book again. It was not long. A woman had come to Mars in person to ask an institution what had happened to her sister, and had been told, by a young man who wrote down that he had told her, that the institution had authority to answer a different question.

*What happened to her.*

Pitch could not answer it. She had known that for nine years. What she had not seen, until she had the question in front of her in the words it was asked in, was that she had been the one person who could say exactly how far the not-knowing went and where its edge was, and that no one had ever needed that from her except a crew who already had it by heart.

She did not use any of the drafts.

She wrote it new, in the gap, in her own hand.

*I built the model the ship flew on. It describes the corridor, and the corridor has behaved as it describes on every transit flown. It does not describe the far end. Nothing at or past the terminus had been measured when* Patience First *left, and nothing has been measured there since. I said this to Dr. Okafor before she went. She had read it already and could give me the page. She went knowing it. I cannot tell you what happened to her. No one can yet, and I will not let my work be read as though it could. — Y. M. Pitch, Eden Space Habitat.*

Present tense. It was the one that had turned out to be true, and she did not know for how long.

She read it against page one hundred and ninety, since that was what it would be checked against, and found nothing to correct.

There would be six copies. They would go out the way the form letter had gone, by the ordinary mail, to addresses some of which were nine years old. Kilbride had said that some would come back and that she would know which.

Pitch drew the half-leaf toward her. *Received. Read.* And the third word, under its stroke.

She considered it for some time. The letter would be sent, and it was a true letter. It was not an answer. The form had only the one word for what came after reading, and whoever had struck it at that counter had been right to.

She left the stroke where it was. In the margin beside it, small, she wrote *Replied to*, and the date.
