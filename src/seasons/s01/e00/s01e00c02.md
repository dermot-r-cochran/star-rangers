The household sage did not believe in comfort dressed up as ceremony, which was the only reason Elvira trusted him with this. He kept the house's systems, or they kept him; the household had never settled which, and called him its sage. She found him at the long table in the record room, on bare feet as always, writing by hand. One display sheet lay beside his page, grey and still.

"The cat," he said. Not a greeting.

"Her collar carries a tag."

"It does. An ordinary one." He drew the sheet between them. "It answers this house and nothing else, and the house hears it for three hundred metres past the wall. That is by design. A tag that answered anyone would be a way of following whoever wore it."

The sheet drew the garden in plain line: the terrace, the sundial, the gate. Then five marks beyond the gate, a minute apart, each a little further down the lane.

"Five fixes," he said. "Then nothing. She did not stop and she did not turn."

"Downhill."

"Toward the harbour. That is a heading. It is not a place." He looked at her over the sheet. "The house keeps two drones for the roofs and the field line. It may send them over the public road and the quay if it files a notice with the town, and the notice is open. By morning anyone who cares to look will know that Dún Rí flew its drones at night for a cat. You are certain?"

She had been taught that overreacting to a small absence showed the household only that she could be provoked. This was not small.

"I am."

He did nothing she could see. Somewhere above them a hatch opened and closed.

The sheet redrew itself at intervals, without hurry. The first drone went high, to watch the hill for anything small and warm, and found two foxes and nothing else. The second went down the lane at the height of a cat's shoulder and read the stone. It found what a person with a lamp would have walked past: a dark spot at every fourth step, where a split pad had opened again.

Elvira had watched her favour that paw for two days. That afternoon she had held her up by the scruff and told her there was nothing there.

The spots went down the hill, along the foot of the harbour wall and out onto the quay. They ended at its edge, at a length of it where three vessels had lain that afternoon and none lay now. On the last coping stone there was one whole print, the split showing across the pad as a line. The sheet showed it at the size of her own palm. She did not touch it.

"The harbour's register is open," the sage said. "Three coasting vessels cleared on the evening ebb. None declared a port, and none of that size is required to. The house has asked all three. All three say they carry no cat."

"Which is what a master would say who had not looked."

"Yes."

"And the harbour itself?"

"Saw nothing. It was built not to look. It counts hulls and tides and keeps no pictures of who walks its quay." He said it as a thing he approved of, even tonight.

The first drone followed the river down to the bar, which was as far as the notice ran. From there it could still count three vessels, two standing east and one turning north along the coast, and could say nothing about what any of them carried. The second searched the water under the quay, and along the line the ebb would have taken something small, and found nothing. A few lines came up at the foot of the sheet in the house's plain type, and Elvira read them herself.

*Trace ends at the quay edge. Two readings fit: aboard one of three vessels, or in the water. The water search is not proof, and the house has no figure to offer for either.*

She read it twice. It was the first thing anyone had told her that evening that she entirely believed.

"What have you found?" the sage asked. It was the question he had asked her since she was small.

"A direction. Down the hill, onto the quay, out on the ebb." Her voice did not shake. "That is all this tells me. Not where. Not whether."

He inclined his head. Then he turned the sheet to something she had not asked for: the same garden, and every fix the collar had given the house since the spring, laid down together. The marks lay thin along the paths and thick in three places. The sundial. The east wall. The old well.

"The house has no instrument that reads anything at those three," he said. "I have looked. It has this."

Elvira knew the three places. She stopped at them herself, and had told no one in the house, and had never thought to write down when.

"Then the next move is yours," he said.

She took the sheet. She would not wait at the gate for the cat to find her way back, and she would not treat a heading as if it were an answer. She would go down at first light, to the harbour register and then to each of the three masters at whatever port would admit to them, and she would not stop because the first of them told her there was nothing to find.

Before she slept she wrote down, as near as she could recall it, the hour she had last stopped at the sundial. It was one line. She dated it.
