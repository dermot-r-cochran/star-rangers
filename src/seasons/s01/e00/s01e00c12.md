::: pov agnete-solvang

The relief hull brought the date in, as every relief hull had, and with the date it brought the new sheet.

The platform filed one return a year. Solvang had filed two of them and this would be the third, and the third was the one that went home with her, since the relief list and the return went up in the same bag and she was on both. The sheet had not changed in twenty years. The platform's state. The dose log, summarised. The hourly launches, summarised, which was the shortest line on the sheet and the only one that took the whole year to write: item fourteen, run on the hour under full lattice power, absorbed below the board's threshold within light-minutes, fault register clear, every hour of every day since the platform was crewed, and the summary this year read as the summary had read for twenty years — there was no second way to summarise a thing that had happened eight thousand seven hundred and sixty times since the last return without once happening differently. The catalogue's additions. The relief list. And at the head of it, above the station's name, the line the Archive called *Membrane*, where every return the platform had ever filed wrote the one word the first crews had chosen and nothing more official had displaced.

She wrote it. *Deadwater.*

Under it, where there had been the station's name, there was now another line, and the station's name had moved down to make room. *Zone.* Behind the sheet, clipped to it, was a circular from the Archive Branch, two pages, which she read standing, because the hull's bag closed at the sixth hour and three years as a Section Lead had taught her that a circular read sitting down took the afternoon.

The circular said what she had known since her certification and had never had occasion to write on a form. A [membrane](/star-rangers/lore/concordant-membranes/) was a universe, the whole of one, as the manifold saw it from outside. A [Concordant Zone](/star-rangers/glossary/concordant/) lay inside one, and was the region a rule set held in, and was never smaller than everything an instrument could see. Older filings had used one word for both, the Archive now held that this was the commonest error of scale in its own records, and the correction was to carry two lines where there had been one. *Where the scope of a finding is the zone, write the zone.*

She looked at the line for the length of time the line deserved, which was not long, and did not fill it.

It was not that she had nothing to write. The platform had measured for twenty-one years, in one system, with instruments that came home and passed their audits, and in twenty-one years every measurement had given the same answer, and the answer was the one on the membrane line. The medium absorbed. The near stars' light came in with the ordinary constants in it, year on year, as far as the chart ran. The system boat had gone out to the fourth planet's moons this year and come back after nine weeks with rock, ice, a thin atmosphere on the largest and nothing in any of it more or less promising than any moon's, and the launch from the boat's own small lattice at the far end of the run had fallen the way the platform's fell. The rule set held everywhere the platform had been. Nothing the platform had reached had an edge in it.

So the zone was the one they were in, and she could have written that, and at home the sheet wrote it as nothing. The home stations filed the same return. She had filed it from two of them, before this posting, and the membrane line on a home return was blank, had always been blank, and was blank for the reason that the first crews here had needed a word and nobody at home ever had. A blank on this sheet would say the platform's year had been measured where the home stations measured theirs. That was false. A word would say the platform had a name for the zone, and it had none, and had not met an edge to name it from.

She wrote on the back, under *Remarks*, in the plain terms the Archive preferred: *Zone line not filled. The platform has no name for it and has not met its edge. Left for the Archive.* Then her initials, and the date the hull had brought.

The relief list was under the return, and she read it as she had read the two before, with the second column first.

Seven on the platform. Three going home on the hull. Two at term, herself and the Senior on the instrument side, and one, a Deputy on her own branch, at seven months of it. The Medical Corps' column ran down the right-hand edge, and against the Deputy's name it carried the Medical Corps' initials and nothing else. The Corps had stopped writing *radiation* in that column some years before Solvang came, and had not written anything in its place, and had initialled the space where the word had been, which made the space a finding. Against her own name the column was blank. That was a different blank. She did not know of a sheet anywhere in the Corps that told the two apart, and she did not think the Medical Corps had one either, and she did not write to ask.

The Deputy had done good work on the moons. His sections of the catalogue were the cleanest the platform had filed in her time, and he had asked for nothing, and he had been found twice in the common room in the platform's night with the chart in front of him, not reading it, and had said he was fine. She had not asked him what he was looking at. She had been looking at it for three years and knew.

The chart was on the table, as it always was, and she did not look at it on her last morning. She had read down the route column her first morning here, star after star, *none* against each in the same hand, and had read it most mornings since, and the column had not changed, and she had gone through the term it was supposed to cut short and reached the end of it at the ordinary time with the ordinary word in the ordinary place, *term*, and no idea why the column had let her. She did not think it was anything she had done. She did not put it in a column either.

She put the return in the bag with the relief list under it, and the bag went home by the route at the sixth hour. Nothing else on the platform went anywhere.

::: pov benedek-ronai

The Deadwater returns came to him as a run of twenty-one, because the pass was going through every filing the correction touched, and a platform on a membrane was every filing a platform had ever sent.

Rónai had been on the pass since the start of the year. The 2826 pass was the Archive's own and ran on the Archive's own authority, desk by desk, and the desk he was on was scale: every sheet in the Survey Archive that said *membrane* where it had meant the zone, or the zone where it had meant the membrane, or had carried one line because the sheet of its day had only one. Most of the work was reading. A filing that had used the one word for twenty years had, almost always, used it correctly for twenty years, because the only membranes anybody had filed from were the three or four with names, and the name had always been the membrane's. The correction was to the sheet, not to the record. He had initialled a great many sheets since the year began and altered very few.

The Deadwater run was correct in every line it had. Twenty returns, *Deadwater* at the head, the crews' word kept by the registry as the registry kept what it was sent. The twenty-first had the new line and did not fill it, and on the back, in a Section Lead's hand, the reason.

*The platform has no name for it and has not met its edge. Left for the Archive.*

He read the remark twice, which was once more than it needed, and the second time was for the shape of it: she had declined to supply a word, and had said why, and had said whose the line was. That was a correctly filed blank. The Archive had a doctrine for correctly filed blanks and he had been trained on it.

The line was his to fill. The circular said so. He sat with it for the better part of an hour — which the line did not deserve and the file did.

A name was out. The registry named what it met, and a name went into a file when a crew sent one, and no crew had sent one, and the Archive did not put into a file a word that nobody had carried to it. Twenty years of the membrane line said exactly that: the registry had kept *Deadwater* because the crews had chosen it, and had not improved on it, and had not been asked to. If he wrote a name on this line it would be the first name on any sheet in the Survey Archive that had come from a desk and not from a hull.

A blank was out too, and that was the part the circular had not foreseen. On a home return both lines were blank. He had the home returns on the same desk, a stack of them, waiting their turn in the pass, and the blank at the head of each one meant what it had always meant: *here. Ours. The one we are in.* A blank on the Deadwater sheet would read, to anyone who read the two sheets side by side, as the same word. It would say the platform's twenty-one years had been measured in the zone the home stations measured in. The Section Lead had seen that and said so without saying it, and he thought she had been right not to say it, because the sheet was not the place.

What the line needed was a word that was not a name and was not nothing, and the file had one word in it.

He wrote *Deadwater's*.

The membrane's own word, with the mark after it: the zone that universe held, as far as anyone had stood in it — which was one. It claimed nothing the platform had not measured, and named nothing it had not met. If a second zone were ever met in that membrane the line would need a second word, and somebody would have met it, and they could send one.

He took the sheet to the Principal, because a line filled at a desk and not from a return went up one desk before it went into the file, and she read it where he had written it.

"That isn't a name."

"No."

"The circular asks for the zone."

"That is the zone." He had the home stack in his other hand and set it on her desk beside the sheet, with its blank head uppermost. "It is the only one anyone has stood in there, and it is the one word anyone has sent. A blank would say they were here."

She looked at the two sheets, the one with the word and the one with the space, for as long as he had, near enough, and initialled under his initials, and gave him back both.

"When they meet a second one," she said, "they can tell us what to call it."

"Yes, Principal."

He filed the Deadwater run. Then he drew the home stack toward him, because it was next in the pass, and turned up the first return, from a Tier 1 staging site the record did not need to name, and read the head of it. *Membrane.* Blank. *Zone.* Blank. He had read that head ten thousand times in his service and had read it, every time, as two lines nobody had needed, the way a form carries a line for a middle name.

He did not write in them. Nobody ever had. He had thought, until that afternoon, that those were the same thing.

The receipt came in early summer, to the posting she had been given on the home side, which was a staging site and had a chart on its table too.

The Archive sent a receipt for every return, a copy of the head of the sheet with the Archive's marks on it, so that the officer who had filed it could see what the file now said. She had never read one past the first line before. She read this one.

*Membrane: Deadwater.* Her hand.

*Zone: Deadwater's.* Not her hand. The platform's word with a mark after it, and the mark was the Archive's, and under it two sets of initials she did not know.

She had left the line for the Archive and the Archive had put in it the one word the platform had sent and nothing the platform had not. She read it as she had read the route column her first morning there, for longer than a line needed, and then she put the receipt in the file where receipts went.

The relief list had come back with it, as it did, with the Archive's stamp on the Medical Corps' column. Against the Deputy's name, the initials. Against hers, nothing.

Nobody had been asked to fill that one.
