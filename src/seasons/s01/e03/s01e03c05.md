They came out along the causeway at the hour when the stones stopped being warm, and Elvira knew before they reached the outpost that something had been signed.

Not here. The causeway was quiet in the way it had been quiet since the grove, which was the ordinary way, and she had stopped checking it a week ago and then noticed that she had stopped, and filed both. Whatever had been signed had been signed somewhere else. Thorne carried it the way he carried everything he was not going to say: the cup too hot, held level.

The Krenyi analyst carried nothing she could read at all, and Elvira noted that too, because it was unusual for her not to be able to read a person, and because she did not find it uncomfortable.

"We want the books," Syra said, at the door. No greeting; Elvira had not expected one and would have revised her model of the analyst downward if one had come. "The ones from before you. The station's forty seconds is older than the station's file. Yours is older than the station."

"Mine is older than me," Elvira said. "Come in."

She took the predecessor's books down from the shelf above the water vessels. Eleven volumes, the hand getting worse across them, the last one stopping in the middle of a page. Syra took the first and sat down with it at the composite table and became, immediately and completely, a person reading, and did not move again.

Thorne stayed in the doorway. He looked out at the stones.

The light was going. It went differently here in winter, not down but sideways, and the old post at the end of the causeway, the one the reeds had half taken, did the thing it did at this hour, which was to stand in a light that was not quite the light everything else was standing in.

"There," Thorne said. He was tired enough to be talking to himself. "By the post. Where it's —"

And he said the word.

It was a folk word. It meant nothing. It was the word the kitchen at Dún Rí had used for a draught with no door behind it, for a candle that leaned the wrong way, for the places on the terrace where the cat would not sit. She had used it herself, once, when she was small, and the household sage had looked at her over whatever he was writing and said: *Not that. That word is for people who have decided not to look. Look at what's under it. The layer underneath.*

She had not liked the word since.

She had not liked it on a terrace step in 2806 UCSD, in the last of an autumn afternoon, with a boy of seventeen standing at the bottom of the garden with his hands in his pockets saying it about the light beside the gate — the badly sited gate, the one with the latch, where the cat would one day stand for one breath and then turn the other way. His father had been in the house with the warden about the field line. The field line had been an argument for a century and would be one for a century more and nobody on either side minded very much, which was why it was still an argument. The boy had wandered off the terrace because the field line was not interesting and the garden was. He had looked at the light by the gate and said the kitchen's word for it, in the voice of someone who had been taught the word at a kitchen table too, and she had been annoyed in the exact and limited way she was annoyed now, twenty years later, in a doorway on a marsh.

*No,* she had said. *Look at what's under it.*

*I don't know what's under it.*

*The layer underneath. It moved. Not the ground. The layer underneath the ground. Watch it again and you'll see it isn't the light that's wrong.*

He had watched it again. He had been right to; it had moved again; she remembered that he had not said anything clever about it afterwards, which she had thought well of, and that his father had called him from the house, and that he had gone.

That was all. She had not kept him. She had not kept the afternoon. There had been no reason to; he was a boy from a house across a field line, and the light by the gate had been doing that for years, and nothing he had said or she had said had done any work. She had not *forgotten* it, because forgetting was a failure of keeping and she had never undertaken to keep it. It had simply not been there, until a tired man in her doorway used the word, and then it was there whole, with the step cold under her and the smell of the roses that had no business blooming so late.

Elvira stood very still in the outpost and did what she did with a failed circle, which was to take it apart in order.

The man first. He was the boy. The beard and the twenty years and the Corps had not changed the thing she had noticed on the step, which was that he looked at a wrong thing twice before he said anything about it. She had revised her model of him upward three times in the autumn for that quality, and had not once thought to ask where he had learned it. She had assumed six stations. It had been one garden.

The words second, and this was the part she minded. *The layer underneath.* She had written, in her own hand, in her own record, that she had built her vocabulary alone at this outpost out of nothing, because the official glossary had no term for a thing she could measure. She had believed it. It was wrong in the one way she cared about: not the idea, which was hers, but the words, which were not. The words were the sage's. She had been given them as a girl, had given them to a boy on a step because he had used a word she did not like, had come to an outpost seventeen years later and found she needed a term, and had reached for the one that was there and called it her own. It had been a reconstruction. She had filed it as an invention.

She noted, for the record, that it was the first time in three years she had been wrong about a word.

The third thing she took apart last, because it was the one that was hers. *She just didn't like the word I'd used.* He had said that at this table, a month ago, with the file squared under his hands, and she had stopped reading the page in front of her and had not known why. She knew why now. He had been carrying it for twenty years, through six stations and a marriage he did not talk about and a Corps that had no entry for the phrase, and she had let it go before his father's voice had finished calling him in.

Behind her, Syra turned a page.

The predecessor had been a careful keeper and then had stopped being one, and Syra could date the change to within a season from the hand alone.

That was not what she had come for. She noted it and kept reading. The books were measurements: water levels in four vessels, the phase of the moon against the state of the stones, the dreams, which the predecessor had entered in the same column as the weather and which Syra approved of entirely. The forty seconds was not in them. Nothing in them was forty seconds. The outpost had never kept a clock good enough to notice, which was itself a finding, and she wrote it down.

She was aware of the room, because she was always aware of the room; it cost her nothing and she had never learned how to stop.

Thorne was in the doorway doing what humans did after a Pelagos Minor, which was to stand somewhere quiet and be unable to use it. He had signed the word *success* with a hand that was not steady and had noticed it was not steady and had signed anyway, and Syra had thought better of him for the whole sequence and said nothing, because it was his and not a measurement. Now he had said a folk word at a post, and the practitioner had gone still.

Syra knew stillness. It was the first thing anyone noticed about her people and the last thing anyone understood, and she had spent most of a hundred and fifty years watching humans read it as composure, which it was not, or as coldness, which it was not, or as a surface with nothing under it, which was the human report about the human. So she was exact about what she was watching now. The practitioner had not frozen. Freezing was a startle that had failed to resolve into movement. This was the other thing: a body that had stopped shifting because shifting was noise, so that whatever it was reading could be read clean.

The lamp on the table guttered, the way lamps did here when the stones changed temperature, and Thorne's shoulders came up a finger's width before he knew they had, and the practitioner's did not. She turned her head toward the lamp at the speed of a decision rather than a reflex. Then she turned it back.

Syra had a reading. She was clear with herself about the word. A reading was what the body returned; a finding was a measurement with error bars that would stand in an inquiry, and there was no instrument in the Survey Corps or out of it that measured what she was reading, and she was the last person in the room who would offer one as if there were. She filed it where she filed sensations: as hers, unsupported, and not for the record.

There was a second reason she would not say it, and it was not professional. *What are you, really* was the one question her people declined, every time, in every register it was asked in — not out of secrecy, since a Krenyi asked said plainly that there was an answer and that they would not give it, but because the question read a person backward from a kind and stopped attending to who was in front of you. She had declined it for a century and a half. She was not going to put it to anyone else. Whatever this woman was, she was the person who had kept these books in this order and had taken an anchor out of a grove in one attempt, and that was what was present, and Syra attended to what was present. It was the whole of her courtesy.

The practitioner came to the table and sat down across from her, and said, without preamble, which Syra also approved of: "I had something wrong in my record. I want to correct it while I know what the correction is."

"Say it."

"The phrase I use for the substrate. *The layer underneath.* I wrote that I made it here, alone, out of nothing. I didn't. I was taught it as a girl. I gave it to him" — a tilt of the head at the doorway, no more — "twenty years ago, and came here and built it again and thought it was mine."

Syra considered this on its merits, which was the only way she considered anything.

"Does it still do work?"

"Yes."

"Then it's yours now. A term belongs to whoever is measuring with it. Where it was made is a fact about the past." She turned a page. "Write both. The correction and that it costs you nothing."

The practitioner looked at her for a moment — not long, and not in the way humans looked at Syra when they had just decided she was unkind. Then she said, "It does cost me nothing," in the tone of someone confirming a reading against a second instrument, and took her second book from the shelf and wrote.

A human told that a thing they had believed their own had in fact been given to them generally did one of two things, in Syra's experience, and both of them took a while. This woman had done neither. She had taken the correction, checked it, filed it, and gone on, with nothing in her hands or her shoulders to show that anything had been set down.

Syra had watched that done before, at tables where everyone present answered to *Nai*. She did not say so. It was a reading, and she did not file readings, and the woman across the table had not asked.

She came to the door when Syra had the fourth book open and the light was gone, and stood beside him looking at the post, and said: "I was on the step."

He did not say anything. He had been waiting a month for this sentence and had not once imagined it would arrive as a statement of fact about geography.

"Your father was in the house about the field line. You came down the garden with your hands in your pockets and said the kitchen's word for the light by the gate. I didn't like it." A pause of the ordinary length. "I still don't."

"You remember."

"No." She said it carefully, the way she said anything she did not want misheard. "I didn't keep it. There was nothing in it to keep — a boy, a word, a light that had been wrong for years. You said the word again just now and it came back whole. That isn't remembering. Remembering is when you have been carrying something. I hadn't."

He thought about twenty years of carrying it. Six stations. A phrase he had used on purpose in the mud at their first meeting, to see what her face would do, and what her face had done was be impressed, every time, and he had received it every time as a thing he had failed to earn.

He had tried one other reading, in the third week, late, and he would put it in no report either. That the causeway had done it: that a Type II interface sitting under an outpost for three years could carry a person across the gap he had been failing to do the arithmetic on, so that the woman on the step and the woman in the mud were twenty years apart for him and not for her. It had lasted the length of time it took to open the manual. Oscillatory interfaces were non-displacing; the Corps had measured it on more membranes than he had postings, and the table said *detectable, non-displacing* in the same flat type it said everything. And her own account put her at Dún Rí in 2822, warden-heir, sixteen years after the step and four before the mud, which left nothing for a boundary to have skipped. The causeway had done nothing to her. It had been wrong about the right thing, that reading: the gap was real, and it was not in the time.

"I used it to test you," he said. "The phrase. More than once."

"I know. Aldera logged it." The smallest movement at the corner of her mouth. "She logs everything. She doesn't tell me unless I ask. I asked."

"And you were —"

"Impressed. Yes. Because I thought I'd made it and you'd arrived at it independently, which would have been remarkable." She looked at the post. "It wasn't remarkable. It was the same garden."

The reeds moved. The wind was ordinary here now, and he still noticed every time it was.

"I kept it for twenty years," he said, not as a complaint. It was simply the figure.

"I know. You shouldn't have had to." She said it in the plain voice, and he understood that this was what she had for an apology, and that it was a better one than most. "It was the sage's, not mine. I only passed it on. I've told the analyst and written it down. It's in the record now, where it should have been."

He found that he had nothing to add to that, which did not happen to him often, and that the thing he had been carrying since the third week was no lighter and no heavier, only shared, which was not what he had expected and was better.

::: pov elvira

She went back in when he had gone along the stones to wait for the analyst, and stood for a moment by the water vessels, and looked at the four notes beside them saying where each had stood and for how long.

The boy had been real, then. She had not invented the phrase and she had not invented him; the Corps had simply been a long way round to the same step. That was a clean result. She liked clean results and distrusted them in equal measure, and tonight the distrust had somewhere specific to go.

Because there was a fourth thing, and she had not taken it apart with the others, and she knew why.

The sage had given her the phrase. The sage had been careful about lists — which shapes the world kept and which it did not, which words were for people who had decided not to look. He had been the one person at Dún Rí she had trusted with the stones and the glass, on the night the cat left, because he did not dress comfort up as ceremony. And he had been of the house, and the house had lied, for family reasons, in the shape she could recognise across a room and had recognised nowhere on the causeway in three years, not in Thorne, not in the analyst, not in the cat.

She had never known what the lies were about. She had known the shape of them from the inside the way one knows a draught, and had been given a phrase for looking under things, and had been sent to a marsh — had sent herself, she corrected; nobody had sent her — with the phrase and nothing else.

Whether any of it had been about her was a question she had lived inside for long enough to have stopped expecting an answer to. She noted that it had not moved tonight. She noted that she had checked.

She opened the second book, under the date, in the column for observations she had not intended to make, below the correction she had already written, and added one line.

*The word by the gate was the kitchen's. The phrase was the sage's. I rebuilt it; I did not make it. What I was taught to look under, and by whom, and why, is not in this book.*

Then she went to see what the analyst had found in the fourth volume, because the forty seconds was still forty seconds, and that, at least, was a thing with an instrument.
