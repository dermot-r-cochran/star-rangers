He brought the clock himself, because a thing the station had decided to lend was a thing somebody from the station should carry, and because the analyst's finding had been plain enough that he had not wanted it to sit in a file for a week before anyone acted on it. *The outpost has never kept a clock good enough to notice.* Syra had written it as a finding. He had read it as an instruction.

It was a field chronometer in a sealed case, the kind the Corps issued to a party that would be away from a station for a season, set that morning against Threshold's two clocks and carried along the causeway at a walk, with the case under his coat so the cold would not get at it before the wall did. The marsh in early winter was the colour of the sky, and the sky was the colour of the chalk, and the stones were dry and dark and did not look like anything.

Elvira had the niche cut before he reached the door. She had chosen the north wall, above the water vessels and their four notes, at a height she could read without stooping, and she had been at the chalk with a short blade since first light, by the look of the floor. The chalk here was not clean. It came out in soft white slabs with flint in it, and the flint came out when it chose to, in flakes with an edge on them a surgeon would have been glad of.

"It wants a flat seat," she said. "The bracket's wider than the case."

"The bracket's the station's idea of a wall."

"The station has never seen this wall." She was working one-handed, the other flat on the chalk beside the cut to feel it, and she did not look round. "Hand me the case. I'll offer it up."

He unwrapped it and gave it to her, and she set it in the niche, and it rocked, and she took it out again and went back to the chalk with the blade. He watched the flint come away. He had been going to say something about gloves — the Corps issued them with the chronometer, in the same case, for exactly this — and he did not, because she had cut a niche in chalk every winter for three years and he had cut none, and a man who had spent the autumn finding out how much he had not told her was not going to begin the winter by telling her how to hold a knife.

The flint went in under the heel of her hand. He saw it go, and saw her see it, and saw that nothing else about her moved: the hand came away from the wall, the blade went down on the shelf, point inward, and she looked at the cut the way she looked at a circle that had failed, for what it told her and nothing else. It was not deep. It bled the way a cut on the heel of a hand bleeds, generously and at once, and she had none of the cloth the Corps also issued and he had not brought, so she pressed the hand to her coat and said, "Take the case," and held it out with the other, and the case was wet where she had been holding it before the blade slipped.

He took it. He turned it to the window to see whether the blood had reached the seal, and it had not, and he set the case on the shelf and wiped it with his sleeve, and by then the back of his right hand had begun to sting.

He knew it before he had a name for it, which was the wrong way round for him and the right way round for what it was. The sting first. Then the place: the fourth of his six stations, a hatch rim with a burr on it nobody had filed, a Section Lead with her forearm opened to the elbow, and himself holding the dressing in place while the medic gloved, and not gloved himself, and the inside of his wrist coming up in a weal while the medic worked, and the Section Lead watching it with the interest she gave to any reading and saying, in the voice her people used for anything true, *I told you in your first week. It fades.* It had faded. It had taken two days, and he had been told the mechanism three times in those two days by three people, and once, before, by her, in his first week, as her people did, because withholding it would have cost someone something.

He looked at his hand. The skin where the case had lain against it was reddening along the line of the blood, and under the red a weal was coming up, as a nettle raises one, and it stung as a nettle stings, and he had felt it before, and the clinical file called it the plainest field sign there was, where no instrument was to hand.

He did not look at her. He looked at the hand, because the hand was what he had, and twelve years had taught him that what you had was what you described, and that the moment you began describing something else instead was the moment the report stopped being yours.

Twenty years. A face that had not moved a day inside them. A woman who had taken an anchor out of a grove in one attempt and written *that should have been harder* and meant it. The analyst gone still at the table in the lamplight while this woman turned her head toward a guttering lamp at the speed of a decision, and Syra, who filed nothing she could not support, filing nothing. He had declined to assemble it. He had told himself for a season that a pattern with a sample size of one was a thing you held, and he had been right, and the sample size had just changed on the back of his hand.

"Elvira."

"It's nothing." She had the hand away from her coat and was looking at it. "It's closed."

"Mine isn't."

He held it out, back uppermost, where the light was. She looked at it. Then she looked at her own, at the heel of it, where the cut had been, and where there was now a line shut and pink, the way a cut looks on its second day, in a hand that had been bleeding freely a minute before.

He said it plainly, because he had learned once, the expensive way, what it cost to leave the important sentence unsaid on the grounds that there would be a better occasion, and because there was not going to be one.

"I've had this before. Once. On a posting, from a cut I helped dress." He kept his voice where it was. "I was told what it was before it happened, by the person whose blood it was, because her people tell you early. The clinical file keeps it as a field sign. Where there's no instrument to hand." A pause of the ordinary length, which he made himself leave. "Krenyi blood, on a human's skin."

He stopped there. He had described what was on his hand and what the file called it. He was not going to describe her. He had spent twelve years teaching junior surveyors that the thing you were looking at and the thing you concluded were two entries, and that the second one was not yours to write on somebody else's page.

::: pov elvira

She had the hand up in the window light, his, and she read it the way she read anything, from the edge in.

The mark first. It lay where the case had lain, in the shape of the wet on the case, and nowhere else: the skin red along that line and raised a little, as if something had brushed it. She had seen a nettle do it. She had not seen a nettle do it in the shape of her own hand.

Her own hand second. She turned it over and looked at the heel of it. The cut was closed. It had closed by the time she had found nothing to press on it and pressed her coat there instead, which was how long cuts took to close on her and always had, and she had been told as a girl that she healed well and had filed it, young, under things a body did, some bodies better than others. She had never once turned it round. A thing that had always been so was a thing there was no reason to measure, and she had grown up in a house that was careful about which things got measured.

She did not take the third thing apart yet. She set it to one side where she could see it, because it was going to be the one that was hers, and she had learned at this table a week ago that the one that was hers went last.

The file third, then. She had read the Corps' general references on the analyst's people in the third week of the autumn, for the analyst's sake, the way she read the manual on any instrument she was going to have in the room. She remembered the sentence on fear. *They do not experience the deep-fear response that shuts down clear thinking.* She remembered reading it and thinking it a sensible way to put a thing she had never found a sensible way to put, and going on to the next sentence, which had been about fury. She had not turned that round either. It had not occurred to her to. She had been reading about the analyst.

The survey man was standing where she had left him, with his hand out and the sentence finished, and he had not added anything to it, and she noted that, because she had seen men add things to a sentence like that until there was nothing in the room but what they thought. He had said what was on his hand and what the file called it. He had not said what she was. She found that she had revised her model of him upward again, in the smallest increment she had, and that the model was now about as high as it went, and that she had nowhere left to file the revision but with the rest.

"The file," she said. "What does it want. For a finding."

"A sample. A medic. Not a hand."

"Then it'll have one. I'd rather it was a finding than a sign." She let go of his hand. "Tell the station. Tell the analyst first; she'll want it from the record and not from you. If there's a form, I'll fill it. If there's a receipt, I'll insist on one."

Something went across his face that she filed as the first thing he had found funny since Pelagos Minor, and he did not say what it was, and she let him keep it.

Now the third thing.

She had been nine. There had been a wall at Dún Rí, the terrace wall where the roses were, and a fall off it, and a knee opened on the edge of the step, and the household sage had dressed it himself because the footman had been sent for and the sage had been nearer. He had put on gloves to do it. Thin ones, the kind he wore for the glass. She had asked why, because she asked why about everything then, and he had said the salve stained, and had dressed the knee, and the knee had been closed by supper, and she had been told she healed well.

She did not know whether the salve stained. She had never seen it on anything.

She stood in the outpost with the chalk dust on the floor and the clock on the shelf and took that one apart as far as it would go, which was not far. The sage had put on gloves. He had given a reason. The reason might have been true. He had been of the house, and the house had lied, for family reasons, in a shape she had known from the inside the way one knows a draught, and she had never known what the lies were about, and she had checked a week ago at this table whether any of it had been about her and had noted that it had not moved.

It had moved now. She was exact about how far. As far as a pair of gloves and a reason she had not tested, and no further; the sage was not in this room and the question was, and she had no instrument for what a careful man had known then, or had not, and had said or not said for reasons that were the house's. She noted that it had moved. She noted that she had checked. She noted, because it was true and because she did not like leaving a true thing out of the record on the grounds that it was hers, that she had wanted it to move, and had wanted it for some time, and that the wanting had been there under the three years and under the step, and that the survey man had not put it there, and had only, this morning, with his hand out, declined to look away from it.

She would not hide it. She found, checking, that she had not considered hiding it, which was not a decision; she had simply gone straight to the form and the receipt, and had noticed afterwards that there had been another road and that she had not seen it. She filed that too. It was a fact about her, and she was collecting those this morning.

"Give me the case," she said. "The seat's flat now. I want the station's time on that shelf before I write anything down, so I know what time I wrote it."

::: pov elvira

She wrote it that evening, under the date and under the time, which she took from the shelf, to the second, and which was the first time a line in this book had had a second on it.

She did not write *Krenyi*. The word was the file's and the finding was the station's and she had told the survey man she would rather have the finding; until she had it, what she had was his hand, and she wrote his hand.

*Cut the heel of my hand on a flint, 09:14 station time, seating the Corps' clock. Blood on the case. The survey man took the case bare-handed and the blood raised a mark on him along its own line, red, a little proud, stinging; he says he has had it before, once, from a cut he helped dress, and was told what it was by the one whose blood it was. The file has a name for it. My cut was closed before I found a cloth. It always has been. I was told as a girl that I healed well. The sage dressed my knee in gloves when I was nine and said the salve stained; I did not test it. The station will have a sample when it asks. I have not asked what I am. I have asked what the file wants.*

She read it back. It was the longest entry in the book and it was all measurement, and she let it stand.

Then she turned the page, because there was a second thing, and it went in the column for observations she had not intended to make.

*Three years of standing in this and not being afraid. I filed it as a flaw, and then as a practice. What I was taught to look under, and by whom, and why, is still not in this book. What was under me is.*

She closed the book. On the shelf the clock kept the station's time, to the second, in a room that had gone three years without one, and she looked at it for a while, and then she went out to the stones to reset the circle, which would fail, correctly, as it had the night before, with one more thing in the room that could be measured.
