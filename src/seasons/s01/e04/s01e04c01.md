The Station Manager's office had a window.

It was on the landward side, where a window on this station showed the service road and the marsh and, at the end of the spit, the white stub of the light. Galahad had been at Threshold a season and had not been in the room. He had known the desk by what came out of it, which was two responses in eleven years, and he had read both on his first night and had not needed to read them twice.

Cors Delveyne kept nothing on the desk he was not using. Today that was one sheet, face up, and Galahad could read its heading from the door: the advisory of the late autumn, with the survey's certification line at the foot of it and no countersignature beside.

"Sit down, Thorne. It's a review. It isn't the other thing."

He sat.

"You copied this office in the same transmission," Delveyne said. "I'll put that on the table first, because it is the part I have no quarrel with. I have had papers go round this desk. I hear of them from Traffic Control a week on, and by then I have a station with two voices and a controller who has learned which one to ring. You went over. I read it at the hour they did."

"That was the intention."

"I know it was. It is why you are sitting down." He turned the sheet a few degrees, squaring it to nothing. "Now the part I do have a quarrel with. Traffic Control has one line for where a paper comes from, and on that line a survey's advisory and a station's are the same word. So for twelve days Threshold told every hull in the sector that its margins were not to be trusted, on a finding I had not read, resting on a model your own unit withdrew the next morning."

"Withdrew in part. The withdrawal went the way the advisory went. Same hour, your office copied."

"It did."

"A correction that takes a slower road than the thing it corrects is not a correction."

Delveyne looked at him for a moment and let that stand, which Galahad took for agreement and then, more carefully, for a man declining to argue with a sentence he would have written himself.

"The ships came through," Delveyne said.

"Yes."

"Nothing happened."

"No."

"So I am asked to review an advisory under which nothing happened, and I cannot tell you whether it was needed, and neither can you."

"No," Galahad said. "That is what a margin is."

Traffic Control had kept the margins for those hulls and for ten days after, and had then gone back to its standing window on its own authority, the unknown having gone on being unknown without incident. That was Traffic Control's to decide and it had decided it. What was on the desk was narrower. It was a signature, and whose desk it had not crossed.

Twelve years in the Corps. He had watched people take the short route and had watched what it cost them, and the cost had always been paid later, and always by the person who signed. He had known that at the operations table with the traces still up. This was later.

"I had two days," he said. "A fourth paper from the survey office would have had the answer the first three had, in the time the first three took."

"You don't know what it would have had. You did not send it."

It was said without heat, and it was true. Galahad turned it over once, the way he turned over a figure that had been offered to him as a depth, and found the floor of it where Delveyne had put it. He had read two responses and built a desk out of them. He had been right about the two days. About the desk he had a sample of two and a season in which he had not once walked down the corridor to enlarge it.

"No," he said. "I didn't."

"Then we have each said one thing the other can't answer, and we can get on." Delveyne put a finger on the certification line. "The route is one question. This is the other, and I'd sooner ask it of the person it concerns than of her team lead. Whose certification did the survey stand on?"

"Mine is the signature."

"I can read whose signature it is. I asked whose certification."

::: pov syra

This was the room.

She had known on her first morning, on the dock, that a thing which has been classified wrongly needs somebody to say so in the right room to the right person, and that a dock was not the room. She had not known the room would have a window, or that it would take a season to be brought to it.

"The certification is mine," Syra said. "It was issued by a body that is not the Survey Corps. Your intake log flagged it for review on the day I arrived. The flag is the station's. It has not been actioned."

"No," the manager said. "It has not."

"I did not chase it. A flag about me, chased by me, is a different document. That was correct and it was also slow, and I would like both recorded."

He wrote something. She waited until he had finished, because he was a person who finished.

"Then I have an advisory to Traffic Control resting on a reading by an analyst this station has not yet recognised," he said. "I am not saying the reading is wrong. I am saying I cannot be the one to say it is right. I never could. That is the whole of my eleven years, analyst, and I would like you to have it from me and not from the file."

"Say it."

"The protocol gives a station two chronometers and tells it to log a discrepancy as an instrument anomaly and investigate. It does not say what to do when the anomaly comes back. It says a survey team may not make an Etheric-layer assessment without a certified analyst, and in thirteen years the Corps has not posted one to my establishment. So I could call it an instrument matter, which I was entitled to call it. Or I could call it a boundary matter, which nobody here was entitled to call anything." He did not raise his voice; she doubted he had one to raise. "I do not sign what I cannot stand behind. I signed the smaller word."

Syra considered it on its merits and found it true rather than exculpatory. She had met the distinction at a lighthouse table in the autumn and had not expected to need it twice on one shore.

"The word was wrong," she said. "It was wrong on the day you wrote it. A technician told you so in the same week, and a watch officer told you so on the same day."

"They did."

"You could have forwarded it."

"On what ground? The protocol says I forward what requires external review. A technician's unease is not *requires*. I wanted a line. Something that said when." He opened the drawer at his right hand and took out a single leaf, old, in a sleeve. "The watch officer gave me one. She was the only person on this station who ever did."

"*Escalate to external survey review if any widening beyond forty seconds is observed*," Syra said.

He stopped with the leaf half out of its sleeve.

"The Archive holds it," she said. "It was passed this year. I read it there."

"Then you know my test. I took her number and made it mine. Every recalibration for eleven years I have read the maintenance entry myself, and every time the units have come back to forty. Not forty-one. I would have sent it out on forty-one. I'd have been glad to."

She believed him. That was a reading, and she filed it as one. What she could support was narrower, and she said that instead.

"The maintenance log records that the units were returned to agreement."

"Yes."

"From what?"

He did not answer at once.

"It records the corrections," Syra said. "It does not record the readings they corrected. The watch log is kept in station time, and the right-hand reading is outside it by standing practice. Between one recalibration and the next there is no line on this station that says what the difference was. You set a sound test and kept no instrument that could fail it." She let the sentence finish. "I am naming a gap in a record. I am not saying whose."

"I know whose," the manager said.

**LOG ENTRY — Rook-7**
**Location: Threshold Station, Station Manager's office**
**Context: Administrative review, advisory of the late autumn. Called in at minute 31 to produce a source.**

Source produced: general operations watch log, Dock Seven. Full series, on loan through the supervising Principal as before. One hundred and three consecutive watches, two readings on each time line, left-hand first. The series ends with its keeper's posting off station in the late autumn. The cadet who followed was told on the first watch that the log is kept in station time, and writes one reading. *The form was explained. That was the correct fix. It also closed the only line of this kind the station has had.*

Discrepancy across the series, in seconds: range 38 to 42. Watches reading 41 or above: twenty-nine. The longer run gives no period. It gives the range again.

Objection from Station Manager Delveyne, recorded because it is a good one: a watch-stander reads two wall faces in turn, and turning the head takes about a second. Answer: the survey's own logging samples both faces on one trigger. It began forty watches after the cadet's and is the better series; hers is the longer. Same range, to the second. *Two records, different methods, one result. I have learned this season what one of those is worth alone.*

Station Manager Delveyne then read the cadet's series in full. He did not skip to the range. Duration: 17 minutes. Nobody spoke. Team Lead Thorne looked at the window. Analyst Syra did not look at anything, which is how she waits.

Questions put to me afterward, with answers:

- Who kept it. *A cadet of the general operations watch. Not on the survey roster.*
- Whether she was asked to. *No. The form's line said Time. It did not say which.*
- Whether she has been told it was read. *No. Nothing in the record would have improved by it.*

His instruction: "Leave it so." Logged.

Standing item, carried since my first day: the administration's responses are either uninformed or a deliberate suppression of data; a third possibility, that the responses are correct, retained at a low weight. Restated on the seventh day as: incompetence or a decision.

Finding: a decision. Taken in 2815 by the person now across the desk, on a criterion supplied by a night-watch officer, and held for eleven years with a consistency I would log in a unit as correct operation. The criterion was sound. The station kept no record able to report that it had been met. This is neither item on my list. *Weights corrected. I note that I held a two-item list about the author of five documents for a season and did not ask the author. The asking took four minutes.*

Time from end of reading to decision: 2 minutes 10 seconds. He wrote it by hand. Text, as written:

*ADMINISTRATIVE RESPONSE. Station Manager Cors Delveyne. 2826 UCSD, Mid-Winter. Incident Report 001 reopened. The classification "calibration issue" is withdrawn. It was mine. Carried from today as an instrument anomaly, unresolved. Widening beyond forty seconds is observed, in the general operations watch log and in the survey's own series. Forwarded to the regional survey office for external review under Section 3, on my determination. Attached: the boundary analyst's reading, Type II interface, as the analyst's. Her certification stands flagged in this station's intake log; the flag was not actioned, and is actioned with this paper. On the advisory of the late autumn: sustained on content. Route not sustained, and noted on the survey lead's record.*

Length: ten sentences. The first response, 2815, was three. I have both on the desk. *The hand is the same. It is the same hand in the same way that the right-hand chronometer is the same instrument: it has not changed, and I had not read it against anything.*

Outpost chronometer, Marsh Causeway, for completeness: seated early winter, running, on station time. Series too short to say anything. The response does not cite it, and I did not offer it.

**END LOG ENTRY**

::: pov galahad

There was a line at the foot of the response for his initials, under the sentence about the route.

He read the whole paper from the top before he went near it. *Sustained on content. Route not sustained.* It was fair. It was also going to ride in his file to the eighth station and the ninth, and be read there by somebody on a first night, in order, who would build a team lead out of it. He found he could not object to that on any ground he had not just been taught the weakness of.

He initialled it.

Then he took out his handheld and found the note. Four lines. He had drafted it on his first night with the five documents on the desk in his quarters, and had carried it unfiled for a season, because he had wanted the connection made in a hand the administration could not answer with a paragraph.

"There is one more thing for the packet," he said. "The first report is 2815. The last boundary survey of this shore is 2818. Nothing in the record joins them. I have a note that says so, and says nothing else, because I don't know anything else."

Delveyne held out his hand for it and read the four lines.

"The survey asked the station for its open anomalies," he said. "There is a form. I had none open. I had closed the only one three years before." He gave the handheld back. "I answered the question that was on their form. It was a true answer."

It was not a paragraph. It was two sentences, and they had been available on any afternoon of the autumn to anyone who had come down the corridor and asked.

Galahad added a fifth line to the note, under his own four and marked as the manager's, and filed it to the packet. It was the first paper on the station with both their hands in it.

"The regional office will want the lighthouse register," Delveyne said. "The older baseline. Where is it?"

"Two volumes in the survey office, under receipt. The third never left the tower. It is still being written in."

"Whose receipt?"

"The attendant's. The analyst told her to insist on one."

Something moved in Delveyne's face that was not quite amusement. "Then send up the transcription and take her books home. I'll not have the Corps forward another body's property on my determination. And Thorne." He had the old leaf in its sleeve again and was putting it back in the drawer. "For as long as I have had this desk I have sent down that spit for tide tables and nothing else. Tell her what it was we borrowed."

On the way out Galahad went by Dock Seven, which was not on the way. The two chronometers were where they had been. He stood under them at seven minutes past four by the one above the seal, and the one above the hatch made it forty seconds later, and both had been calibrated, and both were right.

The reports had been careful. The responses had been brief. He had taken that, on his first night, for a finding.
