::: pov nuala-beirne

There were two of them on the spit, and one of them had walked it before.

She could tell that from the gallery, a long way off. The tall one came down the middle of the shingle without looking at the water on either side, which nobody did until they had been out and back at least once and found the water was not interested. The other was the robot. It carried a case in front of it in both arms, level, the way you carry a thing that is somebody else's.

The wind was in the east today and dry, coming off the land for once, and it had laid a frost on the landward steps that had not lifted by noon. The sea was flat and the colour of a slate. Up the shore the station's docks stood on it as they always did. She had looked at them more this winter than in all the winters before it, and had caught herself at it, and had gone on.

She went down and had the door open before they reached it.

"You've an hour and forty of the falling," she said. "Wipe your feet. The both of you."

The survey man wiped his feet. The robot considered the mat, and then its feet, and then did it, thoroughly, one and then the other.

The third volume was open on the table at the page, because the signal was due inside the hour and that was where the book went on a signal day. It had not been off this table or the shelf up the stair since the autumn. The analyst had settled that at this table in about one sentence: a book that was still being written in was not a book you lent, and the robot could read it where it lay. So the robot had stood over it for what was left of the tide with the gale sounding in the glass, turning each leaf by its top corner, and the first two volumes had gone up the spit in the survey man's coat, and she had been left with a sheet of the Corps' paper.

She took the sheet out of the tin now. The tin was where the Board's forms lived.

It was the best receipt she had ever been given, and the Board had once sent her a clock. *Received on loan from the attendant of the Sceir Light, being the property of the Lights Board: the register of the light, volumes the first and second. Boards sound. Spine of the first cracked at the head and not by us. Pasted slips, sixty-one, each counted and none lifted. Two leaves loose at the back of the second, in this order.* And the order. And under it the survey man's name in his own hand, and under that a line that was not his: *Written by Nai Syra, who has read them.*

"On the table," she said. "Beside the other."

The robot set the case down and opened it and stood back.

She did not hurry. She had the cracked spine under her thumb first, and it was cracked where it had been cracked. She went through the slips with a fingernail, every one, and none had a corner up that had not had one up before. The two loose leaves were at the back of the second in the order the paper gave. Somebody had put a clean sheet either side of them so they would not rub.

"That's not on the receipt," she said.

"No," the survey man said. "That was the analyst. She said it was not a change to the book, it was a change to the case."

"She would."

She wrote across the foot of the receipt, *Returned, two volumes, as described*, and the date, and *N.B.*, and blotted it.

::: pov galahad

He had expected her to paste it in.

The register had slips pasted through the whole middle of the second volume, where some supplier of printed forms had come and gone; it was a book that had taken paper in before. And a maintenance record said what keeping a thing correct had cost. He had thought, walking out, that she would want the season of the book's absence to be in the book.

She put the receipt back in the tin and shut the lid.

"The book's for the clock," Nuala Beirne said, without his having asked. "The receipt's for the book. That goes with the forms."

"Yes."

"Now. You've brought them home and you've been careful and I'm obliged. What was it for?"

He had the manager's sentence in his head, and he had a kilometre of shingle behind him that was a good deal longer than a station corridor. He noticed that he had minded it less.

"I'll say what the book supports," he said. "The shore has disagreed with the standard for as long as anyone on it has written the difference down. Always the one way. By more, these last years, than it used to. That's all. It went to the regional office on Tuesday in a packet, and that's all the packet says of it."

"And why does it?"

"We don't know."

"Is that in your packet?"

"It's the first line."

She unfolded her arms. He remembered that from the autumn, and what had earned it then.

"Then I'll believe the rest of it," she said.

He told her the rest. There were two clocks on a dock wall up the shore, the station's own and a repeater of the standard, and forty seconds between them. The station had corrected that away for eleven years and had it back inside two days every time. The manager who had closed the first report on it had reopened it this week in his own hand. He told her about the transcription. He told her that the books themselves had gone nowhere but a locked press in the survey office, and that the manager would not send another body's property up the line on his own say.

"Him that sends down for the tide tables."

"He asked me to tell you what it was we'd borrowed."

"He could have walked down."

"He could," Galahad said. "I've lately been told the same about a shorter walk."

She looked at him, and let him keep it.

"Your forty seconds," she said. "And my nine a season. Is that the one thing?"

It was the question he would have asked, and he had spent a season learning what it cost to answer that kind of question a day early.

"It's the one shore," he said. "Whether it's the one thing, nobody can say yet. I could give you a guess. My unit gave the station a very good one in the autumn, with a figure on it, and withdrew it in the same file three days later. I'd rather owe you the answer."

"Owe it, so."

He gave her the letter. It was the manager's copy, to the Lights Board, and it said in the fewest words Delveyne had been able to find that a transcription of the Board's register at the Sceir Light had been made by the Survey Corps with the attendant's leave and forwarded for review, that the register had been returned entire, and that nothing was asked of the Board.

She read it standing, and all the way down.

"It'll go in the file under this light's number," she said. "With the requisition. And it'll be read by them that read the requisition, and they'll read it as a clock asking for attention."

"It says nothing is asked."

"It says it in terms the Board will read as a request for a movement." She folded the letter along its own fold. "I'll have a third one by the spring. I'll be glad of it, and it'll make no odds."

The signal came while she still had the letter in her hand. She put it down and went to the clock, and he stood where he was and did not go nearer. She read the one against the other and made the correction, and came back to the table and wrote the line. *Date. Signal received. Clock reads. Correction applied, in seconds. Initials.* Five things. He read the fourth upside down, from habit, and it was a three.

On the facing page, some way up, in a hand that was not hers and ink that had gone brown, there was a note in the margin. He read that upside down as well. *No action.*

"You're wondering will I write beside him," she said.

"I was."

"He'd the column and thirty years of looking at it. He wasn't wrong in his figures. If I write beside him I'm arguing with a dead man in the Board's book." She capped the pen. "You've a paper up there for arguing in. This is for the clock."

"Somebody may come to read the third volume. From the regional office. I've written that it's read here, at this table, and doesn't leave."

"They'll have an hour and fifty of the falling, and there's the one bed."

"I'll put that in as well."

He had one more thing and had gone back and forth on the spit about whether it was his to ask. He asked it, because the other choice was to carry it.

"It's being read now. The book. Somebody will look at every line you write from here on. Does that change how you keep it?"

She took longer over that than she had over the receipt.

"It had better not," she said. "The same five. If I start keeping it for you, it's a different book, and you'd have no use for it."

::: pov nuala-beirne

She carried them up one at a time.

The stair was the stair, and three volumes of the Board's register were more than she took up it in one arm any longer. She put the first on the shelf, and the second against it, and came down for the third, its last line dry by then, and put that against the second. The shelf had been two-thirds bare since the autumn. She had not thought she minded.

It was where the book lived. Anybody who climbed the stair could have reached it down, and for a long time nobody had, and now somebody had, and had brought it back with a clean sheet either side of its loose leaves. There would be others. They would have an hour and fifty of the falling, and they would wipe their feet.

She went on up to the gallery.

The cold had come down hard with the dark, and the east wind had dropped to nothing. The two of them were most of the way in along the spit, the tall one in the middle of the shingle and the other behind, and beyond them the station had its three working lights on the water. Somebody up there had sat at a desk this week and taken back a word he had written eleven years ago. She did not know him, and bore him no ill will for the tide tables. The lamp went round over all of it, the spit and the two walking and the docks and the marsh, the way it went round over everything.

Nothing on the water. There never was.

She counted it anyway. Two and dark, two and dark. Fifteen seconds, group to group, by a clock that was right to the second and would be for a while.

Nobody was out there. It was lit.
