::: pov iona

The outpost's medical kit had been issued for a human.

Iona had the issue list on her handheld and had read it at her desk and again at the head of the causeway, standing on the first stone with the new kit under one arm. Sub-station pattern, sealed. Dressings, a pressure cuff, splints, a thermal wrap. Six sheets of graft film, which took on a wound and became part of whoever was wearing it. A card in the lid with the station's casualty channel on it. Nothing on the list was wrong. It had all been chosen for somebody else.

She had never walked the causeway. Her work was at the other end of the station, in a ring where people crossed twenty metres in no time at all and paid for it afterwards in ways she wrote down. It took her the better part of half an hour to cross a little over a kilometre of wet stone at a walk, and she noted, with the part of her that noted such things, that she arrived with nothing to screen for.

The marsh in late winter had water standing in it to the horizon. The reeds were the colour of straw and lay all one way. Ahead of her the analyst walked in the middle of the stones and did not look at the water, and had not spoken since the station, which Iona had stopped reading as anything a long time ago.

The paper was in her coat.

It could have gone out on the channel. A finding was a short document, and the channel would have carried it to the outpost in the time it took to press the key, and it would have arrived there as an item in a queue between a tide warning and a stores notice. She had thought about that for less time than it deserved, and had gone to find her boots. A finding about a person was handed to the person. It was not in the clinical file as a rule. It was in the file under *practice*, which was where the file put the rules it had not been able to make anyone write down.

She had drawn the sample herself in the early winter. Elvira had walked in to the clinic with a request in her own hand, three lines, and had sat on the edge of the couch and read the consent sheet to the end, which almost nobody did, and then asked what became of the vial. Iona had told her. Elvira had asked for that in writing as well. So the clinic had written, for the first time in Iona's knowledge of it, a receipt for blood.

Then seven weeks. The file did not let one clinician find a person's kind. It wanted the panel read twice, by two readers who had not seen each other's sheet, and this region's second reader sat at a desk the station sent to by the supply run. Iona had read her own panel the same afternoon she drew it. She had known for seven weeks what the second sheet would say. She had also spent a career writing down what happened to people when somebody acted on the first reading alone, and she had put her sheet in a drawer and told no one, the analyst included.

The second sheet had come in that morning. It agreed.

The door of the outpost was open before they reached it. It was low and set into the chalk, with a rail beside it, and on the rail a tabby cat in a grey collar that watched the kit under Iona's arm and not her face.

"Dr. Vale," Elvira said. "Analyst. You'll want to wipe your boots, the chalk takes everything. Is it back?"

"It's back."

"Then come in and put it on the table. I'd like to read it sitting down. I've the receipt here, and I'll want to know about the vial."

::::: scene 2

::: pov elvira

It was a single leaf.

She had expected more of it, and noticed that she had, and set the expectation to one side with the others. The Corps' form had a heading, her name as she had given it, the date the sample was drawn and by whom. Then two boxes side by side, *First reading* and *Second reading*, each with a hand in it she did not know and a desk. Then one line.

*Finding: Krenyi. Readings agree.*

Under that, the method, in four words she would look up. And at the foot: *Sample destroyed after second reading*, and a third hand, and the date.

She read it from the edge in. She checked the foot against the receipt in her other hand, and the receipt was answered. Then she turned the leaf round on the table so that it faced the analyst.

"I asked that you be here," she said. "I said in the early winter you'd want it from the record. This is the record. Read it and tell me whether it's supported."

The analyst read it. It did not take her long, and she did it without touching the paper.

"It is supported. Two readers, blind to each other. The method is the file's own." She turned it back. "I would have preferred a third. Two is what the file asks for."

"And what did you have before it?"

"A reading. From the autumn, at this table. I did not file it and I did not say it."

"No. You wouldn't have." Elvira found that she had known that, and for about how long. It had not weighed anything. "Thank you for not."

She looked at the one line for a while.

The word was the same word that had been in the general reference in the third week of the autumn, when she had read about fear and then about fury and gone on. It had been a word about the analyst then. It had sat in her own book since the early winter as a gap she had written round on purpose, *the mark and not the word*, because a hand was a sign and she had wanted a finding. Now she had it. It was one line long, and it said nothing about a terrace wall, or a pair of thin gloves, or a house that had been careful about which things got measured. She checked that. It did not move them. A finding said what the blood was. It could not say what anybody had known.

Dr. Vale had a second sheet, and a pen held out by the right end.

"Disclosure," the doctor said. "It's yours to direct. The clinic's own record holds it regardless; I can't take a sample and not have taken it. After that there are two more places it can go and you choose both, either or neither. The station's casualty sheet, which is what anyone answering a call out here is handed at the door. And the survey file, where the line for this outpost says *practitioner, classification contested*."

"Who wrote that line?"

"I don't know. It's older than the survey that's here now."

"It was true when they wrote it." Elvira took the pen. "All three. I'll not have the file go on being right about something that's stopped."

She ticked them and signed, and wrote the time beside her name from the clock in the north wall, to the second, because she could.

Then she put the pen down and asked the analyst the thing she had been building since the early winter. It was the only question she had found that she knew how to ask.

"I read the Corps' reference in the autumn. I read it about you. I've read it since about me, and it reads the same, which I take to be in its favour. What does it leave out that would cost someone if I didn't know it?"

::: pov syra

It was the right question, and Syra noted the size of it.

It was the question her people answered before they were asked, at any posting, in the first week: the thing that would cost someone. It was not the other question. The practitioner had set the two side by side for most of a winter, by the look of it, and had brought only one of them to the table.

"The reference is accurate," Syra said. "It was written from outside, by people looking at us. Two things are in it and are not put as plainly as they should be. A third is not in it, and is only mine."

"Say them."

"Your blood on a human. You know that one. You learned it the way most of them learn it." She did not look at the doctor, who had gloved for a vial in the early winter and had not needed to be told. "The second runs the other way. Nothing given to you will hold. Blood, a graft, a tissue. Your body takes apart whatever did not begin in it. If you are hurt past what you can mend yourself, a medic can keep you warm and still and carry you, and cannot do more. So you do not work alone past that line. You have been working alone for three years, at a boundary, with a knife."

"I didn't know where the line was."

"No. Now you know that there is one."

The practitioner did not look at her hands. She looked at the shelf above the water vessels, where a sealed case sat with the station's mark on it, and then back.

"And the third."

"You are not afraid of the grove. You wrote that it was structural, and it is. The reference calls what we have instead *clear thinking*. I would not. The alarm does not ring in us. That is all it is. So I count, on purpose, what a frightened person would have counted without trying. That is my practice and I do not offer it as my people's. You have built something like it here, alone, and called it failing correctly."

"That's cheaper than I'd feared."

"It is not cheap. It is only all." Syra let that stand, and then said the rest, because withholding the shape of it would have been a kind of lie. "That is what costs. There is more that does not. Some of it I would tell you if you asked. Some I would decline, and I would say that I was declining. You have not asked either sort."

"No."

The practitioner said it without weight. She did not seem to be bracing against anything.

"I've a pair of gloves and a reason I never tested," she said. "That isn't enough to ask with. I don't know yet what the question is, and I'd sooner not spend it on the wrong one."

"Then it will keep. I am at the station."

Syra considered whether that was a thing she could support, and corrected it.

"I am at the station while the station has me. My own paper is under review there. You should know that the person you asked to witness yours has not had hers recognised."

"Does it change what's on the leaf?"

"No."

"Then I'll not hold it against the leaf."

There was one more thing, and it cost nothing either way, which was why Syra had left it till last.

"There is a courtesy," she said. "*Nai.* It is said to us, and between us. It means *still relevant here*. It is a state, and not a title or a kind, and a person may decline it. I would say it to you. I have not, because nobody asked you first."

The practitioner was quiet for the length of time she gave a new instrument.

"I had a phrase once that I thought I'd made," she said. "You were at this table when I found out whose it was. I'd like to look under this one before I answer to it."

"That is the correct order," Syra said.

She had been called it first at a table she could still have drawn. She did not say so. The practitioner had not asked.

::: pov iona

The old kit came down off the shelf with three years of chalk on its lid.

Iona wiped it and turned it to the window. The seal was whole. It was the issue seal, and it had the date on it and a storekeeper's initial, and nobody had broken it for a cut hand or a turned ankle or a night with a fever in it.

"You never opened it."

"I never needed it," Elvira said. "I checked the seal each spring and wrote that I had."

"No. You wouldn't have needed it." Iona set it on the table beside the new one and broke the old seal herself, since somebody ought to. Dressings. Cuff. Splints. The six sheets of graft film in their sleeve, with a use-by date that had passed the winter before last.

She took the film out and put it in her coat.

"That would have done nothing for you," she said. "It wouldn't have hurt. It would have sat on the wound for an hour looking like treatment and then come away. And if somebody had walked out here to help, they'd have put it on you with their bare hands, because nothing in this box told them otherwise."

She opened the new kit. She had packed it herself that morning, against a list she had written in the seven weeks, when she was not allowed to know what she knew and could at least count dressings. The gloves were on top. Four pairs, loose, so that a person did not have to find them. Under the gloves there were closures that were only mechanical, and the wrap, and a folding litter she had argued out of the transit ring's own stores. In the lid, where the casualty channel had been, there was a card in her own print.

*The person who lives here is Krenyi. Glove before you touch blood. It will burn your skin and it will pass. Do not give her anything of yours or anyone's: no blood, no film, no graft. It will not hold. Close what will close. Keep her warm and still. Call the station and say this card's first line.*

Elvira read it from the edge in, as she had read the finding.

"*It will pass*," she said.

"People stop helping when their hands hurt. I'd rather they knew it stops." Iona shut the lid. "I keep a line of yours in the transit protocol. *Record the cost as carefully as the success.* This is the same line. It's just pointing at whoever comes through that door."

"Nobody comes through that door."

"The survey lead did, in the early winter, with a clock. He took a wet case off you with his bare hand." She said it as she would have said a dosage. "He was lucky in what he knew. The next one may only know what's on the card."

Elvira looked at the old kit, open on the table with its dressings in their rows.

"Three years," she said. "I had it down as mine. A thing I kept in good order and never had a use for."

"It was never issued for you. They're not, at a post with one person. They're issued for the second person in the room."

Iona watched that arrive. It did not arrive as it would have in most of her patients. Nothing in the face moved. But Elvira got up and took one pair of gloves out of the new kit before the lid was sealed, and looked about the room. She seemed to be looking for where a stranger's hand would go first.

There was a nail inside the door, at the height of the latch. It had held a lamp once, by the mark on the chalk.

::: pov elvira

She wrote it that evening, under the date and the time.

*Finding returned, two readers, agreeing: Krenyi.* It was the first time the word had gone into the book. She looked at it in her own hand for as long as it took the ink to dry, and it did not look like anything but a word.

*Read by the analyst at my asking; she says it is supported. Sent to the clinic, the casualty sheet and the survey's line. Asked the analyst what would cost someone. Three answers, written overleaf as she gave them. Did not ask the rest. She has said some of it would be declined. I have that written too, so that I do not later think I was not told.*

*A word offered. Not yet looked under.*

She turned the page, to the column for observations she had not intended to make.

*The kit sat sealed for three years and I took the seal for a sign that I did not need anyone. It was never for the one who lives here. It was packed for the one who comes.*

She read it back and let it stand.

The gloves hung on the nail by the door. They were thin and pale and very clean, and they looked like nothing much. She found she did not mind them there. The reason for them was written on a card anyone could read.

She closed the book and went out to the stones.
