*LIGHTS BOARD. NOTICE. The Sceir Light will not be exhibited from dusk on the day stated to dusk on the day following, for the fitting of a movement. Vessels relying on the light for a fix are advised accordingly. By order.*

It had come to the station's operations desk, the station being the only address on that shore the Board could find to advise. The operations desk had sent it up to the Station Manager. The Station Manager had sent it along the corridor to the survey office with four words across the top in his own hand. *Yours, I think. C.D.*

Galahad read it and passed it to Rook. Rook had the Board's notice for something under a second, which was long enough.

"She said the spring," Galahad said.

"She said a third one by the spring, and that it would make no odds. I logged both halves." Rook held the notice out by one corner. "Query. What becomes of the movement that comes out?"

"It goes back to the Board, I'd think. It's theirs."

"It is a clock that has been on that shore for two years. At every signal it has been put right by hand, and the register has what that cost. On the day of fitting it will be put right for the last time." A pause of the kind Rook used for emphasis and logged as processing. "The register can say how far that clock goes wrong between one signal and the next. It cannot say where the clock would go if nobody came. The dock's pair part, reach forty seconds and hold. The attendant asked in the winter whether her nine a season and our forty were the one thing. A movement left running in that tower and never corrected would go on, or it would stop somewhere. Either would be an answer for her. We have none."

Galahad sat with it.

It was good. It was the kind of idea the autumn had been short of, an instrument that asked the shore one thing and did not need a model to read. A clock, a wall, somebody to look at it on signal days. He could see the series. He could see himself reading it in the summer.

The fitting was in six days.

He knew the short road, because he had taken its like before. It was a kilometre of shingle on the next falling tide and one sentence at a door. *When it comes out, don't crate it.* She might even have done it. He turned that over. He would be asking the attendant of a light to keep back her own Board's property on a survey's say, and to keep it running in a tower where the Board, for its own reasons, allowed one clock. He would be asking her to go round her desk. He had a note on his record about that, with his initials under it. The ink was a season old.

He took the notice back from Rook and went down the corridor.

Delveyne heard it standing, which Galahad had learned was how he heard a thing he meant to act on.

"You want me to ask another body to leave a withdrawn clock running in its own tower. Uncorrected. For as long as we care to look at it."

"Yes."

"And you've come here before you've gone there."

"Yes."

"That Board took until the spring to answer a letter of mine that asked it for nothing. Your fitting is in six days."

"I know."

"Then you will very likely lose it."

"I know that too."

Delveyne looked at him a moment longer. Then he sat down and wrote the letter by hand, with Galahad still in the room. It was four sentences. It asked for the movement and said what for, and it said that nothing was wrong with the light. He copied it to the attendant.

"She'll tell you what the Board will make of it," he said. "I'd be glad to hear it myself."

On the sixth day Galahad walked the spit with an hour and fifty of the falling tide, down the middle of the shingle, with Rook behind him.

The Board's inspector was at the door of the tower with a handcart. There was a crate on it, lashed, the size of a seaman's chest, and the man was checking the lashings with the care of someone who would be answering for the contents. He was dressed for the walk. He gave the Corps coat one look and Rook two.

"You'll be the letter," the inspector said.

"I'm the survey. The letter was the Station Manager's."

"It reached the Board on Tuesday. It is being considered." He said it without irony, and Galahad understood that at the Board it was a full sentence. "I brought the new one down on yesterday's tide and I'm taking the old one up on this. I'd no instruction on the letter when I left this morning. So I'll tell you what I can do on my own sheet, and what I can't, and you'll not have had the walk for nothing."

"I'd be obliged."

"I can't leave it. Withdrawn, it's stores, and my docket has to show where the Board's stores went. And I can't leave it running in there if I could. One clock in a light. That's as old a rule as the Board has, and it came down to me without its reason. I've never needed the reason. An attendant on the stair at four in the morning reads the face that's in front of her."

"We have two faces on a dock wall," Galahad said. "We manage it with a callout on every transaction. Traffic Control will tell you what the callout costs."

"Then you know." The inspector tightened the last strap. "Here's what I can do. The docket has a line for disposal. A movement two years old with nothing wrong with it would go down as *returned to store*, and the store would have it stripped for parts by midsummer. I've written *Held. Correspondence pending.* Nobody at the Board strips a thing with correspondence on it."

"It's stopped."

"It's stopped. She stopped it herself last night at dusk, with the lamp, and wrote the hour." He took up the handles of the cart. "If the Board says yes, you'll have a clock that's been two days in a crate and a week on a shelf, and somebody will have to start it and say what it was started against. I don't know if that's any use to you."

Galahad did the arithmetic. It was no longer the instrument Rook had described. It would be a clock restarted against the standard on a known day, somewhere on this shore, if a Board he had never dealt with chose to say so. It was less than he had wanted. It was still more than the autumn had given him.

"It's some use," he said. "Thank you for the line."

"Thank the form. It had the room."

The attendant was in the doorway behind him with her arms folded. She had heard all of it.

"He wrote again, so," Nuala Beirne said. "Him above. I'd my copy Wednesday."

"He did. He asked what the Board would make of it."

"They'll read it as a clock asking for attention." She looked at the crate going away up the shingle, and at the door behind her. The new movement was already in its place in the tower, not yet started. "I'll have a fourth by the autumn. Tell him I'd take a kettle sooner."

::: pov lights-board-inspector

He timed the character from the shingle, nineteen days on, with the watch set at the Board that morning.

Two flashes. Dark. He took five groups and had fifteen seconds group to group, inside the tolerance, and wrote the figure against his thigh. The light was correct. It was being timed now by a movement he had chosen himself from the three in the Board's store, and had carried down this spit on a cart. He had seen it started on a bench at the Board against the Board's own standard and had watched it run a week there without a fault. He found that he had wanted the figure to be correct more than he usually wanted anything on this walk. He wrote that off against the cart.

The door was open. The sheet took its hour. Lamp. Glass. Structure, the pointing good. Stores, and no kettle on the requisition, which he had half looked for. Gauge against the plate.

He had come on a signal day on purpose, and he did not tick the clock until the signal came.

She read the one against the other. She did it as she had with the last movement and the one before it, and he could not have said from her back what she saw. Then she put her hand to the movement and made the correction. It was a small one, and it went the way he had known it would. He realised he had known. He had carried a new clock a kilometre down a spit knowing it.

She came to the table and wrote the line.

"Two," she said.

"In nineteen days."

"In nineteen days. It's a good movement. It'll run as true as the last, and the last ran as true as any I've kept."

"I saw it run a week on the Board's bench. It didn't want a second."

"I'd say it didn't," she said. "It wasn't here."

He ticked the two boxes, *corrected against signal* and *correction logged*, because both were true.

He had never asked to turn the register back. An inspector read the page it was open at, and the page was always the season's. The note in the margin he had known by heart since his first year at the Board, and had seen with his own eyes perhaps a dozen times. *The tower has its own habit and always has.* It was an inspector's sentence, and a sound one. The man who wrote it had looked at a column and said what it showed.

"The fitting before this," he said. "The winter one. And the ones before that, if they're marked. I'd like to see the pages."

She looked at him. Then she went up the stair, and came down with the first two volumes as well as the third, one at a time, and set them on the table and found the places without hunting. There was a thing she did at the end of each season that was not in the column. He had seen her finger on the page once and had not asked.

Four of them. The fitting of two winters back, in her hand: *Movement replaced. Lamp out one day. Set to signal.* And three weeks under it, the first line of the new clock. A two.

The one before was the hand before hers, in ballpoint. A one. Before that there was a pasted slip, and a fitting written up at a length that suggested the attendant had not enjoyed it. The first correction after was the figure the book had been showing all that decade. The oldest was in copperplate, in the first volume, brown. The first correction was very small. It went the same way.

He read all four twice.

The sentence in the margin said the tower. He had five clocks' worth of column on the table in front of him, and a sixth up the stair that he had passed on a bench under his own initials. The tower had been repointed in those years, more than once, and he had signed for a gallery rail himself. Nothing had been asked to carry the habit from one clock to the next, and it had been carried.

He did not know what that meant. He was a Board man, and the Board kept lights, and this one was kept. He had no box for it and did not think he was owed one.

But there was the line under *Clock*, and he had to put something in it that was true.

He wrote: *Corrected against signal and logged. Rate as previously noted.* He looked at it. Last summer the previous note had been her requisition, in the Board's file. Today the previous note was his own line from last summer, and under that her requisition, and under that a dead man's margin. Every one of them had been written about a different clock.

So he went on, in the space that was left. *Movement new, fitted nineteen days. Correction two seconds.*

It was the first figure he had ever put on a sheet for this light that was not the character.

"They'll send you another," she said. She was at the sill.

"They've nothing better to send. That was the best of the three." He capped his pen. "I've put a number on it. I don't know who reads numbers at the Board. Somebody may."

"And your man's letter? The old one, in the crate."

"Being considered."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's not anything yet." He folded the sheet. "It's on a shelf with a docket on it that says *held*, and I look at the shelf when I pass. That's what I have."

She unfolded her arms, which he had not seen her do on an inspection before.

"That's more than most send down that spit," she said.

He went up to the gallery before he left and timed it again from the rail. It was correct. The glass carried its low note, as it always did. He stood a while with the watch in his hand. It was the Board's time, set that morning, and it was right, and so was the clock below him as of a quarter of an hour ago. He knew now which of the two would need telling again.

Going up the shingle he passed the two from the station coming down. The tall one was in the middle of the spit and the robot behind. He gave them the time of the tide, since it was the thing he had.

**LOG ENTRY — Rook-7**
**Location: Sceir Light, ground room; then Threshold Station, Survey Office**
**Context: Reading of the register's third volume at the attendant's table, with her leave. Spring review of open items.**

Feet wiped. Not instructed this time. *Noted by the attendant, who did not remark on it.*

Third volume read where it lies. New entries since mid-winter, in order: signal days, corrections, initials. Then: *Movement replaced. Lamp out one day. Set to signal.* Then, nineteen days on, today, a correction of 2 seconds. Direction: as every prior entry.

The Board's inspector had the first two volumes down before we arrived. They were open at four places. The attendant had not put them back. I asked whether I might look at the places. She said I had already imaged them in the autumn and could look at my own. *Correct. I looked at my own.*

Transcription, volumes one to three: four earlier replacements of the movement, each entered by the attendant of the day. Analyst Syra read them in the autumn for what they show about the clock, which is that the direction is not the clock's. I filed the fitting days as *maintenance events* and excluded them from the series as discontinuities. That was sound practice. It was also a classification made in the first week, and I did not go back to it.

Re-filed. At a fitting the new movement is set to the signal on a written day and is then not touched until the next signal. That is a clock started against the standard and left alone. It is the instrument I proposed in the survey office. The register holds five of them, the oldest in copperplate. Each ran for one interval. *So did every other line in the book. A correction is the end of a short run that nobody was watching.*

Limit, stated: one interval is as far as any of them goes. The size of the first correction after a fitting varies, the interval varies, and I will not take a rate from five cases. After the winter fitting of 2824 to 2825 the season's sum fell from nine to seven and was nine again in two years. One case. Not a period. What a clock on this shore does past one interval is not in the register, because its keepers were keeping a light.

Schema, category *Sources not made to answer a question*, item 1, amended: the register is two hundred and seventeen years of short runs, each started against the standard and each ended by hand. *Team Lead Thorne asked the Station Manager to ask the Board for a long one. I proposed it. I had the short ones in my own file at the time and had them labelled as gaps.*

I reported this to Team Lead Thorne on the spit, outbound. He walked nine paces before answering. Then: "We'd still want the one left alone." *Agreed. The short runs say which way and about how fast. They cannot say whether it stops. That is the attendant's question, and it is still owed.*

He told the attendant he still owed her. She said she had it written down. I have not seen where.

Open items, spring, 2827 UCSD:

1. External review, regional survey office, forwarded mid-winter. Return: none. One acknowledgement of receipt.
2. Analyst Syra's certification, flag actioned mid-winter. Return: none.
3. Withdrawn movement, Sceir Light. Stopped. In the Lights Board's store under *Held*. Board's answer to the Station Manager's letter: being considered.
4. Outpost chronometer, Marsh Causeway. Running. Not yet read against the standard. The outpost's own records now cite it to the second.
5. Entity displaced from the causeway grove in the late autumn. Not present in the sector. Location: not established.
6. Cause of the forty seconds: unidentified.

*I arrived in the early autumn with two instruments that disagreed and a file that said there was nothing to record. I now hold a watch log, a survey series and a register of short runs. They were kept by people who were not asking, and they agree with one another. What they agree on is that the shore disagrees with the standard. That is more than I had. It is not a cause.*

Lamp lit at dusk as we cleared the shingle. Character counted. Correct.

Item 6 carried.

**END LOG ENTRY**
