::: pov priya-nakamura

It was the first referral with her name on it, and it was about a light switch.

Priya Nakamura read it standing, because Senior Sergeant Osei had handed it to her standing, with the face of a man passing along something that had been passed to him. *Common lighting variance, unscheduled. Deck 7, berth row C. Six occurrences in nineteen days.* Under that was the monitoring officer's reconciliation. Six times the row's overhead light had gone down for most of an hour and come up again, at hours the habitat's schedule did not hold. Under that was the clause. A variance may recur if a schedule for it is on file. After a seventh unscheduled occurrence the panel is set to the habitat's schedule and its hand control is covered, pending review.

Nobody is unkind about it. A cover is a small grey plate with two screws. Review, on Eden, takes a season.

She did what she does with anything that is a claim, and made a table. Twice in the small hours. Once across the end of first shift. Once in the middle of lunch. She tried four periods against the six hours and none of them fitted, and she wrote down that none fitted, which is a result.

Then she went and asked, because the cheapest instrument on Eden is the one in the wall.

Row C is nine doors past the laundry, with a window at the far end that looks onto the terraces. The panel is by the fourth door. She stood in front of it and said, "Warden. Who turns this row down?"

"A resident, at this panel, by hand," the Warden said. "The panel is there for that. A row is allowed its own evening. It isn't mine to tell you who, or why. I can ask whether they'll see you."

"Do you know when the next one will be?"

"No. I looked for a pattern and didn't find one I would put a number on. Six is not many."

"What does the row do, when it goes dark?"

"Mostly nothing. I leave the floor line lit, and people walk by it. During the last one, two doors were open."

"Has anyone complained?"

"Nobody has said anything to me at all."

She wrote that down. On Eden a thing that has produced no forms from the people nearest it is a finding, and she did not yet know what it was a finding of.

The resident would see her. The resident was behind the fourth door, with a thimble on and somebody's work jacket over her knee, and said her name before she was asked for it: Nóra Ní Ruairc. She mended for the laundry. She was from Drithane, which Nakamura had to place, and placed as the cold one, where the Survey people went to look at the sky.

"It's the crossing, at home," Nóra Ní Ruairc said. "When the sky sparks, we put our own lights out. There's no law about it. I've a row and a switch, so."

"There's nothing to see from here."

"No."

She did not add anything, and Nakamura, who asks the follow-up question for a living, looked at the one she had and found it was not hers to ask. She asked the useful one instead. "When is the next?"

"I don't know."

The hours came on a calendar, the woman said. Every valley makes one, and her sister sent her the valley's own each year. This year's had not come, and would not for a while yet. Nothing lifts from Drithane while the world is in the band if it can help it, and the post is not an exception. So the paper that says when the crossing hours are cannot leave until the crossing is over. She had been reckoning this one from last year's, by the rule her mother used.

"And the rule is good?"

"It was good the first night. I've been a little later than the sky every night since. I can feel it going, and I can't mend it." She turned the jacket over. "I'll not write down hours I haven't got, Detective. Not for a form."

Nakamura did not argue. It was her own rule, in somebody else's mouth.

Back at her desk she asked Reeves whether anyone on Eden held the hours.

"The Survey Corps holds the band's ephemeris to the second," Reeves said. "The Corps is the other chain. A request runs through the Passage oversight's liaison desk on a form of four pages. The median return is fourteen weeks. Might I ask whether the Detective would like it sent regardless?"

She sent it regardless. Then she looked at what she had. By lunch on the first day she had who, and why, and what for, which is not usual, and none of it was what the form wanted. The form has a box for a reason, and nobody reads the box. It wanted hours.

The seventh occurrence would close the matter for her. She did not know when it was. Neither did the woman who was going to cause it.

The referral had been in the queue two days before the Senior Sergeant found it, and Barsik had spent both of them on the terminal, which is warm.

He could make nothing of the six hours. He noted that, and noted why: he has never been off this habitat, and the hours were not this habitat's. He had read the detective's notes over her shoulder from the cabinet top, upside down, and knew where the hours were from. He knew who on the floor had been there.

So he gave the trainee the table as a reading exercise. Six hours. Find the rule.

She went still, which with her is hard to tell from the ordinary condition, and he could tell. Then she went to her basket, and came back with a sheet of paper folded in eight. He had read about it in the last page of her Drithane report. He had never seen it.

She asked what the doctrine said.

He had been turning that over since the first day, and gave her all of it, because it was short. The doctrine is about what an agent files. The paper was not a finding. The agency had not issued it, and had not sent her to get it. A boy had given it to a rabbit, at a leaving, as a present. It was hers. There is no line in the doctrine about what a rabbit owns, and he did not propose to write one this week.

*Exercise six is not set,* he told her.

He told her one thing more, because a thing she already knew is not an instruction. A sheet of paper carried in the mouth arrives with the mouth on it. She had watched him carry, in the summer, and had written it down.

Then he went back to the cabinet top. The next part was not his to watch from close.

::: pov bubochka

The paper had lived under her blanket since the spring.

A boy on Drithane had given it to her, on the morning she left. It was his valley's crossing calendar, copied out in his own hand, with the hours of the sparks marked in red. He had copied the next crossing too, all of it, because he was a thorough boy. He had said it was in case Eden wanted one.

She had thought he was being kind. Eden did not have a sky. What would Eden want with the hours of somebody else's?

Now Eden wanted one. Not all of Eden. One row.

She did the sum first. On Drithane she had kept two clocks: the valley's, and Eden's, which went on running in her head. She had written both down every night, because she writes everything down. So she could turn a red hour into an Eden hour, which the detective could not do. The first red hour came out on the detective's first, to the minute. The other five came out a little before hers, and a little more before each time.

The next red hour was tonight. Twenty to three.

Nobody could tell her to give the paper away. Barsik had been clear about that. Then he had gone up on the cabinet, which was him being clear a second time.

She looked at the red marks for a while. Her log had the hours. She had copied them in on the way home. It did not have the boy's handwriting. A log cannot hold that.

Then she took the paper in her teeth, as gently as she could, and went.

Row C was bright and empty. She sat down under the panel by the fourth door and did not put the paper down. At night a row's floor belongs to the night pass, and she had counted what the night pass does with paper.

Twenty to three came.

The light stayed on. Nothing happened at all. A long way away, over a valley she knew, the sky was starting, slow and gold, and here there was a clean floor and nine shut doors. She was the only one on Eden who knew what time it was.

She had written, once, that the custom was for not being the sort of place that would miss them. She sat in the sort of place that had.

A housekeeping unit came down the row, slow and square. It stopped in front of her. A rabbit is not an article. It went round.

At seven minutes past three the fourth door opened. The woman came out with her hand already lifted to the panel, and saw a white rabbit sitting under it, holding a piece of paper.

She looked twice. People from Drithane do.

Bubochka set the paper down on the doorsill, on the berth side, where the floor is somebody's.

The woman picked it up and unfolded it. She read the name at the top out loud, quietly, the way you say a neighbour's name. "Gleann na gCaorach." Then she found the red hour, and looked at the clock on the wall, and stood there.

"Twenty-seven minutes," she said. Not to the rabbit. To the hour.

She did not turn the light down. The hour had gone, and she was not a person who would keep it late on purpose.

Then she looked at Bubochka again, the way the shepherd had, with arithmetic in it. And then, like the shepherd, she said nothing about it at all. Bubochka had not known until then that those two things came from the same place.

The woman went in and sat down at her table with a form. She left the door open while she wrote.

Bubochka went home the long way. Her mouth felt empty, and she put that in the log.

::::: scene 3

::: pov priya-nakamura

The form came back into the queue at two minutes past seven with the schedule box ticked.

Clipped to it was a sheet of paper folded in eight and soft at the folds. A valley's name ran across the top in a child's capitals. Under it were columns of nights, and beside some of the nights an hour, in red. Nóra Ní Ruairc had not copied it out. She had sent the thing itself.

Nakamura does not take a schedule on trust because it has arrived. She gave it to Reeves, who has the Compact's almanac, and Reeves turned the valley's hours into Eden's and laid them beside her table.

They did not match.

The first night matched to the minute. On the second, the row had gone dark four minutes after the red hour. Then nine. Then thirteen, eighteen, twenty-two. She looked at that column for a long moment, and it was the best moment of the case. A schedule made up to fit her table would have fitted it. This one disagreed with the past, by a little more each night, in the direction and at about the rate she had been told to expect by the person it embarrassed.

There had been a red hour in the night just gone, at twenty to three. The row's log held nothing for it. The row had stayed lit.

She went to the fourth door and asked where the calendar had come from.

"It was brought to me."

"By whom?"

"A neighbour." The thimble was on again. "I didn't ask more than that, Detective. At home we don't."

Nakamura wrote that down. She wrote one more thing down, in the notebook that is not the bureau's. One corner of the sheet had two small dents in it, close together, and a faint dry ripple where the paper had been damp. She did not measure the distance between the dents. She knew what she would have measured it against, and that the control for that experiment was asleep in a tray.

The next red hour was that evening, at twenty to ten. She was on row C at half past nine.

"The file needs the hours," she said, and held out the sheet. "Reeves has made it a copy. It doesn't need the paper."

Nóra Ní Ruairc took it in both hands. There was a pin already in the wall by her door, where a kitchen calendar goes.

"There is a schedule on file now," the Warden said, from the panel. "I can turn the row down for you at the red hours, if you'd like."

"No. Thank you."

"I thought not. I'll leave the floor line as it is."

"The sky doesn't mind the small ones," the woman said, and put her hand on the panel, and waited.

At twenty to ten she turned it down.

Nakamura had come to verify an hour, and it had verified. She wrote *one of one* by the floor line's glow. Then she looked up from the notebook, because the row had changed.

Three doors were open. A man stood in one with a towel over his shoulder. Two children who should have been in bed were not, and nobody was saying so. Nobody had asked them to come out, and she had the Warden's word that nobody had said anything at all. The window at the end showed the terraces, which are dark at that hour, and far up the curve of the ring a few small lights where other people were still awake. On the sill sat the station cat. She has stopped being surprised by this, and has not stopped noting it.

Nobody on the row looked at the window. Nóra Ní Ruairc stood with her back against her own door frame and her face lifted a little, towards a ceiling. There was nothing there. She had said there would not be.

Nakamura stayed until the light came back. She did not log that part. She had no column for it.

In the morning she closed the referral. *Variance scheduled. Schedule verified against one occurrence. No further action.* The four pages to the Survey Corps were still out. She left them out. Someday they would come back, and she would like to see what they said.

::: pov bubochka

Barsik told her in the morning, in a few words. The wall. The pin. Three doors.

So the paper was by a door now, where a kitchen calendar goes. She still had the hours. She would always have the hours.

The red hour after that fell two days on, at ten past three in the afternoon, with the bureau floor lit and busy all round her tray.

She did the thing she had learned at the drithle. She let everything of her own that was busy go quiet. The counting. The filing. The watching of the floor. One at a time, the way a valley puts its windows out, the far end first.

The Senior Sergeant walked past and did not look twice.

Eden did not have a sky. She had thought, in the spring, that this might be the trouble with it. She took that page of her log out now and looked at it. Eden had a woman who turned a light down for a sky she could not see. It had three open doors. And a long way off there was a boy who had written out the hours, in case.

She did not have a heading for that either. She started one.

Far away the sparks were starting, slow and gold. Row C was not missing them.

Neither was she.
