::: pov maud-okoro

Maud Okoro had the fourth place from the ramp, and had had it for nine years.

A loading chain is a simple thing. The crates come off the trolleys at the bottom and go up through a line of hands. At the top there is a ramp, and past the ramp is somebody else's hold. She had stood in most of the places in twenty-three years. The bottom is heavy and the top is fussy. Fourth is the best. By fourth, a crate has stopped surprising anybody.

There is one rule. You take what the hand below gives you, and you give it to the hand above. You do not open it and you do not ask. If every hand on the chain asked, nothing would leave Eden before the week was out. The hand below you has looked. That is what the hand below is for.

She believed that. It was true nearly always, which is how a thing comes to be believed.

She had passed a rabbit up once, years back, for a delegation. Somebody had said it was a rabbit. She could not have told you now who had handed it to her.

Today it was the Rangers' supply shuttle, and at the top of a Rangers' ramp somebody reads the list out loud, every crate by its number, as it crosses. The quartermaster mostly did it herself. Today she had given the board to a cadet, and stood a pace behind him with her hands behind her back.

Maud knew the cadet, the way you know anybody who grew up near your deck. He was the boy with the cat. She had seen him come through this dock once when he was small, with a grey-and-white cat over his shoulder and the look of somebody who had settled a thing. He would be eighteen now. He wore the Rangers' shell, with two bars on the tab, and the second bar was new enough that he kept not looking at it.

He read well. A number, a look at the crate, a mark, the next. He did not hurry and he did not hold anybody up. Nobody on the chain was watching him. There is no reason to watch a list.

The cup came up between a case of filters and the mail.

She saw it start, because fourth has a view of the bottom. A small girl stood where the trolleys turn, holding something in both hands. She said something to the man who has the bottom place, and he bent down to hear it. Then he took the thing from her, carefully, and turned, and gave it to the next hand up.

Nobody carried it. It was passed. Each hand took it because the hand below had, and each face did the same thing as it went by, which was to go soft.

When it reached her she saw why. It was a drinking cup with a cloth tied over the top, and through a hole in the cloth came three leaves. It was a strawberry, a baby one off the children's row, with its string just cut.

"For her sister," the hand below said. "On the station. She went up in the spring."

Maud knew whose sister. Everybody on the chain did.

She took it. Of course she took it. It weighed nothing, and it was a kind thing, and to say anything else she would have had to say it to eight people and a child. She turned and gave it to third. "For her sister," she said, and got her hands ready for the mail.

::: pov lev

The quartermaster had told him the job in two sentences. Everything on the list goes. Nothing else does.

Then she had given him the board and stepped back. That was how he knew it was his.

The ramp was the Rangers'. The dock was Eden's. There was a line across the floor where one stopped and the other started, and his boots were on the ramp side of it. He could say what crossed. He could not say anything at all about the dock. He had lived on the dock's side of that line since he was eight, and it was strange to stand and look at it from here.

He read the list. A crate came, and he found its number, and said it, and marked it. The column for the mark was headed *Carried*. Beside it, at the edge of the sheet, was a narrow column headed *Not carried*. In all the lists he had read, nothing had ever been written in that one.

The filters came, and he said their number. Then the hand below him held up a cup.

It was not a crate, so it had no number. He looked for it anyway, all the way down the list and back, because that was the job. There were filters, and mail, and nine kinds of seal. There was no cup.

He looked at the cup. A cloth was tied over it, and three leaves came out through a hole in the cloth. He knew the leaf. He had kept one of those on a windowsill when he was eight, and for one autumn it had been the only one there was. The ones on the terraces now had all come off it.

"For her sister," the hand said. "On the station."

Lev looked down the chain. He should not have. Everybody on it was looking up at him, and they all had the same face on, the kind one. At the very bottom stood a small girl with her hands still held out in the shape of the cup.

His own hand had already started to take it.

It had done that once before, on a walkway, when he was eight. He had stopped it then because the thing in his pocket was his lunch. There was no lunch this time. There was the list.

He stopped it. It was harder to stop than he remembered.

"No," Lev said. Out loud. In front of everybody.

Then, because a no ought to say what it is a no to, "It isn't on the list."

It took about four seconds. Nobody said anything. They were long seconds, and he was in them by himself.

He did not take the cup. It was not his, and it had not crossed. He held the board against his chest and wrote in the narrow column. *One cup. One plant.* The column did not ask why. He was glad of that. He had no better why than the list.

Then he turned round. The quartermaster had not moved.

He said it once, in order. "There is a girl at the bottom of the chain. She has sent a plant for her sister on the station. It is not on the list, and I have not carried it. Is there a way for it to be on the next one?"

Then he waited.

"There's a form," the quartermaster said. "There has always been a form. People would sooner hand a thing to the chain." She took a sheet from under the list and gave it to him. "She fills it in, or somebody fills it in with her. It goes to whoever writes the next list. That isn't me, and it isn't you."

He looked at the form. It had four boxes. *What it is. Who sends it. Who it is for. Who will meet it.*

They were in the right order.

"That column has been empty since I had the board," the quartermaster said. She was not praising him. She was giving him a count. "I write a note, mostly. A note is quieter."

He did not know what to say to that, so he did not say anything.

He folded the form once and held it out to the hand below, the one that still had the cup. "It goes back down, please. With this."

The hand took it. The cup went back down the chain the way it had come up, hand to hand, and every hand was as careful with it going down as it had been coming up. He had not known a chain would do that. He did not think the chain had known either.

"Next," the quartermaster said.

The mail came. He found its number, and said it.

::: pov maud-okoro

The cup came to her from above. In twenty-three years nothing had.

She took it. Her hands did not know what else to do with a thing held out to them. There was a folded sheet under the string now. She gave both to the hand below and said, "It goes back down. With that," because those were the words that had come with it.

Then she stood with her hands empty and thought about it, which fourth place does not usually leave time for.

He had not been unkind. That was the part she kept turning over. She had heard *no* said on this dock by angry people, and by tired people, and by people who wanted to be seen saying it. This had been none of those. He had said it the way he said a crate's number. Then he had found the child a form.

She had passed that cup. So had the five below her. Not one of them had looked at the list, because the list was at the top, and the top was somebody else's.

The mail went up. A case of seals went up. A drum went up that took two of them.

Then a tin came into her hands. It was a flat tin with a galley label, and the hand below said, "For the crew. From the galley. It's only biscuits."

She had passed that tin, or its cousins, on more loadings than she could count.

"Is it on the list?" Maud said.

It came out louder than she meant. The hand below looked at her as if she had asked whether it was on fire.

"It's biscuits."

"I know it's biscuits." She turned her head. "Cadet. A tin from the galley. Is it on the list?"

There was a pause while he went down the sheet and back.

"No."

So she gave the tin to the hand below. Her face was hot. It had taken about four seconds, and it had not been comfortable, and the galley would hear of it by supper.

Nobody else on the chain did it that day. The next thing to come up without a number was a jumper in a bag. It went through nine pairs of hands and stopped where the cup had stopped, at the ramp. That was all right. That was what the ramp was for.

But the tin had stopped at fourth.

::: pov barsik

He watched it from the gantry above the transfer dock, where the rail is warm. He goes up by the stair now, and not by the jump. He notes that. A file that leaves out the condition of its own keeper is not a complete file.

The rabbit was on the bin at the end of the dock. He had not sent her.

Ten years ago he came to this rail to see whether a loading chain would stop for a boy. He did not find out. The boy had been brought to the crate before there was a chain to stop, and he had filed that as the correct outcome and left the other question where it was.

It was put again today. He recorded what happened with some care, because it was not what either side of the file would have guessed.

The chain did not stop. It has never stopped, and it did not stop today. Nine pairs of hands took a cup from a child and passed it upward, kindly, exactly as hands on this dock passed a small warm thing upward in 2826. What stopped was the cup. It stopped at the one place on the chain where a person had been put on purpose and given a list. The boy is that person now. He said it out loud, in front of the others. It took four seconds.

Then the chain carried the cup back down. He had not expected that, and he looked at it for some time. A chain carries everything it is handed. He has had that on file for ten years as the difficulty. Nobody had handed it a refusal before, to see. *One instance. Not a finding.*

The third note is a correction. His line of 2827 says the counter is also a person who has not yet learned that it is rude. That line no longer describes this subject. He has learned. His hand went out. It could be seen from the rail. He stopped it and said the word, and it cost him what it costs anybody. Barsik read the board afterwards, from a crate, while the quartermaster signed it. The service the boy has joined keeps a column for the word. There was nothing in the column above his entry.

*He is eighteen, he has a cat, and he is theirs.*

One more, for the list he keeps of people on this habitat who can stand being rude for four seconds. At the fourth place from the ramp a dock hand held a tin, and asked a question, and handed the tin back. The list is longer by one. It has been getting longer at about that rate for ten years.

He wrote *correct rate of progress* beside it. He had only ever put that in a trainee's log. He had not thought to put it in a habitat's.

For completeness. The specimen is two decks down, in the warm under a window, and Eden's pet register has it as *pet (Muffin), one*. Eden's hazard register still has it as Class III. The two registers are kept in different rooms. The sheet he lodged where the floor could read it is still lodged. Nobody has answered it.

He is in no hurry.

::: pov lev

When the shuttle had closed he stepped over the line. It was only a line. He had crossed it the other way that morning.

The girl was sitting on an empty trolley at the foot of the chain, with the cup between her knees and the form on a crate lid. She had filled in all four boxes, in capitals, with a pencil somebody had lent her. In the first box she had written STRAWBERRY, and under it, smaller, IT HAS ROOTS.

She held the form out to him. She did not say anything. She waited.

Lev knew about waiting. He read it through, all four boxes. It was in order.

"I'll hand it in," he said. "I don't write the list. I don't know if it will go."

"Is it likely?"

He thought about that properly, because she had asked properly. "More likely than not. I can't say better than that."

She nodded, the way you nod at an answer that is not the one you wanted and is still an answer. Then she picked the cup up in both hands, to take it home until the next shuttle.

"You have to change the water every morning," she told him. He was a Ranger, and nobody might have told him.

"Yes," Lev said. "You do."
