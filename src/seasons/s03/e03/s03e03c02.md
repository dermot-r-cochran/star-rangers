:::pov emeka-lindahl

The hull was two hours on the approach before it reached the lock, and Lindahl spent them at the watch desk. The post was his until it was not, and he did not mean to hold it from a corridor.

Thirty-three days. The post had passed to him on the morning its Starwarden's hull cleared, by one line in the log, *held by the Chief Ranger pending a Starwarden*, and he had put his initial under it, as he had three times before in nine years. A fold-approach post does not stop for a vacancy. Six instruments went on agreeing with one another every ninety seconds, twenty people went on standing the bill, and the line office, nine hours up the relay, went on receiving a day's summary that said so. He ran the watch. That had been his before and would be his after. What a Chief held in the gap was the other thing, the answering, and he had never much liked the fit of it, and had never said so, since it was not on any form.

The watch called the officer who had gone up the dead Starwarden. It had called every one of them that, from the morning the hull cleared, and nothing was meant by it. A Starwarden who went up did not write back to the post, and the post did not write after. It belonged to whoever came next, and the watch had wanted a short word for a person who was perfectly well and was not to be asked.

The list had come on the twelfth day. *Starwarden: Shepherd, T., effective on assumption.* Under it, nothing. He had known the name, as anyone did who read lists: a rank and a half ahead of her cohort, a different corps on every rotation, no post before this one. He had looked under the name for a note from the line. Some Line Captains wrote one. This one had not, and he had turned the sheet over, which was foolish, and had been a little ashamed of turning it.

So the post had been given a name and left to make up its own mind, and what a watch expects of an officer who has risen quickly is not hard to guess. They rearrange. He had served under one. A Starwarden a week into the rank looks for the thing that will make the post hers, and the nearest thing to hand is always the bill, which is not hers to write.

She came through the lock with one bag and the order. A single ring at the collar, and no colour in it.

"Chief."

"Starwarden."

He had the log open on the desk and the state sheet beside it, squared. She put the bag down by the hatch, clear of the watch, and read the log. She read it standing, and she read all of it: thirty-three days, six watches to a day, from the line with his initial under it to the last agreement before her hull. It took most of an hour. The watch worked round her. He did not offer the summary. She had not asked for it, and a person reading a log does not want it read to her.

Then she wrote. *Post assumed*, and the time to the minute, by the post's clock and not the hull's, and her initial. It was a short line and it was the whole act. From the minute in it the post answered to her: six instruments, twenty people and a stretch of approach, in whatever state they were.

He had expected to feel it go. He felt the desk under his hand, as before.

The sheet was the second thing. It was his statement, on the line's form, of the post as he handed it over. *Instruments: six, in agreement; audits within date. Watches: set; bill attached. Stores: to the next hull and forty days past it. People: twenty, fit for duty. Open matters: none.* His initial. Under his initial the form kept a line for the officer assuming, three words already printed, *received as stated*, and a space.

She read the sheet. She did not take up the pen.

"It wants your initial under mine," he said. "The line doesn't require it. It is usual."

"I have assumed the post. That is in the log."

"And the sheet."

"The sheet is what you say it is, Chief. I have been aboard an hour." She set it down squared, as he had had it. "If it goes up with my initial it says I checked. I have not."

He gave her the other side of it, since it was a position and she ought to have it before she chose. "It will go up with one initial, and the line office reads a blank. Three Starwardens have signed that line in my time. The last signed it on the sill. A sheet that goes up without it reads as a Starwarden who would not take the holding officer's word."

"Is the sheet correct?"

"It is."

"Then it says so, over your initial. Send it as it stands."

He did not argue it a second time. He had stated it once and she had heard it, and it was hers to decide.

"The bill has a line for the Starwarden," she said.

"The middle watch. Midnight to four, by the post's clock."

"Who has stood it?"

That was not on the sheet. The sheet had no line for it. "It has been covered."

"I read the log," she said. "I will take it as the bill stands, from tonight."

That was all of it. She had not said *you*, and she had not said *thirty-three nights*. The bill was his, and she had not touched it. She had stepped onto the one line of it that had her rank printed on it.

He asked whether she wanted the office cleared. The dead Starwarden's charts were still on the bulkhead, and the chair was set to a height that suited nobody aboard. She looked in from the hatch and said it could stay as it was until she knew what she would put there, and carried her bag to quarters herself.

He sent the sheet up at half past two, by the post's clock, with one initial on it. Then he did what he did with a bill that looked wrong, and worked it through twice.

A Starwarden who signs *received as stated* has put a date on the post's condition. Whatever is found amiss afterwards was stated wrong, by the Chief who stated it, before her time. That is what the line is for. Three had signed it, and he had taken each signature as an officer accepting his word. An officer who does not sign has nothing standing between herself and the post. If the fourth instrument fell out of agreement tomorrow, there was a line in the log that said the post was hers, and no sheet that said it had been sound when she got it.

It came out the same both times. He had braced for an officer who would not take his word. This one had left his word where he had put it, with his initial on it, and had put nothing of her own on top.

:::pov tissadelle

*A Starwarden answers for it.* She had read the sentence every day since the rank came through, and at the watch desk it had turned out to have no clause in it about being shown round first.

That was the line in the log. It was not conditional and could not be made so. From the minute beside her initial the post was hers as it was, not as it was described, and the distance between those two was the thing she had been promoted to carry. She had written the line when she had finished reading and not before. It was the only order of doing it she could have defended, and she had known, writing it, how little an hour's reading was.

The sheet was a different kind of paper. It was a Chief's statement about twenty people and six instruments he had answered for through thirty-three days, and she had no standing to doubt it or to agree with it. *Received as stated* would have been a second statement, hers, about the same twenty people, on an hour's acquaintance and a log. She knew what the line was used for. She had seen it used, on other rotations, by officers who were not careless: it closed the account of the officer before. Signed, the Chief's thirty-three days became something she had accepted, and then they were partly hers, the good of them with the rest. They were not hers. She had not stood one of them.

And she did not need the account closed. The post answered to her either way.

He had read it as doubt. She had watched him read it, in the moment before he gave her the line office's side, squarely, as a thing she was owed. She had not corrected him. The correction would have been a speech about herself, made to a man who had been on his feet since the forenoon. He would work it through, or the post would show him, and either was better than being told.

What the sheet had not said was in the log, and it was the one thing in thirty-three days of log that she had read twice. The bill cut the day in six, and every watch closed on its officer's initial. For thirty-three days the forenoon watch had closed on *E.L.*, and so had the middle: sixty-six initials in one hand, none of them a line late. Under *People* the sheet said twenty, fit for duty. That was true as well, by the form's measure. The form had no line for what it had cost one of the twenty to keep it true, and he had not written it in the margin.

So she had taken the watch. It was the one line on his bill that was already hers, and taking it changed nothing he had written.

*One ring,* Tobble said, inward. He had waited until the hatch to quarters was shut. *The bar is gone. The colour is gone as well. I counted twice, since they do not usually go down.*

*It has not gone down.*

*I did not say the rank. I said the count.* A pause, in which he was plainly looking at something. *There is nothing under it.*

*Under the ring?*

*Under your name. On the list. I have looked since the list came, and there is nothing there to count.*

She had looked too. She knew how a name came onto a list under a post, as anyone carried on the lists knew it: the desk asked a word of the last Chief the officer had answered to, and kept nothing. The last Chief she had answered to was Arashobal, who would have given the material part and stopped. The material part was one summer, a section on the outer docks and a window stated as a band. It did not seem to her a great deal to hang a post from. Somebody had thought it enough, and the list was made so that she could neither thank them nor ask what else had been said.

*Nothing,* she said. *That is how it is meant to arrive.*

*Then it arrived correctly,* said Tobble, and let it be.

She unpacked the one bag. The middle watch was at midnight.

:::pov tissadelle

The instruments agreed with one another every ninety seconds. On a middle watch that was a hundred and sixty agreements, and she read each one as it came.

Nobody needed her to. The instruments read their own agreement, as they had on Eden, where she had stood this watch from the other end of the ladder, a Deputy at somebody else's desk with a table of classes she had learned in a week. The table was the same table. The approach was not. It was wider and emptier, and nothing had lit a drive inside it in the thirty-three days of log she had read, and nothing did tonight. Six principles and one word, *agreed*, a hundred and sixty times.

The watch with her was a Senior and a Deputy, and neither said more than the watch asked. They looked at her when she was not looking at them. She let them. They had been handed a name with nothing under it, and they were doing their own finding out, by the only means a watch has, and it was not hers to hurry.

A few minutes past midnight the hatch behind her had opened, and after about as long as it takes to read a bill it had closed again. She had not turned round. An officer who has woken at one hour for thirty-three nights is owed the chance to look in and go back down without its being seen.

At four the morning watch came up. She wrote the close: the hour, *instruments in agreement throughout, nothing inside the approach*, and her initial. The middle watch before it had closed in the Chief's hand, and so had the thirty-two before that.

She had stood four hours of this log, and she put her initial under four hours.

:::pov emeka-lindahl

He slept from a little after midnight until the forenoon, which he had not done since the dead Starwarden's hull cleared.

At midnight he had been awake, as he had known he would be, and had gone up as far as the hatch. She was not at the desk. She was standing where the agreement log ran, with the Senior on one side of her and the Deputy on the other, reading. The bill was on its hook as he had written it. He read it from the hatch to be sure. After that there was nothing in the compartment that was his, and he went down.

The acknowledgement came in the first hour of his watch, eighteen hours after the sheet. That was as soon as the relay could carry anything up the line and back, and it meant the line office had not sat on it. He opened it at the desk. His sheet, as sent. The Line Captain's initial against the date. Under his own initial the three printed words and the space, and against the space no query and no note.

He had told her how the line office would read a blank. The line office had read it, and had asked nothing.

He filed the acknowledgement with the sheet and stood his watch, one in the day. At the turn the Senior off the middle came by the desk, for no reason the bill gave, and said that the Starwarden had read the agreements. All of them. He said it as a report.

Lindahl said that he had seen the log. He did not tell the Senior what to make of it. Nobody had told him.
