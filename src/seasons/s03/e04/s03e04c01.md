:::pov lukas-akande

The clock over the near door said sixteen minutes past three, and the last time he had read it, it had said twelve.

Akande did not move. He looked at the bench first, because that was the order: the bench, then the three recesses in it, then the three practice sources in the recesses, then the seal on each. Grey tape, a number, his own initial across the join from midnight. One. Two. Three. The count was correct. He got up and read the numbers against the custody line with his finger under each, which he had not done since he was a Deputy, and they were the numbers.

Nothing had happened. He was as sure of that as he was of the floor.

He could not say he had seen it not happen. He remembered twelve past, and the cold in his feet, and thinking that the practice bench had by now been carried nowhere for nine days with very great care. Then it was sixteen past. He had not got up. He had not turned his head. He had been sitting upright on the bin with his hands on his knees, and somewhere in that he had not been there.

Four minutes.

The shelter kept no eye on the bench but the watch's. That was the Survey's rule for the membrane, where a recorder would have been one more instrument to bring home and audit, and the rehearsal ran by the membrane's rules or it rehearsed nothing. So the middle custody watch was stood alone, by a Principal, because the Chief had written the bill that way in the first week: three Principals among fourteen names, the hard hours to the officers who had stood the most of them. He had thought it a sound bill. He thought so now.

He sat down again and took up the log.

The custody line had a form. *Hour. Seals. Count. Attended throughout.* Then the initial. He had written it on four benches before this one, on other floors, for other Chiefs, and he could have written it tonight in his sleep, which was a phrase he looked at for a moment and put away.

It would pass. That was the plain fact of it. At six tomorrow evening the department would walk the bench to the far door, and the Survey would take the three practice sources apart on a table and ask each one whether ten days of being carried had changed it. They would say no. They were sealed lumps doing their one thing; four minutes of a man's absence was not a thing they could have recorded if they had wished to. The Survey would receive the carry. The department would have its real bench by the end of the week, and a Chief Ranger a few months in the rank would have her first paper with nothing wrong on it.

And there was nobody to tell. He considered that honestly, since he was considering the rest. The watch after his came on at four. The Senior who would relieve him would read the seals and the count and find them right, because they were right.

He had been on the lists nineteen years. He knew what a Chief did with a name that had broken her first carry. The names were hers to put on the roster; they were as much hers to take off. There was a list behind the list, and thirteen days to the hull, which was time enough to bring up a Principal who would stay awake.

He wrote the hour. *Seals: intact at 0316. Count: correct at 0316.* Then, where the form said *attended throughout*, he put one stroke through the two words, so that they could still be read, and wrote beneath them.

*0312 to 0316: officer of the watch not attending. Custody not certified for the interval. L.A., Principal.*

It looked very small. He read it back for a word that claimed more than he knew, and for one that claimed less, and found neither, and that was all the comfort there was in it.

At four the Senior came on, read the seals, read the count, and read the line. She read it twice. She did not say anything, and he was grateful for that, and went to his bunk and lay on it with his eyes open until the forenoon.

:::pov ingrid-halvorsen

The Chief Ranger came to the far door herself, at a little after eight, with the log under her arm.

Halvorsen had not expected her until the tenth evening. The far door was the Survey's; it was a table, two of her Seniors, and a clock that had been told nothing about the rehearsal. For nine days the department had been a light at the other end of the staging floor and a summary each morning that said what the summary always said. She had read nine of them. She had read fifty of them, five winters' worth, from five other departments, and they had all said it.

"Chief."

"Chief Ranger."

"Custody is not certified for four minutes of the ninth night. Twelve past three to sixteen past. The officer of the watch entered it himself, at the time." She opened the log at the page and turned it to face the table, and took her hand away. "The carry is broken. I am beginning it again at midnight. There are thirteen days to the hull, and a carry is ten."

Halvorsen read the line. A stroke through two printed words, and three lines in a square hand under it. Under those, an initial she knew by now, with nothing struck and nothing added.

She read the seals and the count as well, because they were there.

"The seals were intact," she said.

"At sixteen past. Yes."

"Then I will tell you the Survey's side of it, since you should have it before you spend ten days." She did not hurry. "Those are sealed sources. Nothing that can be done in four minutes to a sealed source leaves the seal as it was. My Seniors would have opened them tomorrow night and found them unchanged, and I would have received the carry. If this line were not in the log I would have received it. I do not think you are carrying a fault. I think you are carrying an honest man who shut his eyes."

"So do I," the Chief Ranger said.

"You could ask me to receive it as it stands. The requirement is the Survey's. It is mine to state."

"I could." She did not go on, and Halvorsen waited to see whether she would be asked, and was not.

It was an odd thing to be left holding. The Survey asked a ten-day carry of every department before it handed over the bench, and had asked it since the second revisit, in a sentence Halvorsen had always thought complete: *custody unbroken, the log whole.* She had meant that the two were one thing. A log was whole when it showed the custody unbroken. She had received five such logs. Each had a custody line for every quarter-hour of ten days, and each line had said *attended throughout*, in the hands of people she did not know, on watches stood alone in a shelter with no eye in it.

She had received them. That was the word on her own sheet, over her own initial, five times.

She looked at the stroke through the two words. It was the first line in six winters of custody logs that she could say had been written by somebody who would have written the other thing if the other thing had been so. She had no means of saying that about the lines above it. She had no means of saying it about five winters.

That was not a reading. She set it down where she set such things, and it did not sit quite still.

"I do not receive the ninth day," she said. "The Survey's sentence has two halves and you have brought me one of them. I will not waive the carry."

"No," said the Chief Ranger. She did not sound relieved, and she did not sound otherwise. She turned the log round again and closed it. "Midnight, then. By your clock or ours?"

"There is only the one clock at this door."

"Then by that."

She went back down the floor with the log under her arm. Halvorsen watched her go as far as the light, and then told her Seniors that the table would not be wanted tomorrow, and that it would be wanted in ten days, and did not tell them what to make of it.

:::pov lukas-akande

He was sent for at nine, and went in his shell with the collar fastened, as a man goes who expects to be handed a paper.

The department's office was a partition and a desk. The Chief was standing at the desk with the log open, and she was reading it, not at his page but some days back, and she finished the page she was on before she looked up. She was a good deal younger than he was. He had stopped noticing that in the second week, and noticed it now.

"Principal."

"Chief."

"I have told the Survey the carry is broken. It begins again at midnight, as day one." She turned to the ninth night and put a finger beside his line without touching it. "This stands as you wrote it. I have initialled under it. It goes to the Archive with the rest."

"Yes, Chief."

"Is there anything in it you would write differently this morning?"

He had been asked that by officers who meant *would you like to take it back*. It did not sound like that. It sounded like a count being checked. "No, Chief."

"Then here is the other entry, so that you have read it before the watch does."

It was under her initial, in her hand, and it was two lines. *Bill amended from the midnight watch: no custody watch in this department is stood alone. The bill was the Chief's.*

He read it twice. He was not sure, the first time, that he had read the second sentence correctly.

"The bill was sound, Chief," he said. He gave it to her squarely, since she ought to have it from the man it concerned. "Three Principals, and the middle hours to each in turn. I have stood a watch alone on four benches. I did not lose four minutes because of the bill."

"You lost them on a watch that one person could lose them on. I wrote that watch." She said it as flatly as she had said the hour to him on his first day. "On the membrane there will be a bench that cannot be carried twice. I would rather have found this out on a floor."

"It doubles the custody bill. The department has the audit to drill and the window besides."

"It does. So I have taken a line of it." She turned the bill round on the desk. It had been written out fresh, and there was a line on it that had not been there yesterday, at the foot, and the word against the line was *Chief*. She had given herself the morning watch, four to eight, with a Deputy.

He found his own name. The middle watch, midnight to four. It was where it had been.

He had not expected that. He stood looking at it, and it did not change.

Against his name, in the second column, was Deputy Sarr. Sarr was one of the two he had been taking through the custody count each forenoon since the third day, at the practice table, in hours that were his own and on no bill, because a Deputy who could not read a seal number against a line without moving her lips was his to mend and nobody else's. He had told no one. There was nothing to tell; it was a Principal's ordinary work, and it had cost him a few hours' sleep a day for six days, which was not a thing a man of nineteen years reported.

Sarr would be four hours a night at his elbow now, with a bench in front of her and a custody line every quarter. He could not have drawn up a better table to teach her at. His forenoons had been handed back to him, and nobody had said one word to him about how he managed a Deputy.

"One question," the Chief said. "What have the nine days cost you that is not in the log?"

"Nothing, Chief. I am fine."

She looked at him for about as long as it takes to read a line and decide not to initial it. Then she nodded, once, and did not ask it again.

"Midnight," she said.

He went out. In the passage he did the sum, because he could not leave a sum alone. The bill had been written out fresh, in ink that was dry. She had come back from the far door at half past eight. The log had gone up to her at the turn of the day, at four, with his line in it. Between four and eight a person might read a log, and rewrite a bill, and find out which two Deputies a Principal had been sitting with in his own forenoons, if she had not known it already.

He did not see how she could have known it already. He had not been asked for his forenoons and they were in no log.

He put it with the things he could not show, and went to find Sarr, who would need to be told she had a watch.

:::pov ingrid-halvorsen

The second carry came to the far door on the evening of its tenth day, with two days in hand.

Her Seniors took the practice sources apart on the table and asked each one whether ten days had changed it, and each one had not. Halvorsen read the log while they worked. Sixty watches, two initials under every line. *Attended throughout*, in fourteen hands and one more: the morning watch had closed for ten days on a Deputy's initial and, under it, on the one she had seen below a stroke on the ninth night of the other carry.

Nothing was struck. She read for it, line by line, as she had never read a custody log before, and noticed that she was reading for it, and that a log with nothing struck in it could not show her any more than the last five had.

It was not quite so. She went back and found the middle watch. *L.A., Principal*, and a Deputy's initial beside it, round and careful, ten nights. The same man, on the same hours. The Chief Ranger had put him back where he had shut his eyes, and had put her own name on the bill to make the numbers come out.

She had the other log too. It had been brought down with this one, unasked, the nine days of the broken carry bound in front of the ten days of the whole one, to go to the Archive as one book.

Halvorsen wrote the Survey's line on her own sheet. *Carry of ten days, second: custody unbroken, the log whole. Received.* She put her initial to it.

It was the sixth time she had written the word, and the form was the same form. She looked at it a little longer than the other five had needed.

Then she sent word down the floor that the department might draw the bench in the morning.
