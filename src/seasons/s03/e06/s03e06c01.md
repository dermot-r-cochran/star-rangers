:::pov emeka-lindahl

The Starwarden was on his knees under the chair with the column key, and had been for some minutes, when Lindahl brought up the stores return.

He did not ask. The office was the Starwarden's, and what a man did under his own chair was his business. He set the return on the desk, squared, and waited.

"It goes down to here." The Starwarden's voice came up from the deck. "And up to here, which is where I have had it. I have put it in the middle."

He got out from under it and stood, which took a moment. He was the tallest officer the post had held in Lindahl's nine years, by a good deal. The chair at his height had been a thing the watch pointed out to new arrivals. In the middle of its travel it looked like any chair.

"Somebody is coming who will sit in that," the Starwarden said. "I don't know who. I don't know how tall."

"No, Starwarden."

"So I have set it for the average." He looked at it. "Which is the one height I can be certain was set for nobody."

Lindahl considered that it was not his chair.

The Starwarden knelt again, with a grunt, and ran the column back up to where it had been. "There. Whoever it is will know at a glance that it was mine and not meant for them. They can alter it without wondering what we took them for."

The order had come two days ago, on the relay, in the morning's papers. *To a ship*, on another line: second to her captain, the rank styled Starwarden First Officer, a second ring. Effective on the stores hull, which was due in four days. He had read it at the watch desk and handed it to Lindahl to read, as he handed him most things, and had said only that it was sooner than he had thought. Two years and seven months. It was not a short time for the post, and not a long one.

The charts were still up. Six sheets on the bulkhead over the desk: the approach, in the Starwarden's own plotting, which was small and upright and unlike anybody else's aboard.

"Shall I have those struck, Starwarden?"

"Leave them. They are of the approach, and the approach is staying." He hung the column key on its hook. "And they are in my hand. Nobody will mistake them for the post's."

He sat down, at his own height, and read the stores return, and initialled it.

"I have been thinking what I hand you," he said.

"The post, Starwarden."

"Yes. In what state."

Lindahl waited.

"When I came, you gave me a sheet. Instruments, watches, stores, people, open matters. It was a good sheet. I should like to give you one."

"There is no form for it."

"I can write without a form."

"Then it would be a letter," Lindahl said. "And I should have to decide where to file it." He gave him the position plainly, since it was a kind offer and ought to be declined with its reason. "I have had the watch nine years, Starwarden. I stood the forenoon this morning. There is nothing you could put on a sheet about the instruments or the people that I did not see myself before breakfast."

"You know the watch. I do not doubt it." The Starwarden turned the pen over. "You will be answering for the rest."

"I have done it three times."

"And nobody stated it to you then, either."

"No."

It seemed to trouble him. Lindahl could not see why it should. A sheet was for an officer who came through the lock knowing nothing. He had not come through the lock. He had been here, each time, and the morning after had been the same morning with one fewer at the mess table.

The Starwarden let it go. He did that well; it was one of the things Lindahl had liked in him from the start. He had come aboard with his bag on his shoulder and found the sheet waiting, and had signed the receiving line right there at the lock before the bag was down, and said that if the Chief said it was so then it was so. Lindahl had taken that as it was meant. He had never had cause to think again about it.

"One more," the Starwarden said. "The middle watch. It is mine on your bill. Who stands it when I have gone?"

"It will be covered."

"By a Senior?"

"It will be covered, Starwarden."

A pause. The bill was the Chief's. A Starwarden did not write it and did not ask twice how it was filled, and this one had never needed telling.

"Very good," he said.

He stood his last four middle watches as he had stood the rest, at the desk, with the line's papers, looking up at the agreement log when it occurred to him. On the last he closed at four, in his small upright hand, *instruments in agreement throughout, nothing inside the approach*, and went down to finish his bag.

The hull was at the lock by six. Lindahl went with him as far as the inner hatch, which was as far as it was usual to go.

The Starwarden stopped on the sill. He had his bag on his shoulder. He looked at the deck plate under his boots as if it had a number on it.

"I signed your sheet about here," he said.

"You did."

"I never asked you whether it was right."

"It was right."

"I know it was. That is not quite what I mean." He seemed to look for the rest of it. Then he shook his head, at himself, and shifted the bag. "Chief."

"Starwarden."

He went through. He had to stoop for the outer hatch; he always had.

Lindahl stood until the lock showed clear, and then went up to the watch desk and wrote the line. *Post held by the Chief Ranger pending a Starwarden.* The hour, by the post's clock. His initial under it.

The first time, years ago, he had expected to feel the post arrive. It had not, then or since. The desk was the desk. Six instruments agreed with one another, and ninety seconds later they agreed again.

He had not followed what the man meant on the sill. He turned it over once during the forenoon and could make nothing of it. A sheet had been stated, and received, and had been right. He did not see what else there was in it to ask.

At the turn a Deputy came to the desk with the galley sheet and asked whether the dead Starwarden's number should come off it from today or from the week's end.

The hull was not five hours out.

"From today," Lindahl said.

He had never found out who started it. It was there each time by the first turn of the watch, like the summary, and nobody could be shown to have said it first.

At midnight he went up. The bill had a line that read *Starwarden*, and no Starwarden. He did not write his name against it. The bill was correct as it stood; the log would say who had been at the desk.

He reported at the line office the next forenoon, between hulls, with an hour and a half before the one that would take him on.

Wender received him standing. She had met him twice, at the turn of the summer rotations, and had thought him a careful officer who was easy in his post. She had not yet decided whether the second thing counted against the first. He stooped in the hatch, and said, "Line Captain," and she said, "Starwarden," and that was the last day anybody on her line would call him so.

The business was short. His order, which she had initialled the week before. The date of his clearing. The post, held by its Chief Ranger pending; she had told the desk that kept the lists the evening before, in one line, and the desk had not yet answered. Desks did not hurry over a command.

Then she asked the one thing she had standing to ask.

"Is there anything about the post that is not in the summaries?"

"A good deal, Line Captain." He said it without smiling. "None of it is mine to state. It was the Chief's from ten past seven yesterday."

"You held it two years and seven months."

"I can tell you how I left it. I left it the way I found it, as near as I could. I can't tell you how it is. I have been off it a day."

It was a correct answer. She found it a slightly irritating one, and waited, since he had the look of a man with a second half.

"I offered him a statement," he said. "Before I went. The same sheet he gave me when I came: instruments, watches, stores, people. He said there was no form. I thought that was the Chief being the Chief, so I went through the line's forms that night. He was right. There is a sheet for a Chief to state a post to a Starwarden, with a line under it for her to receive it. There is none the other way. Nobody states a post to the Chief. He takes it as it stands."

"He has the watch. He knows the state of it."

"That is what he said." The Starwarden looked at his hands. "I signed his sheet on the sill, Line Captain. With my bag on my shoulder. I meant it as a courtesy, and he took it as one. It was most of a year before I sat down and read what the line says. It says that I had the post as he stated it on that date. Anything found wrong from before it would have been his, and none of it mine."

"Was anything found?"

"Nothing. He keeps a sound post." He let out a breath. "But I had it from him with his word under my feet for two years and seven months. And yesterday he had it from me with nothing. He has had it four times that way, and I do not think he has once noticed."

Wender looked at that for a moment.

"The form is the line's," she said. "It is mine to amend. I could have a receiving line put in for the Chief."

"You could."

"You do not advise it."

"I should ask him first whether he wants one. I don't believe it has crossed his mind that there isn't. If the line sent down a new form, he would work out why, and then he would have to decide whether three Starwardens had been taking something from him." He shook his head. "We had not. I had not. I only stood where it was easy to stand."

It was a position, and he had given it in order. She did not thank him for it; it was not that kind of thing.

"Then the officer who follows you will be given his sheet," she said, "and will sign it or not."

"Yes."

"And you leave no word for that officer."

"We don't, Line Captain." He seemed surprised to be asked. "It is theirs. I should only be describing a post I am not on."

His hull was called. He stooped out through the hatch, and she did not see him again; a First Officer on another line's ship is another Line Captain's.

She had thought him careless. For about an hour, across the business of the afternoon, she went on thinking it. A Starwarden who signed for six instruments and twenty people before he had put his bag down had signed for nothing he had seen.

The evening's summaries came up at the usual hour, one from each post on the line. She read them standing, in the order of the line, nearest first. The far one was last.

*Post held by the Chief Ranger pending a Starwarden. Instruments: six, in agreement. Watches: stood. Nothing inside the approach. E.L.*

It was nine hours old. Whatever it said had been so at a desk she was not standing at, in the judgement of a man whose face she would have had to think to recall. Against the date there was a space, and the space was hers. It had been hers every evening since the line was, for every post on it. She had the pen in her hand already. She had picked it up before she began to read, as she always did.

She looked at the pen.

Then she initialled the summary, since it was the only thing to be done with it, and set it on the others. She did not send for the forms. She did not write anything for whoever would be sent out to that post, there being nothing about it that she could have stated.
