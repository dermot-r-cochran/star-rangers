:::pov albercombe

The first thing wrong was that nobody had swept, and Albercombe would have denied noticing it.

Eden's bonded hall sends every empty cradle home swept. The last consignee's packing is lifted out of the corners, the clamp faces are wiped, and the cradle goes back on the tug as clean as a thing can be that has carried ceramic feedstock or seed stock or somebody's optical blanks across open space. Halcyon's hall does the same for Eden's. Neither hall's standing orders say so. No office would care, because an empty cradle is tared wherever it lands and a handful of packing weighs what it weighs. It is a thing two floors have done for each other for as long as anyone on either of them has worked there, and Albercombe knew it the way he knew which of Eden's lifts ran slow, which is to say that he had never been told and could not have said when he learned.

The row of Halcyon empties by the hall's outbound door had packing in the corners.

He had not come about that. He had come about a lens grinder on Deck 4 whose cradle had come off the tug at nine in the morning and been signed over to him at a quarter to six in the evening, and who wanted to know where it had spent the day. It was a contract matter. It read exactly as boring as it was. Albercombe had said so to the Senior Sergeant, and to the lift, and had gone down to the dock ring expecting a queue.

He found a bay. It was a stretch of floor along the hall's west wall, between a charging rank and a pillar, and it held nine cradles with Halcyon's bond seal on them and nothing else. No marking on the deck. No line in the hall's floor plan, which he had on his slate, because he was the kind of officer who brought the floor plan.

"What is that," he asked the shift chief.

"That's not anything."

"It has nine cradles in it."

"They're waiting for the instrument."

"The instrument's standing idle. I can see it from here." He could. The measuring bed was empty and its one light was steady. "Who designated the bay."

"Nobody designated it. It's not a bay." The shift chief was not being difficult; she was being accurate, in the way of a person who has been accurate about this for three weeks and is tired of it. "The crews like a second look at a Halcyon seal before it goes on the bed. After Halcyon's business at the close. You'll have read about it."

He had read about it. A loading supervisor on Halcyon, a stolen bond stamp, thirty-one crossings audited. It had been charged on Halcyon's own deck and had been, in the two words he reserved for the best kind of case, not Eden's.

"And the hall's log shows these as."

"Not arrived. A cradle arrives when it's certified. That's when the stamp goes on." She shrugged. "It's always been when the stamp goes on."

So the lens grinder's cradle had spent eight hours and three quarters in a bay that was not a bay, on a floor that was entitled to work its cradles in any order it liked, and the hall's ledger was perfect. He wrote that down, with distaste. There was no offence in it anywhere. There was not even a rule.

The sheet was on the crew board, between a rota and a notice about a retirement. Three columns of figures and a heading: *Halcyon certifying light.* Twenty-seven lines, each a crossing by its number, each with Halcyon's figure, Eden's figure and the difference, and the difference always ran the same way. The top line he knew. Everybody on Eden's dock ring knew the top line.

He had Lucene-9000 check Eden's column against Eden's metrology log from where he stood. Every Eden figure on the sheet was Eden's figure. Lucene-9000 added, unasked, that it could not check the Halcyon column, since those figures were held at Halcyon, and that the heading was on a separate strip of paper. He had not seen that. He lifted the corner of the strip with a thumbnail, as a man lifts a thing he may later have to say he did not damage, and read what was under it against the light.

*Eden reading heavy.*

The same figures. Somebody on Halcyon had written them out as a fault in Eden's instrument, and the sheet had crossed in a tug crew's pouch, and somebody on Eden had gummed a strip over the accusation and turned it round. He could not find the hand that had done it and did not look hard. Changing a heading is not a crime on any deck he knew of.

He stood in front of the board for some time. There was a form of words for this, and he had been using it for years with a clear conscience: a thing that began on another habitat was not Eden's, and went upstairs with two words on it. He could not write them. Half of it was a bay on his own dock ring. The other half he could not reach, and it was the half with the figures in it.

He said the word he says about once a week. Then he took a copy of the sheet, strip and all, up to the Superintendent, who read it once and wrote four sentences, and sent it where a bureau sends the part of a case it cannot finish.

:::pov larsen

Eden's referral came up with the sheet attached and a note from the Inspector that ran to nine words, three of them about the lifts.

Larsen wrote to both Commissioners the same afternoon. The Eden letter said what the bureau had sent him and what he would do about it. The Halcyon letter said that a paper on Eden's dock ring appeared to have begun on Halcyon's, that the task force's Field Observer would fly both bonded halls on the dates attached, and that he would be glad of anything Halcyon's bureau held.

Duarte's office answered in a day, helpfully, the way that office always did. Halcyon's bureau held fourteen complaints. Shippers, all of them, in the seven weeks since the quarter's close, all saying one thing: that Eden's hall was sitting on Halcyon cradles for the better part of a shift before it would certify them. Halcyon's bureau had taken each complaint seriously and closed each the same way, because the delay, if there was one, was on Eden's deck, and a bureau's reach ends at its own habitat's wall. Fourteen closures, each correct. Nobody had sent them up, because no single one of them was a case running past Halcyon's edge. Each was a grievance against a neighbour, and a bureau has nowhere to file those.

Under the closures was the sheet from Halcyon's own crew board.

He laid it beside Eden's. The same twenty-seven lines, the same crossings in the same order, the same three columns. Halcyon's copy had no strip. Its heading was the one Albercombe had read against the light.

So on one floor twenty-seven true figures said that Eden's instrument could not be trusted, and the crews there had taken to running every Eden-bound cradle twice. On the other the same twenty-seven said that Halcyon's hall was still sending cradles out short, seven weeks after its own bureau had charged the man who did it, and the crews there had found a stretch of wall to stand the cradles against while they looked at the seals. Each habitat's ledger was perfect. Each bureau had its half, and each half read as the other habitat behaving badly.

He had been looking at the top line since he opened the envelope and had been reading round it.

*HC-2290. Halcyon 1,904.0. Eden 1,907.4. Difference 3.4.* Beside it, where every other line had a crossing number, was the reference of a file on Orbital Five-O's own record. The reference was right. The figures were right. They were his, copied without an error from a page he had filed under his own name in the deep of the winter, with every measurement appended and no account of them offered.

He had filed it empty, and both floors had read it, since the record was open and anyone could. Then each of them had done what a floor does with an empty line, and put a cause on it.

:::pov alala

She flew Eden's hall first, because Eden's letter had gone first.

The heads came up, and the pointing, and one dock hand who had seen her in the autumn told another that it was all right, she was filed. Then the forgetting, on schedule. She took the rib above the west wall and let the subsystem keep the tallies.

A Halcyon cradle came off the tug at the hall's inbound door and was carried, not to the bed, but to the wall. It stood there. Two of the crew looked at its seal, one with a hand lamp, the way a person looks at a thing when she has been told there is something to see and has not been told what. Then they left it and worked other cradles: Eden's own, a pair from one of the other habitats, which went straight from the door to the bed and were clamped, oscillated, timed and stamped in the time it takes to say so. The Halcyon cradle went onto the bed at the end of the shift, and read whatever it read, and was stamped, and from that moment it had arrived.

Over two shifts she counted twelve Halcyon cradles. The shortest stood against the wall for three hours and ten minutes and the longest for nine and a quarter. The hall's log, which the Commander had sent her so that she would know what the paper said, had every one of them arriving at the minute it was certified. The paper was not wrong. It recorded an arrival, and the wall was not an arrival. Nothing on Eden recorded the wall except the cradles, and now her.

The subsystem observed, unasked, that a cradle held nine hours and then certified is certified correctly. She agreed. It was the second opinion she had expected.

Halcyon's hall she flew two days later, announced, banded, and looked at for about as long. It is a brighter floor than Eden's, and louder. The trouble on it was at the bed. A cradle bound for Eden was clamped and run, and the figure went to the ledger and the seal went on. Then, before the carrier came, it was run again. The second figure went nowhere. A slip came off the instrument and a loader folded it into the pouch at her belt, and the cradle went out to the tug with one certified mass and a private one.

One cradle in three, by her count. Only the ones for Eden.

She could not read the slips from the rib and did not need to. What she could see was what the crews did with their eyes, which was the thing she had been watching people do, from above, for the whole of her service. They looked at her once. They looked at the sheet on the crew board every time they passed it. The board was by the door to the wash-room, and nobody on that floor went through the door without turning their head, and some of them stopped. She timed it, since it was there to time. A loader passing the sheet gave it, at the median, forty seconds. That was on the fourth day of her count, of a sheet that had been up for weeks.

She had thought a good deal, since Eden's mind had asked her its question, about what ninety seconds were. She had not thought to wonder what the other thing looked like, the thing people do not forget on schedule. It was a list of numbers with a sentence over the top. It was not even a long sentence.

Each evening, at the end of first shift, a woman with the flash of Halcyon's metrology office at her collar came out to the board with a stylus. On two of the four evenings she added a line. She did it openly, with the floor going past her, and initialled what she added. Alala noted the initial as a mark on a public board and put no name to it; the Commander could read a rota as well as she could.

She filed what she had. Twelve cradles and a wall. One run in three repeated. Forty seconds. A hand that initialled. She added the note the Commander would want, which the subsystem had worked out from the plate on the instrument's housing while she watched the board: the calibrated spring is re-certified on a count of runs and not on a date, and at the rate Halcyon's floor was now running it, the instrument would come due nineteen days early and stand idle while it was done.

Her own five files disagreed about her in five house styles, and the body that could say which was right had declined to. Nobody had ever put that on a board. She noted that it had not occurred to anyone to, and was not sure what the note was for, and filed it with the rest.

:::pov larsen

He asked Calloway's office for the quarter's reconciliation so far, which he was entitled to, and for one extract from it: every bonded crossing from Halcyon to Eden since the close, both figures.

Two hundred and twelve crossings. He did the sum himself, on paper, because he wanted to have done it.

On twenty-six of them Eden's figure stood above Halcyon's by three hundred grams or more. Those were the sheet, line for line, under the one that was his. On twenty-nine, Eden's figure stood below Halcyon's by as much. Those were on no board anywhere. The other hundred and fifty-seven sat inside three hundred grams one way or the other, and nothing in the whole run differed by more than seven hundred, which is well inside what two instruments certified to a part in four thousand are allowed to do to each other on an ordinary day. They had been doing it every day since the Compact had instruments. Nobody had ever written it out, because it was not a finding. It was what agreement looks like when you measure it.

Nothing on the sheet was false. He checked that twice, with some care, because he would have preferred a forgery. A forgery goes down to a bureau and is charged.

What was on the sheet was a table of observations and, over the top of it, in three words on either floor, an inference. He knew the form. He had been taught it on a fold-side watch, by an officer whose name he could still have given: four lines for what the instruments did, and a fifth where the Rangers stopped and somebody's opinion began. He had spent a career keeping the fifth line clean, and had resigned, and had gone on keeping it clean. In the deep of the winter he had kept it so clean that he had filed a page with nothing on it at all.

And he knew what a man does with an empty fifth line when he knows the dock. He had stood at 22-Low at a quarter past three, two winters ago, in uniform, finding that out about himself.

Alala's note about the spring was on the desk. So was the initial. He did not need the rota. A bonded hall's instrument has a keeper, and the keeper's initial is on every certificate the hall issues.

He wrote to Commissioner Duarte that he would come onto Halcyon's deck on the third day following, to the bonded freight hall, at the end of first shift, in uniform; that he would ask the keeper of Halcyon's instrument about a sheet on the hall's crew board, and would ask her nothing else; that Halcyon's bureau was welcome to stand beside him while he did; and that he was bringing a second sheet with him, which he attached, so that nobody would be surprised by it.

It had twenty-nine lines.
