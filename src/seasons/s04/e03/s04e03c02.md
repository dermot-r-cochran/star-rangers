:::pov larsen

Halcyon's bureau sent a sergeant, who stood by the wash-room door with his hands behind him and the face of a man detailed to witness a conversation about arithmetic.

The metrologist was at the board when Larsen came onto the floor, at the end of first shift, as his letter had said and as Alala's tallies had said she would be. She had the stylus in her hand. She looked at the jacket, and then up at the rib where the raven was, and then at the leaf he was carrying, and did not put the stylus down.

"Your letter said you'd ask about one sheet, Commander."

"It did. I've brought another."

"I know what's on the other." She took a folded leaf from her tunic and opened it against the board beside her own. Two columns in the same hand. The left was the twenty-six. The right ran three lines longer. "I keep the bed. You didn't suppose I'd tally one side of it."

He had supposed exactly that, for most of a week, and had the grace to revise it without saying so.

"Then you know the heading is not true of your own instrument. If Eden reads heavy on twenty-six, it reads light on twenty-nine."

"It reads inside tolerance on two hundred and twelve. I know what the whole table says. It says both beds are sound." She said it flatly, as a person states a figure she has had for a long time. "It said that in the winter. I ran Eden's reference mass on my bed with Eden's office watching, and they ran mine on theirs. Sound, both, to a fifth of a kilogram. Then you filed a page that says one of the two figures was wrong once, and nothing says which."

"That is what the measurements support."

"I've read it more times than you have. Sound, and wrong once, and nobody can say whose. Do you know how that reads on a floor? It reads as an even chance that the thing they clamp their work to tells lies. They came and asked me which. Twenty-three years I have kept that bed, and I stood there with your page and had nothing to tell them."

"So you told them Eden's."

"I told them nothing. I put up figures. Every one of them is on a closed record this office is entitled to hold."

"Under a heading."

"Under a heading." She did not look away from him. "A floor can't work under a blank. You can. You have an office to keep it in and a Governor to send it to. They have a bed, and nine cradles an hour, and me."

It was the best case anyone had put to him against the page, and it had been put by someone with every reason to resent it. He found he could not answer it by saying that the page was true. She had not said it was false.

"It won't say which," he said. "I had eight days and both your offices, and I could not make it say."

"No."

"Yours doesn't either. The left column, by itself. It only looks as though it does."

She was quiet. Behind her a carrier went by with a cradle on it, bound for the bed, and the two loaders walking it turned their heads to the board as they passed, both of them, and then to her, and then to him.

"The spring comes due nineteen days early," she said. "I had that before your bird did. They run it twice because of what I put up, and it wears the one thing on this floor I'm here to look after. I'd have taken the sheet down last week if I had known what to put in its place."

"I can't give you a heading."

"I didn't think you'd come to."

"What I can do is put the whole run on my record, under the page it belongs to. Two hundred and twelve crossings, both figures, my name. Nothing over the top of it."

"They'll read less of that."

It was true, and he did not contest it.

She turned to the board. He had expected her to take the sheet down, and she did not. She drew one line through the heading, so that it could still be read under the line, and wrote her initial and the date beside the stroke; an entry on a metrology record is never erased, and she corrected this one as she would have corrected any other. Then she began to copy in the right-hand column from the leaf, crossing by crossing, in the space beneath, with the floor going past her.

The top line she left alone. It had no heading over it now and nothing beside it. It was the only line on the sheet that had never been hers.

He stood until she had written the twenty-ninth. What he thought about, standing, was a dock ring at a quarter past three, and a notice with its fifth line left empty by somebody who could not support what they knew. He had read that emptiness as a door left open for him. He had been wrong about why it was empty and right that it was a door. It had not occurred to him, until a metrologist said so across a crew board, that he had left one himself, on an open record, where any floor could reach it.

The sergeant asked, when she had finished, whether there was anything in it for the bureau.

"No," Larsen said. "It has been corrected."

The sergeant wrote one word and went to his supper.

:::pov alala

She stayed four more days, because the dates in the annex said she would.

The sheet with the struck heading held the floor longer than the old one had. On the first day a loader passing it gave it, at the median, a minute and ten seconds. They read the stroke. They read the initial beside it, which they knew, and the date, which was yesterday's. Several of them put a finger on the right-hand column and ran it down. One man read the whole board with his lips moving and then went and stood by the bed for a while without a cradle.

On the second day it was thirty seconds. On the fourth it was nine.

She set that beside the forty seconds from the week before and looked at the two figures for some time. A short list with a sentence over it had held a floor for weeks. A long list with no sentence was being forgotten very nearly as fast as she was. The subsystem offered, unasked, that a balanced table contains less to act on than an unbalanced one, and that attention follows what can be acted on. She thought that was probably so, and did not think it was all of it. She put both tallies in the file and neither opinion.

One thing on the board did not go the way of the rest. Where a reader's eye stopped at all, on the fourth day, it stopped at the top: one line, two figures, three kilograms and four hundred grams, and a file reference where a heading might have been. They looked at it the way the crew on Eden had looked at a seal with a hand lamp. Then they went through the wash-room door.

The second runs she counted every shift. One cradle in three, on the day the Commander came. One in four, two days after. One in five on the last day she flew, and those were all the work of the same two crews, who ran their cradles twice, folded the slips into their pouches, and did not look at the board at all.

Nobody had told them to stop. Nobody on that floor had the standing to, except the woman with the stylus, and she had said what she had to say on the board and gone back to her instrument. Alala watched her, on the last evening, take a slip that a loader held out to her, read it, and hand it back.

She filed the count as a count. Then she left the hall the way she had come, obviously.

:::pov dorian-calloway

The close reached Calloway in the middle of a quarter, which meant it arrived as a letter and not as a line, and he had to read it as one.

It split the way Larsen's closes split. Eden's half went down to Eden's bureau: a stretch of floor that held cradles the hall's ledger had not yet agreed were there, lawful, logged nowhere, and nobody's order. Halcyon's half went down to Halcyon's: a sheet on a crew board, compiled from records its author was entitled to hold, true in every figure, and corrected by her in her own hand before the bureau had been asked for an opinion. The bureau had found no offence. Calloway read that line twice. There was the word.

He had been offered it in the winter. Duarte's office had suggested, helpfully, that Eden's figure could be *corrected* to Halcyon's, and he had not liked how much he wanted that, and had left it alone. It had turned up after all, nine weeks late and on Halcyon's own floor. Nobody had corrected a figure. The figures stood exactly where two instruments had put them. A metrologist had corrected three words.

The seam was the Governor's, and Larsen had written it as he wrote everything he was not comfortable with, shorter than usual. *An unresolved reading on this task force's record was read on two floors and given a cause on each. The record was accurate and it was open. I have appended the full run of paired figures from the same two instruments since, under my name, without comment. The reading is unresolved. I do not expect that to change, and I no longer expect it to stay where I put it.*

Under that was a recommendation section, and it was empty, which from Larsen was a statement about whose business the rest was.

Calloway sat with it. He had sent that reading up to the Governor on the quarter's last afternoon with no covering note, and had been privately pleased with himself for it, on the reasoning that an absence cannot ambush anyone. He still thought the reasoning sound. Nobody had been ambushed. The absence had simply been borrowed, by people with a use for it, and put to work on two docks, while every office that held the page went on holding it correctly.

His own office had held more than the page. The reconciliation laid the habitats' ledgers side by side once a quarter and had done so since before he had the post. Every pair of figures on that sheet had crossed his desk in a column, and so had the twenty-nine that were not on it, and the hundred and fifty-seven that nobody would ever have troubled to write out. They went to five Commissioners and a Governor. They had never gone to a floor. Nobody had decided that. A floor is not an office, and the circulation list was a list of offices.

He could not put a cause on Larsen's line and had no wish to. Nobody, this time, had even suggested the Rangers, which he noted as a kind of progress. But the circulation of his own reconciliation was within his office's gift and needed nobody's decision. He added two lines to it: the metrology office at each end of a bonded route, the paired figures for that route, each quarter, as they stood. He did it the way he did everything that was his to do, without announcing it.

Then he forwarded Larsen's letter to the Governor. This time he wrote a covering note. It said where the table was posted.

:::pov albercombe

The lens grinder's complaint was still open, because Albercombe had forgotten to close it, and he went down to the dock ring to see what he would be closing.

The stretch of wall between the charging rank and the pillar had two cradles standing against it. The last time he stood here it had been nine.

"Nobody rescinded it," he said to the shift chief.

"Nobody could. It was never ordered." She had the look of a woman who has been waiting to say so. "It's still not a bay."

"It's less of one."

"Third shift still likes a look at the seals. First shift read the long sheet and stopped." She nodded at the crew board. The task force's table was up, four leaves of it, without a word over the top. Next to it the old sheet was still pinned where it had been, with its gummed strip, and under it somebody had added the other twenty-nine in pencil, not neatly. Nobody had taken anything down. "I'm not going to tell third shift what order to work a floor in. They'll come round, or they won't."

He found that he had no quarrel with it, which annoyed him. A hall may work its cradles in any order it likes. He wrote the disposition standing up, on the slate, against the pillar. *Closed. No party at fault. Delay arises from the order in which the hall worked its floor, which is the hall's to decide.* It was the second time since the winter that Eden's bureau had closed a complaint about a cradle with nobody to blame, and he resented the pattern on principle and could not fault either instance.

He could not write the two words on it either. He had looked at it from every side on the way down in the lift. Half of it had been Eden's, and the half had been a hand lamp, a gummed strip and a stretch of wall, and no part of that had come from Halcyon in anybody's pouch.

The Halcyon empties were in their row by the outbound door. Seven of them. He walked along the row, since he was there. Five had been swept; the clamp faces had been wiped, and there was nothing in the corners. Two had not, and had the last consignee's packing lying where it had been left.

There was no column for that. It was not evidence of anything. He looked at the nearer of the two for a while, and then at the floor, where there was nobody he knew by name at that hour, and then he reached in and lifted the packing out of the near corner and put it in his coat pocket, which was the nearest place.

He found it there at dinner and could not, for a moment, think what it was.
