At the foot of the hub lift on Antariksha there is a wall where the habitat posts what it wants read, and Larsen found his own letter on it.

He had not sent it to a wall. He had sent it where he sent all of them, to the Commissioner's office, in writing and in advance: the reference, the dates, and the standard annex stating that the task force's Field Observer would fly the decks of both cylinders. On four habitats that letter goes into an office and stays there. On Antariksha every public notice is posted, in every language the habitat uses, and somebody in the Commissioner's office had taken the view that a raven over the decks was public. So the letter had gone to the interpreters, and from the interpreters to the walls.

It ran to nine versions down one long sheet. He could read the last, which was his own. The eight above it were in scripts he knew by sight and not otherwise. A woman coming off the lift with a child on her hip stopped at the third and read it through, and then read the fifth, the way a person checks a sum by doing it again from the other end.

The case had not looked complicated from his desk. Antariksha's foundry draws glass fibre in the hub, where nothing weighs anything, and sells it by the reel with the length on the certificate. For as long as the trade had run, a consignee on another habitat had signed for the reel and believed the certificate. Since the winter the consignees on Halcyon had stopped believing things. Halcyon's bureau had charged a loading supervisor on its own deck at the turn of the year, with a stolen bond stamp and thirty-one audited crossings behind him, and a floor that has been robbed once takes to measuring. Halcyon's cable shops had measured thirty-eight reels of Antariksha fibre as they came in. Each had been certified at forty kilometres. Each had come up forty-four metres short, give or take the width of a hand.

Halcyon's bureau could not finish that. The reels had been wound on another habitat. Its Detective Superintendent wrote four sentences and sent it up, and Larsen wrote to both Commissioners the same afternoon.

Antariksha's bureau had answered within the day, in his language and four of its own. It had, as it happened, audited the foundry that quarter on an unrelated matter. Every kilogram of glass that had gone into the melt was accounted for, as fibre shipped, fibre in stock or cullet returned to the pot. Nothing was missing from Antariksha. The reels were short on Halcyon.

He had read that as he read most things, for the hand in it. Carelessness varies. A shortfall that is the same proportion on thirty-eight reels is a rule, and somebody keeps a rule. He had come expecting to find out who.

An interpreter was working along the wall with a roll of fresh notices, a man with the council's flash at his collar, taking down a water schedule in nine versions and putting up another. Larsen waited until he had finished the row.

"The annex gives a title," he said. "Field Observer. I'd be glad to know what the wall says for it."

The interpreter looked at the jacket and then at the sheet. He did not need to find the place. "*Sākṣī.*"

"In which of them."

"In every one of them, very nearly. Bengali softens it and Tamil spells it its own way. Nobody on any deck here needs it interpreted. It is one of perhaps forty words we could post once."

"And it means observer."

"It means witness." He said it without apology. "We had observer, Commander. We discussed it. In most of the eight it is the word for a person who has taken a seat to watch. Your letter says that she will see what is done in public and that what she sees goes on your record under her number. That is not somebody who has taken a seat."

Larsen read his own version again, at the bottom of the sheet. It said exactly that. He had written the sentence in the unit's first year and had sent it some sixty times since.

"I'm told people forget her," he said. "In about a minute and a half."

"Where they have not been told who she is, I expect they do," the interpreter said, and went on to the next wall.

:::pov alala

Antariksha is two cylinders turning opposite ways, and she had the first of them worked out before she had finished one circuit.

Every spun deck has a light way and a heavy way. Fly against the turning and the floor lets go of you a little; fly with it and you carry more of yourself than you own. On a ring it is a thing to know. In a cylinder six hundred metres to the axis, at a raven's speed, it is a quarter of her weight in one direction and a third again in the other. She took the light way along the first cylinder's long gardens, high, where it was lighter still, and let the subsystem keep the tallies.

The hub she did not fly. The annex said so, in a line the Commander had added for this habitat and no other: *the Field Observer will not fly the hub, where nothing flies.* She had gone through it in the lift with both feet round a rail, as freight, with a foundry shift going the other way. They had been very courteous about it.

The heads came up. The pointing. A boy on a roof told a smaller boy something, with his whole arm.

She began the count, because she always did. It was her count. She had put it in writing in the winter, to a habitat that had asked.

At ninety seconds the boy was still pointing.

At five minutes a woman at a stall under the second garden had come out from behind her counter to stand in the walk and look up, with her hand flat over her eyes against the long lamps. The subsystem noted, unasked, that the interval was outside the range of every previous deployment. She had noticed.

She flew two shifts. She was not weather for any part of them. People looked up as she came over, and then, and this was the part she had no tally for, they looked up again as she went. A man repairing a rail said a word to her as she passed, at an ordinary pitch, as one says a word to a neighbour. By the end of the first shift she had heard it from the roofs and the walks and the stall. The subsystem observed that the speakers were using at least three languages and one word. It offered the nearest rendering in the language the Compact files in, and a note that the term was not *observer*.

On the second morning there was a dish of water on the rail by the stall. It had not been there on the first.

She had no technique for this. The technique was that nobody hid and nobody needed to, because people did the forgetting themselves. Here the notification had not gone downstairs to sit in an office. It was on a wall at the foot of every lift, eight times over, and they had read it, and it had told them what she was. She stood on the rail a while. Then she drank, since it had been put out for her, and people in the walk watched her do it and seemed satisfied.

The Commander's reading of the case, which he had sent her so that she would know what the paper said, was that somebody on Antariksha was keeping a rule. She could not see into the hub, where the reels were wound. She flew the docks at the cylinder's end, and the carriers going out with reels on them, and saw nothing that held still when it should have moved or moved when it should have held still.

She filed the second shift as a count, with one line under it that was not a count. *Not forgotten. No interval to report.*

The drawer came on the third day, at the end of the hub's morning shift, to the rail at the foot of the lift.

She had an interpreter with her, a young woman with the council's flash, who had plainly been fetched from something else and did not mind. The drawer stopped two paces from the rail. She was a broad, grey woman in a foundry tunic with the cuffs taped. She looked at Alala for some time, without hurry, and Alala looked back.

Then she spoke, and the interpreter, before rendering any of it, asked a question of her own.

"In which of the habitat's languages would the witness prefer to hear this?"

The subsystem answered for her, in the plain way it says what it is to any lawful person who asks. It had one language, and that one was nobody's on this deck. The interpreter took the answer as an ordinary one. Then she began, a sentence behind the drawer and in step with her.

"She says: you are here about the short reels. She read it on the wall. She says you will not see it from up there, because it is not a thing anyone does."

The drawer went on. She used her hands, flat, one drawn slowly away from the other.

"She says: we count the fibre on the draw. In the hall nothing lies down. A fibre with no pull on it is not a line, it goes where the air goes. So you pull it to make it a line, and the counter counts the line, and it goes on the reel pulled. That is the length on the certificate. Take the pull off and glass comes back. It always comes back. On Halcyon they lay it out on a floor that holds it down, with no pull on it, and they measure that."

The drawer said something short.

"She says: a little over a metre in the kilometre, on the fourth line. More on the second, they draw tighter. Everybody who has drawn knows what a reel gives back. It is on no certificate. Nobody asked the certificate."

The subsystem did the sum against the Commander's file and returned forty-four metres in forty kilometres. Alala had already seen the hands.

She did not know, standing on the rail, what a witness did when she was brought a thing instead of seeing it. Every file she had ever sent upward had been made of what she had watched. This had been watched by somebody else, for nineteen years, in a hall she could not fly, and carried down in a lift and put in front of her by name.

The drawer gave the name then, and her line, and her years, and waited while the interpreter said them. *Selvi Arumugam. Fourth line. Nineteen years.* She said one thing more, and the interpreter took a moment over it.

"She asks whether the witness will say it as she said it. She says that on the wall it is the witness's record, and she would not like to find she had said something tidier."

The subsystem had begun a summary. It was a good summary. It was shorter than what the drawer had said, and it had the word *tension* in it, which she had not used.

Alala stopped it. She had been sent a question once, by a habitat, and had answered in her own words, and the habitat had written them into its file under the heading they belonged to and had not shortened them. She had noticed that at the time. She had not expected to be on the other side of it.

She had the subsystem take the statement down as it was given, in the drawer's language and in the interpreter's, both, with the hands noted where the hands had done the work. She put the name over it. Then she had it given back, the whole of it, and the interpreter said it over to the drawer, who listened to her own statement returning and corrected one word.

:::pov larsen

He asked each end one question, by letter, and each end answered it truthfully in a line.

Antariksha's foundry certifies length as drawn. The draw tension is stated in the habitat's own standard, which runs to forty pages in each of eight languages, and the certificate cites the standard by number and does not repeat it. Halcyon measures length as laid, at rest, under Halcyon's standard, which is as long and as careful. Both figures were true of every reel. No reel had lost a metre between the hub and Halcyon's floor. It had been forty kilometres in a hall where a fibre has to be pulled before it can be counted, and forty-four metres less on a deck that holds it down, and it would be either again tomorrow if anyone put it back.

The Compact certifies bonded mass on one measure, inertially, because a habitat's weight is a thing the habitat is doing. Nobody had written the same sentence about length. Nobody had needed to, while a consignee signed for a reel and believed the certificate. The two standards had disagreed since the first reel crossed, and the disagreement had waited until a floor was robbed and started measuring.

So there had been a rule, and somebody did keep it. He had been right about that much. It was posted in eight languages and had a number.

He wrote the close the way they usually fell. Halcyon's half went down to Halcyon's bureau: thirty-eight consignees, nothing to charge, and whatever their contracts said about whose length was meant, which was the consignees' business and their shippers'. Antariksha's half went down to Antariksha's: nothing. The seam went to the Governor, in one paragraph. Two true lengths, and no office whose business it was to say which the Compact meant.

Then the statement. Alala had filed it whole, in a script he could not read and a rendering he could, with a name on it and a note that the name was there at its owner's request. He read the rendering twice. It did not say *tension* anywhere. It said that glass comes back.

He had come to Antariksha with the same letter he sent everywhere, and had been certain of what it did. It told the office with standing to object. He had learned in the winter, from a habitat's welfare file, that he had never once sent it to anyone with standing to answer. He had thought about that for some months without finding anything to do about it. He did not choose who read a Commissioner's post.

Here somebody had chosen. The letter had been read out of its office onto a wall, by people whose trade is to say what a sentence means, and a hundred and fifty thousand residents had been told that a witness was overhead. One of them had taken that literally and brought her nineteen years of evidence on the third day. On the other four habitats he would have got there in a fortnight, by asking offices. He would have had the standard's number and the word *tension*, and no name.

He appended the statement to the case as it stood, both versions, under the close. He did not summarise it in the report. He cited it.

:::pov alala

She flew the six days in the annex, because the dates said she would.

The subsystem kept the tally she had asked it for after the first shift, of faces that lifted twice. It had stopped being a tally on the second day and become, as near as the subsystem would commit itself, everybody. The dish on the rail was filled each morning before she came down to it. The boy on the roof brought other boys.

On the last day she went up the first cylinder the heavy way, low, over the walks. It is harder flying and she did not need to do it. People said the word to her as she passed, and she carried a third more than her own weight the length of the gardens and found that it was about right.

She left the habitat the way she had come, which on Antariksha was through the hub, as freight, with both feet round a rail. The shift going the other way made room.
