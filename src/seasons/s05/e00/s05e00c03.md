:::pov galen-kane

The mandate had two hours left in it when Kane wrote himself off duty. He did it in the docket at eight, by the settlement's clock, in one line with his initial under it. The tradition asked for a guest at the back of the hall. The Corps had no Rangers out of uniform except the ones who were off duty, so he went off duty and put on his own jacket.

It was the fifth world of the passage and the last. Nine weeks out. He set the region's rosters now, and had for some years, and he still wrote his own name against one passage in the year, the long one, on the reasoning that a roster is better written by someone who has lately been on it.

The colony had landed twelve years before with four hundred people and a compact, and the compact had invited his corps before anyone had unpacked. There were more than nine hundred now. They had built a hall and put the benches in before the windows, and this morning they were seating a council of seven in it, with a warrant to keep the peace.

He sat at the back. He had sat at the back of fourteen.

Nobody had ever needed to explain the seat to him. From the back a man can see the whole room and is in nobody's eyeline, and that was what was wanted. For twelve years there had been one office on this world that people looked toward when something went wrong. It had been his corps', and on four passages it had been him. By noon it would be seven of their own and a constable, and they would learn where to look sooner if the old place were behind them.

The convener read the article from the compact itself, the copy that had come down with the landing. The clause was not long. It said that the invitation stood until the settlement constituted an authority of its own, and it said what counted as one, and it did not ask for the Corps' opinion. Then the seven affirmed, one at a time. He watched the seventh. She was a pump-wright from the east run who had twice brought him a water claim and had disliked both findings, and she got through the form without looking at the back of the hall.

At the end of her sentence there was a council. It was nine minutes past ten.

Nothing marked it. The convener went straight on to the warrant, and a child on the third bench asked something and was hushed. Kane had been a peace officer on this world until the last word of the pump-wright's sentence. After it he was a visitor in a dark jacket, with no file here and no standing to write in the one he had closed. He noted the time in his own book, longhand. He would have noted it in any case.

He could have named three things in the docket that wanted another month of him. The east-run claim was one. He had been able to name three on every one of these mornings, in every hall, and for some years that had been how he knew the clause was sound. Whoever wrote it had understood that the Ranger would always have three.

The constable came to the back when the hall had begun to empty. She was the council's choice and an hour into the warrant. She had been the settlement's surveyor, and she had the open half of the docket under her arm already.

"Ranger."

"Not since nine minutes past ten."

She took that as it was meant, without a smile, and held up the docket. "The east-run claim. You had it down for the spring."

"It is all in there. What each side said, and the day they said it."

"What would you have done with it?"

"What the compact says, and it would have taken me until summer." He did not go on. She waited, and he let her. "It isn't mine. You would not thank me, in a year, for having made it a little bit mine on your first morning. Read what they said before anyone tells you what they meant."

She looked at him a moment longer, to see whether that was really all, and it was. She thanked him for twelve years, formally, on the council's behalf. He said that the Corps had been glad of the invitation. That was the form, and he also meant it. She went back up the hall to the seven.

The passage's letters had been held for him at the landing ground. He had collected the pouch on his way up and had not opened it, since a man who reads his post at the back of a seating is making a remark. He opened it now, on the bench, in a hall that was no longer any business of his.

Rosters, mostly. A chapter minute. And one sheet in a hand he would have known across a room: upright, unhurried, with nothing crossed out. It had been in the pouch since before his third world.

She was a Starwarden. She said so in one line, with the season, and gave the post as a fold approach on the outward frontier and did not name it. Then the question, which was six words, and her name.

*When is leaving the correct order?*

She had ridden second to him on one of the last passages he took before the desk, and he had written afterwards in her file, *not one we will keep*. He had meant it as the best thing his corps could say of anyone. He had not expected it back by pouch, as a question. She had gone somewhere that did keep people, to a stretch of approach that nobody was ever going to seat a council over, and she was asking the one corps that knew about leaving how it was done.

The trouble was that his corps did not know. It had arranged, very carefully, never to have to.

He had the one file. It ran four months where his others ran weeks, and it was the only time in thirty years that the order had been his to give. It was closed and reviewed. He did not teach from it. He had never been asked to, and he had been glad of that, for a reason he did not go into on a bench with a pen in his hand.

He wrote the answer there, longhand, on a sheet from his own book.

*Shepherd,*

*The order has been mine to give once in thirty years. The file runs four months. It is in the Corps library, closed, with the dissents attached, and any officer may send for it. Read the dissents first. I do not teach from it and I will not begin by post.*

*Every other time it was not an order. It was a clause. The people we serve write it before we land, on a day when nothing has gone wrong yet. When the thing in it happens we are ended, in the middle of somebody's sentence, and nobody asks the Ranger. I have left fifteen worlds that way and chosen none of the mornings. I would have stayed on at every one. There was always something open.*

*So I am the wrong man to tell you when. I can tell you what the clause is made of, since your corps will not have written you one. It was set down beforehand. It was set down by somebody who was not me. And when it happens, the whole hall can see that it has. I have never trusted a leaving that lacked one of the three, and I do not except the one in the library.*

*Ask again when you have a case in front of you. I answer at the ends.*

*Kane*

He read it through and made no fair copy. Nothing in it was crossed out. If anything had been, she would have been entitled to see what.

The hull that would take him off was due in six days. The mandate had ended at nine minutes past ten. The Corps' doctrine was thorough on the second point and had nothing to offer on the first. He put the fifteenth in his own book, under the time, and walked down to the landing ground, where a man who was waiting to leave could be seen to be waiting.

:::pov tissadelle

She had sealed the question in the winter. The answer came with the first stores hull of the spring, in the post's pouch, under a Frontier Corps frank she had not seen since she rode second.

She read it in the office. Her own charts were on the bulkhead by then. That had taken her most of the winter.

One sheet, longhand. She looked for a crossing-out before she read a word. With Kane that was where the second answer would have been, and there was none.

She had written to him because he could order her nothing, and so whatever he said would be free. It was free. He had used it to tell her that he was the wrong man. He had left fifteen worlds, and on every one a clause had done the leaving, and he had gone when it said.

*I would have stayed on at every one.*

She went back to that line. She had seen him go from one of the fifteen, in his own jacket, and he had not turned round at the hall door. She had taken it then for the thing he was best at. He was telling her it had been done for him each time, that he knew it, and that he trusted it the more for that.

He had not said what he did in the four months. He had said where the file was and which end to start from.

Every post keeps two bills. The watch bill was the Chief's and hung by the desk. The other hung inside the office hatch, where the Starwarden could not go in or out without passing it, and the post called it the withdrawal bill. She had read it in her first week. She took it off its hook now and read it for what it was made of.

Forty lines. Which hatches were shut, and in what order. Who carried the logs. Twenty people off in three parties, the watch at the desk last. The six instruments were to be left running and in agreement, with nobody to read them, and the bill gave the number of days the reserve would keep them so. Lindahl had told her, when she asked, that it was drilled each spring and each autumn and had not been run in his nine years.

The first line was the only one about when. *Withdrawal is on the Starwarden's order.*

*Forty lines,* Tobble said. *Thirty-nine are how.*

*And one is who.*

*Then it does not say when at all.* He considered it. *That is a long bill to leave the first thing out of.*

She did not think it had been left out. A colony could say beforehand what would end its need of a Ranger, because the colony was going to do the thing itself, in a hall, on a day it chose. Nobody at a line office could say beforehand what an approach might do that ought to take twenty people off it. If they could have listed it, they would have built a seventh instrument to read it. So the Corps had written down what it knew, which was the how, and had given the rest to one officer, to be settled on the day, standing in the place.

It was an honest line. She could see that, and she could see what Kane would say it was made of.

She did not write anything under it. She had been four months at the post and she did not have the words. A condition written so as to have one would be read, on the day, as though somebody had known.

What she could do was send for the file. The library lent a closed file to any officer who asked. The request went up the relay in clear and was logged at each desk it crossed. She made it out in her own name and rank: *Kane, G., Frontier Corps. The four-month passage. With dissents.* It would be in the post's evening summary, where Lindahl read everything, and at the line office nine hours after that. Anyone who read summaries would know what the Starwarden of a quiet approach had sent for.

She let that stand, and sent it.

Then she hung the bill back on its hook as it was, and filed his sheet with the copy of hers. The question was still six words. It had gone out with nothing under it, and it had come back with a file to send for and three things a leaving ought to be made of. It was not answered. There was more of it than she had sent.
