::: pov arashobal

*To the Line Captain, by relay. The station's outward corridor: laden hulls to enter within the first half of the band as general operations states it. A hull that cannot make the first half takes the next cycle. Entry on the trailing edge, where unavoidable, at reduced closing rate, lateral margin held to the station side. Stores hulls to take the first window of each cycle until further order.*

It was in the High Captain's own hand, which a staff saw seldom, and it had come down to the staff office for fair copy and the relay.

Arashobal read it at the desk. She read it once, because there was nothing in it she needed twice.

Every sentence in it was true. She did not hold a pilot's plate and could not have said so from the corridor; she could say it from the hand. The officer who had written it had flown boundary corridors for most of thirty years and had commanded the people who flew them, and had written this in the time a kettle takes, without a correction, and it had the plainness of a thing known rather than a thing worked out. If a Chief Pilot three days away had been asked how she would fly a laden hull late into a stated band, and had answered well, this was very nearly the answer.

That was the drift. It took her a moment to place, since it did not sit where the doctrine said to look.

The review culture had a test for an order that reached too far, and she had applied it to other people's paper for six weeks: an order so detailed that its recipient needs no judgement to carry it out is claiming a certification its giver does not hold. This giver held it. There was no officer in the theatre who held it more. The test went through the page and found nothing, and the page was still an order to a Chief Pilot telling her which half of her own band to use.

So the test was a shorthand, and what it stood for was something else. She set that down and looked at what the order was resting on.

It rested on a summary. Three times in a month, at a relay station on the far side of the theatre, a laden stores hull had entered the outward corridor in the last tenth of the band. No incident. The line's summary had carried it up as a figure in a column, and the column had been thirty hours on the relay, and the High Captain had read it standing, and Arashobal had watched her eye stop on it and stay. That had been yesterday. The order would be thirty hours going back. By the time a Chief Pilot read it, it would be an answer to a corridor nearly three days gone, from a hull that could not have reached her in less than three more.

Nothing in the draft said what it was for. Nothing in it said what it must not cost.

She did not write on it. A staff that marked up a High Captain's order had begun writing one, and the office had no orders of its own. She took the sheet as it was, across the eight paces to the other door, which stood open, as it had since the first day.

::::: scene 2

::: pov wender

"It is not sent," Arashobal said.

Wender had known that from the sheet in her hand. A thing that had gone to the relay did not come back across the deck.

"Is it wrong?"

"No. I cannot fault a line of it. I do not hold the plate, so that is worth less than it sounds, but I do not think the Chief Pilot there will fault a line of it either." Arashobal put it on the desk, squarely, the way she put down anything she was about to disagree with. "She will fly it exactly. That is the difficulty."

Wender waited. Six weeks had taught her that the first sentence was the fact and the second was where it bit, and that there was generally a third.

"It is correct for the corridor in the summary. The summary was thirty hours old when you read it. The order will be thirty more arriving. It tells an officer who is standing at the instruments what to do with them, on the word of an officer who is not." A pause, at Wender's speed and not her own. "It leaves her nothing to decide. So if the corridor is not what the summary said, she has an order and no room, and you will hear about it in a column."

"The corridor does not change in sixty hours."

"You do not know that. I do not either. She does, and the order does not ask her."

That was the third, and it was fair, and Wender found she did not want it to be.

She looked at her own hand on the sheet. She had not thought about writing it. The figure in the column had been a laden hull going into a boundary corridor with no second approach left in the window, three times, and her whole service had been the difference between a hull that could go round again and a hull that could not, and the pen had been moving before the High Captain had been consulted. *Lateral margin held to the station side.* She could feel the corridor in that. She had never seen the corridor.

"There is a minute at Threshold," Arashobal said. "Summer of 2828. A Starwarden wanted a band flown as a point. The pilot department said it flew a window as general operations stated it, and that the other thing was not an order the department's Chief could give."

"I was the department."

"Yes."

She did not add the rest. She had never needed to add the rest; it was the thing about her that a room took for coldness. Wender had sat at the end of that table with her hands folded and had told a Starwarden, in one sentence, where his order stopped. Now a Section Lead she had never met had stated a band at a station she had never seen, and the sheet on the desk cut it in half. She had been the department. Whatever she was now, it was not that, and she had written as though the chair had come with her.

"What is it for?" Arashobal said.

"No laden hull in the last tenth."

"That is a method. It is a good one. What does it save?"

Wender took longer over that than she liked. "The second approach. A hull that enters on the trailing edge and has to break off has nowhere to go but out of the band. What I want kept is the room to try twice."

"Then that is the objective, and she can be told it. What must it not cost?"

The stores, she began to say, and stopped, because she had been about to state it as a thing she knew. Those hulls were how the station ate. An order that cured late entries by sending hulls round to the next cycle might be paid for in something she could not see from here: a schedule, a lading, another department's hours. There would be a reason three hulls had been late, and it would not be that three pilots had been slow. Pilots were not slow. She knew that better than she knew the theatre.

"I do not know what it must not cost," she said. "I am not standing there."

"Then it can say that."

It was an odd thing to put in an order. She turned it over and could not find the objection. An order was sound if it was correct given what was currently known, and what was currently known included the size of the hole in it. She had said the first half of that to a room once, in more words than she liked. Nobody had ever handed it back to her across her own desk.

She took a clean sheet. Arashobal did not sit, and did not watch her write, and looked instead at the bulkhead where the previous High Captain's theatre chart had been, as though the outline it had left were worth reading.

*To the Line Captain, by relay, for the station's Chief Pilot and the Section Lead who states the band.*

*Objective: no laden hull committed to the outward corridor without room for a second approach inside the band.*

*Constraint: the band is general operations' to state and is not narrowed by this order.*

*Priority: the second approach before the schedule.*

*This order is not meant to cost the station its stores, and I cannot see from here what else it might. If it will cost something it should not, say so first. The method is yours. Tell me what was done.*

Four lines and a paragraph. It had taken her five times as long as the first.

"The first one was better written," she said.

"Yes," Arashobal said, and took the second to the relay.

::: pov arashobal

The reply was in the theatre's summary on the ninth day, under the line's heading, and it was a Chief Pilot's and not a Line Captain's, forwarded with an initial and no remark.

Arashobal had it first, since the staff weighed everything before the High Captain was handed it, and she read it at the desk.

The pilots had not been late. That was the first line. The stores hulls had been released late from the lading dock on three days in the month, each time by a little under an hour, since a consolidation at the line's depot had begun sending the station's stores in sealed crates that took longer to check aboard. The pilots had been given a hull forty minutes behind and a band that did not move, and had made up what could be made up, and had entered where that left them. The Chief Pilot had known the figure. She had been arguing the lading cut-off with the dock for five weeks, Chief to Chief, and losing politely.

The order had ended the argument. *The second approach before the schedule* was a High Captain's priority in a High Captain's words, and the dock's Chief had read it and moved the cut-off forward an hour the same day. No hull's entry had been touched. The band stood as the Section Lead had stated it. Stores were arriving as before, and an hour earlier to the dock.

There was one more paragraph, and it was offered, Arashobal judged, as information and not as a point.

*For the High Captain's picture of the corridor: the early part of the band here is kept clear on every cycle. It is where an inbound hull that has broken off makes its second approach. We do not put outbound traffic in it and have not in my time.*

She read that paragraph for what it did to the first sheet.

*Stores hulls to take the first window of each cycle.* The draft would have put laden hulls, by order, from the apex of the ladder, into the one stretch of the band the station kept empty for the thing the order was written to save. A Chief Pilot would have read it and known it was wrong, and would have had an instruction that left her nothing to decide. She would have sent up a query, sixty hours the round trip. Or she would have flown it.

Arashobal did not know which. She found that she did not need to. The sheet had not gone.

Its use was over, and she had not kept it. She had not thought of keeping it. She registered that now as a fact about herself, of the kind her people were told about by others, and wrote *records: the first draft, if held* on the cover of the summary before she took it across.

::::: scene 4

::: pov wender

She read it standing.

The pilots had not been late. She had the small, flat satisfaction of that for exactly the length of the line, and then she had the rest.

Forty minutes at a lading dock. Sealed crates. She could not have seen it from the *Fel Trohen* with every instrument the ship carried; it was not in any column, since a hull released late and flown well leaves nothing behind it but an entry time. An officer had been standing there who knew, and who had been short of one thing, which was a sentence from far enough up that a dock would move for it. Wender had very nearly not sent her that sentence. She had very nearly sent her a way of flying.

Then the last paragraph, which she read twice.

*We do not put outbound traffic in it and have not in my time.*

She knew why at once, the way she would have known it in the first minute on that station's flight deck. A band kept clear at the leading edge for the inbound to go round again. It was what she would have done with the corridor herself. It was sound practice, it was local, and it was written down nowhere that reached a command ship, because nobody writes down for a theatre the thing every pilot on the station was shown in their first week.

She had ordered it filled. In her own hand, without a correction, in the time a kettle takes.

The reply did not say so. The Chief Pilot had never seen the first sheet and had no idea it existed; she had been sent four lines and a paragraph, and had answered them, and had added a thing about her corridor that a former pilot might like to know. That was all it was. Wender was the only officer in the theatre who could read it as anything else, apart from one, who was at that moment at the other desk, not watching her.

"There is a note on the cover," she said.

"Records has it," Arashobal said. "They keep what comes down for fair copy, sent or unsent. I asked this morning. I had not kept it myself."

"You would not have."

"No. Its use was over."

"It was not, quite." Wender held out her hand, and after a moment a clerk from the records deck, who had plainly been waiting in the passage with it, came in and gave her the sheet and went away again.

She looked at it beside the reply. It was still better written than the order that had gone.

She thought, briefly, of an Archwarden three rungs below her on a hull that could get somewhere, who might have a line of her own before long, and who would one day write a sheet like this about the one thing she knew best, in the time a kettle takes. The pairing was on the record and did not lapse. Two sheets and a reply would go in a pouch. It would be a lesson, and a good one, and it would arrive with a High Captain's frank on it.

She had told that officer, in a previous Starwarden's office, that she would say one thing once and then stop telling her things. It had been easy to say. She had been a Line Captain, with a hull that arrived.

She did not make up the pouch.

She put the first sheet under the second and the reply on top, and wrote the theatre's file number on the corner of all three, so that whoever weighed them next would find them in the order they had happened. Under the reply she put her initial, and *read*, and the date. There was nothing else on it that was hers to add.

"Acknowledge to the Chief Pilot," she said. "Through the Line Captain. Say that it was done, and that I am obliged for the corridor."

"In those words?"

"In those words."

Arashobal wrote them down as given, and went to the relay.
