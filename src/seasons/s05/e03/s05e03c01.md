::: pov sethka-ru

The star had been quiet for nineteen hours, and Sethka Ru did not hold that in its favour.

He had been watching it for six days, since the station asked for him, through the one port on the sunward gallery that the crew kept shuttered and that he had asked to have unshuttered for him. They had learned to arrange that, and the other thing. At the station's dawn and again at its dusk he was in his quarters, with the door sealed and the air thinned, and was not to be asked for; in the hours either side he was at the port, closed, with the filtration set on and a slate. Station air was heavy work. He had stopped remarking on it some years ago.

The solar watch kept a figure for the star and had given it to him on the first day, in a brief, with a line under it. *Mean quiet interval between major events, this season: six hours and ten minutes.* It was a careful figure. He had gone back through the watch's own log to see what it was a mean of.

Eleven intervals. The longest had been fourteen hours and the shortest two and a half, and laid out in the order they had happened, which was not how the brief laid them out, the last five were all under the mean and each a little shorter than the one before it.

He did not think the watch had been careless. A mean is an honest thing to say about eleven numbers. It was only that nobody was going to fly out into the mean. Somebody was going to fly out into the twelfth interval, whatever that turned out to be, and the brief had no line for it.

*Assume stable* was not a method. He had said so once on a form, and it had been copied into his file by an officer who liked the sound of it. On Kharis Belt IV nobody had needed it said. The margin there came twice a day and was a different length each time, and a person who went out on yesterday's did not come in to report.

The task itself was plain. The approach had three markers, and the inner one rode close in to the star, where the star's own noise hid it from the station's ranging on the best of days. Eleven days ago it had stopped answering. A fold approach with a marker unaccounted for was shut to laden traffic until the marker had been seen, by someone, from a place where the glare did not stand behind it. There were two stores hulls waiting at the outer marker already, and a third due.

A Line Captain's hull had come in on the fourth day, on an inspection circuit, and had found the approach shut and had stood off with the others. He had expected the order to change when it did. A senior officer arriving at a closed approach generally brings an hour with her, and the hour is generally the watch change.

The order came down through his Chief at the station's noon. He read it closed, at the port, with the star to one side.

*Objective: the inner marker's position and state, confirmed by eye.*

*Constraint: the scout's exposure. No figure is given here. The scout holds the certificate for it and measures it on the day.*

*Priority: the scout before the marker. The marker before the schedule.*

*This order must not cost the scout his margin. Departure, standoff and return are at his call. A partial return is a return. The slip is the Line Captain's.*

There was no hour in it. He looked for one, since they hid sometimes in the routing, and there was none there either.

He read the last line again. He had been sent out on a great many orders, and most had been sound, and almost all of them had stopped one line short of this. They said what he was to bring back. They did not say what he was allowed to come back without.

He told his Chief he would go after the station's dusk, when he had closed again, and that he would take four hours and not six.

"The brief says six," his Chief said. It was an observation.

"The brief says six was the mean."

His Chief looked at the slate, where the eleven intervals were in their order, and did not say anything more about the brief.

::: pov tissadelle

The first draft had had a figure in it.

She had written *window: six hours, per the solar watch* under the constraint, because the brief was in front of her and six hours was what it said, and a constraint with a number in it reads as a firmer thing than one without. Then she had looked at whose number it was. The watch had measured the star. The scout would be the one inside the measurement. She held the entry certification for a great many things, flush on the plate, and none of them was this; if she wrote six, she was telling the only officer on the station who could read that star how long it would hold.

She struck it, and the line that replaced it had no figure at all, and looked weaker, and was the correct one.

The station's Starwarden had not liked the last paragraph. He was a reasonable officer with two laden hulls at his outer marker and stores for sixty people aboard them, and he asked, with care, whether *a partial return is a return* was not an invitation.

"It is a permission," she said. "He would come back on his own reading in any case. This way he does not have to decide, out there, whether I will stand behind it."

"And if he comes back with half?"

"Then the approach stays shut until the next quiet, and the slip is on my line and under my name. I have put that in."

He did not argue it. He looked at the order a moment longer, as a man looks at a bill somebody else has offered to pay, and passed it down.

That had been noon. The craft cleared the station after its dusk, on the scout's call. She did not know why he had chosen that hour and did not ask; it was his.

She took the watch on the *Sufficient Cause* herself. There was no reason for it. Her Archwarden had the hull, the hull was standing off at the outer marker with nothing to do, and a Line Captain on a flight deck during somebody else's sortie was an officer taking up a seat. She sat in it anyway, and her Archwarden, who had served with her for some weeks and was forming a view, said nothing and brought the solar watch's feed up on the second board.

The craft was called the *Second Reading*, and for an hour and a half it was a clean return, going sunward. Then it went into the star.

Not in fact. It went to its standoff, which was the one place the marker could be seen without the glare behind it, and from where the *Sufficient Cause* sat, the standoff lay almost on the star's limb. Its signal thinned into the noise and was gone. The station had said that would happen. It was in the brief, two lines above the mean.

So there was a scout at the far point with a marker to look at, and a hull at the outer marker with a Line Captain on it, and nothing between them that would carry a word.

At three hours and four minutes the solar watch's feed changed colour.

It was the light of an event. The watch called it at once, in the flat voice watches have: a brightening in the hard band, major, particles to follow. At that distance from the star the gap between the light and what came after it was somewhere between twenty minutes and forty. Nobody could say it closer. The craft's return to the station's shielding was twenty-five.

She did the arithmetic. If he had seen it when the watch did and had turned at once, he was inside it. If he had waited for anything, he was not.

"Recall, Line Captain?" her Archwarden said. The form was already up.

She looked at it. A recall from this hull would go out into the same noise the craft's own signal had drowned in. If it arrived at all it would arrive late, and it would say, to an officer who had been reading that star for six days, what the star had just told him at the speed of light.

It would do one other thing. It would put her voice in the log, at the moment, being seen to act.

*You have read the order four times,* Tobble said. *It says what it said.*

*I know what it says.*

*Then it is already out there. You sent it at noon.*

"No," she said, aloud. "He has the order."

Her Archwarden took the form down, and did not look at her, and she was grateful for both.

It might be forty minutes before anything could be known. She had commanded a post, and had stood a middle watch on it every night, and had thought she knew what waiting was. That had been waiting for nothing to happen. This was a different article. Every minute of it was a minute in which an officer was acting on four lines she had written at a desk, and the four lines were the whole of her that was out there, and there was nothing to add to them and no way to take one back.

Wender had told her, in a dead Starwarden's office, that the stopping was most of the job. She had taken it for a remark about mentors.

She did not get up. She did not ask for the feed again. At the end of the first quarter hour she asked her Archwarden what the hull's own dose plate read, at the outer marker, behind its shielding, and was told, and it was nothing, and she had known it would be.

::: pov sethka-ru

He had the position in the first twenty minutes at the standoff. The marker was where its last return had put it, to within the width of the craft. That was half the objective.

The state was slower. The marker was turning, end over end, about once in thirty-one minutes, and the face that carried its array went dark and came round and went dark again. A thing that tumbles has been struck, or has vented, or has lost the wheel that holds it still, and each of those is a different repair. To say which, he needed one full turn on the far face in clean light, and the far face came round on the marker's time and not on his.

He had the first quarter of the turn recorded when the star brightened.

He did not need the board for it. The port was filtered to nothing and the cabin still went a shade paler along one edge, and the membranes his people have for a sky with no air in it took the change before the instrument did. Then the board agreed.

Three hours and four minutes. He had allowed four.

He looked at the marker. Twenty-three minutes more would bring the far face round. He looked at the figure for the craft's storm cell, which would hold a person through the edge of a small event and was not built for the middle of a large one, and at the return, which was twenty-five.

There was no decision in it, which he noted. He had flown for commanders whose orders would have put one there. A scout who has been told to bring back the state of a marker, and is a quarter of a turn into it, can find a great many reasons in twenty-three minutes. He knew them all. He had heard them given at inquiries, by people who had come back, about people who had not.

*A partial return is a return.*

He turned the craft. He took the recording with him, the quarter turn, which showed a little and proved nothing.

The particles found him eleven minutes from the station. The plate climbed, and he watched it climb. Watching it altered nothing. He put himself in the storm cell for the last stretch and flew from there on the repeater. The plate was under its line when the station's shadow took the craft, and stayed under it.

By his own reckoning, made afterwards and at leisure, twenty-three more minutes at the far point would have put it over. By the brief's six hours he would have been there for three hours more.

::: pov tissadelle

The station logged the *Second Reading* inside its shielding at thirty-one minutes after the light. It had the scout out of the storm cell, and his plate read and called, at forty.

She heard both from her own second board, where her Archwarden had left the station's traffic channel open without being asked. Craft in. Then, nine minutes on: scout fit, dose under the line. She found she had both hands flat on the arms of the seat and took them off.

The Starwarden's summary came across within the hour. Position confirmed; state not confirmed; marker tumbling, cause undetermined; one quarter rotation recorded. The approach remained shut. A third stores hull had joined the two at the outer marker.

Under it he had written, since he was an honest officer and it was the question: *The scout reports twenty-three further minutes would have completed the survey.*

She answered that line and no other. *The order said the return was at his call. He called it. Approach closed pending confirmation of marker state; slip to the Line Captain's account from noon today.* She put her name to it, and her rank, in clear.

She went across to the station that evening, after its dusk, which she had learned by then was the hour to choose. The scout was at the sunward port, with the filtration on and a slate. She had read the Corps' note on his people years ago, on a rotation, and remembered enough of it not to ask how he was.

"The brief was wrong," she said.

"No. It was a mean." He turned the slate so that she could see the intervals in their order. Eleven of them, and under them a twelfth in his own notation, three hours and four minutes, shorter than the one above it. "They are all true. It depends what you ask them."

She looked at the column for some time. It was the kind of thing she would have caught, with the log in front of her and an afternoon. She had not had the log in front of her. She had had a brief, and she had very nearly copied its figure into an order.

"When is the next quiet?"

"The star has not said." He considered. "Two days. Perhaps three. I will measure it when it comes, and I will tell my Chief, and he will tell you."

Through his Chief. She noted that, and that it was right.

"I am not asking for a day," she said.

"No," Sethka Ru said. "You did not ask for an hour either. I looked."

There did not seem to be anything to add. She thanked him for the quarter turn, which was hers to thank him for, and he said it showed that the marker had not vented, since a vented casing tumbles faster and this was slow. He would not say more than that on a quarter. She did not press him to.

On the hull she wrote up the day. The slip went in under her name with the three hulls against it, and the stores of sixty people, and a figure for each. It was a bad column and it was hers. Nobody at the line office would enjoy it, and she could defend every entry in it to anyone who asked.

There was a space under it for remarks, which she generally left empty.

*Forty minutes*, she began, and looked at it. The form had no place for a duration in which nothing was done. It was not a finding. No officer who read it would be able to act on it.

*It would tell the next one,* Tobble said.

*The next one will find out the same way.*

She left the two words where they were, without a sentence after them, and closed the day.
