::: pov mira-of-brine

The cold run sweated on a schedule, and Mira of Brine had been reading the schedule for nine days.

The coolant transit gallery ran under the outer ring for most of its length, low and close and warmer than the decks above, with the air standing at a pressure the rest of the station found tiresome. She worked there by choice. In the gallery the air behaved like a fluid that knew it was one. It pooled, and it turned at the junctions, and where it met the cold run it gave up its water in a line along the pipe, as neat as a tide mark. The line had a height. The height depended on a great many things, and all of them were things she could feel.

Nine days ago the line had stood level with the lower row of fastenings on the pipe's saddle. This morning it stood a little above them.

That was not much. It was less than the width of the fastenings themselves. The gallery's monitors, which read humidity at eleven fixed points and pressure at four, had every figure inside its band and nearly every figure where it had been in the spring. She had looked at them, since they were instruments too, and they were not wrong. They were reading where the gallery was.

She was reading where it was going. At the third junction the air had always turned outward, toward the ring. It still did, but more slowly, and against something. Between the third junction and the fourth, on the side away from the cold run, there was a little more water in the air each day than the day before, and it was not coming from the air. Somewhere in that stretch a seam was passing vapour. It was passing very little. It was passing more than it had.

The gallery was due its quarterly certificate. She did not sign it.

At Halyx, since the end of the summer, that was a document with a name. *Refusal to certify: a reading requiring disposition.* The form was new, and whoever had made it up had done it quickly and with care. There was a box for the officer refusing. There was a box for the system. And there was a box, the largest, headed *State the reading.*

She wrote: *Vapour gain between junctions three and four, outboard side, rising. About one part in sixty a day at present. Rate itself increasing slowly.*

It came back within the hour from the oversight office with a note, courteously put. *Thank you. For the disposition I will need the reading itself: what is the present value, and by how much is it out of tolerance?*

She considered that for some time.

It was not out of tolerance. That was the entire point; if it had been, the monitors would have said so and nobody would have needed her. The present value was a perfectly ordinary number. She could give it to him, and it would tell him that nothing was wrong, and that would be true of today and of no day after it.

Among her own people the question could hardly have been asked. Nothing in the layers of home stayed at a value. A thing was rising or settling, gaining or thinning, and you said which and how fast. If someone wanted to know where it was at a given hour, that was their arithmetic. Here they built their forms the other way. They wanted the place first, and the motion, if at all, under *remarks*.

She wrote it out again, with the present value at the top, because he had asked for it, and under it: *This figure is inside tolerance and will be for some weeks. I am not refusing the figure. I am refusing the direction.*

That one took longer to come back.

::: pov qiren-tal

Qiren Tal was sent for as an instrument, and knew it, and did not mind. It was a more accurate summons than most.

The oversight officer met them at the gallery hatch himself. He was the same officer. Five weeks earlier he had been confident in the monitors, in the procedure, and most warmly in Qiren's need of leave, and he had been wrong three times in succession without one unkind intention. He had read the Line Captain's report afterwards. Qiren knew that because he had come down to the ring the day it was posted and had said so, standing, in four sentences, and had not asked to be told it was all right. Qiren had thought better of him for the last part.

He was not confident now. He had the form in his hand, and he held it as though it might go off.

"A refusal is a reading," he said. "That is the rule here. I have a refusal. I have closed the gallery to transit from the end of this watch, and I am minded to take the outer ring's coolant off this loop until we know."

Qiren worked that through. The outer ring's coolant served the transit corridor. The corridor had been open five weeks. Taken off this loop it would go back to restricted working, and the transit crews, who had only just stopped counting slipped days, would begin again.

"What does the surveyor say it will do?"

"She says it is inside tolerance." He said it carefully, like a man reporting a thing he did not intend to be caught disbelieving. "And that she is refusing the direction. I do not know what to do with a direction. I thought, since you —" He stopped. "You are the one this station did not listen to. I would rather not do that twice. Will you strike it?"

So Qiren struck it.

They went down the gallery from the third junction to the fourth, bracing at each frame and ringing it at the points a frame offers, and listened with the whole of the lattice, the way they had listened on the ring for six weeks that summer. The surveyor kept to the far side of the cold run and did not speak. It was a courtesy between instruments.

The frames gave the answers they had always given. The pipe saddles were seated. The run carried its load evenly, and nothing in the stretch was taking up a difference. Qiren went down it a second time, more slowly, because the officer was watching and because once is a reading and twice is a check.

"Nothing," Qiren said.

The officer's whole body eased, and then, visibly, he distrusted the ease. "Then it is sound."

"I did not say that. I said I hear nothing. A lattice hears load. If a seam here were carrying wrongly I would have it. This is vapour through a seam that is carrying perfectly well." They looked for the plain way to put it. "My instrument does not reach where hers is reading. It is not a second opinion. It is a blank."

"So I have one refusal and one blank."

"Yes."

He looked at the form. Qiren watched him look, and recognised it, with some discomfort, from the other side. For six weeks in the summer this man had answered the question *is the corridor sound* by being sure that it was. He was answering it now by being sure that it was not, or acting so. It was the same question. He had only turned it over. Either way he would have an answer by the end of the watch, and the gallery would be made to fit it.

The Line Captain had not asked that question at all. Qiren had the two lines of her report by heart, as one has the wording of a certificate. The second was the one they had thought about most, since it was about them: that the question on the ring had been what the refusal was costing the one who knew.

It did not fit here, quite. Nobody was doubting the surveyor. Nobody had counted a day against her name. If she was paying for anything, it was not that.

Qiren looked across the cold run. The surveyor was holding very still, in the way of someone who has been asked the same thing twice in a language that lacks the tense for the answer.

"The form says disposition," Qiren said. "It does not say verdict. May I ask her something?"

::: pov mira-of-brine

The hull specialist did not ask whether she was right. She had been braced for that, and for the kindness that would come with it.

"If nothing is done," they said, "when does it reach the monitors?"

It was the first question in two days that had a rate in it. She felt the whole problem turn the right way up.

"Seven weeks, as it runs now. Sooner if the rate goes on rising. I cannot tell you from nine days whether it will. I could tell you from fifteen."

"And when it reaches the monitors, what is it then?"

"Then it is a small leak that everyone can see. It is still not a dangerous one. Some weeks after that it would be."

The oversight officer had followed this closely. "Then we have weeks," he said, and it was plain that he did not like having them. A closed gallery was a thing he could sign and point to. "I can put a temporary seal over the stretch today. A wrap, three to four. It would hold until the maintenance window."

She tried to take that in, and could not find where to put it.

"Over which seam?"

"Over all of them. The whole stretch."

A covering put on in an afternoon, over a place nobody had found, to be taken off again in nine days. She understood each word. Her people grew a fitting over a season, to sit in one place for a lifetime, and the idea of a thing made on purpose not to last arrived in her like a sentence with the verb missing. It would also, she saw, put a wet warm layer over the exact stretch she was reading, and she would be able to tell him nothing at all from then on.

"If you wrap it I cannot read it," she said. "You would have a gallery that I had refused and could no longer say anything about."

"Then what do you want done?"

Nobody had asked her that either. She found that she knew.

"Nothing done to it. I want it measured. Leave the gallery open and the loop as it is. I will read the stretch at the same hour for six days and put the rate on your form each day, with the figure you like at the top. If the rate holds, the seam is opened at the maintenance window on the ninth day, which is already in the plan, and found and reseated. If the rate climbs on any day, I will say so that day, and you close it then, and you will have my name under the reason."

The officer looked at the hull specialist, who said nothing. It was not theirs.

"That is not a disposition," he said slowly. "That is waiting."

"It is waiting with a figure on it every day. I do not know how to do the other kind."

He was quiet for a while. She did not need telling what it cost him. He had been wrong once this year by doing too little, in front of everyone. He had come down here to do enough. Now he was being asked to put his name to a course that would look, in any summary, very like doing too little again. If the seam let go on the fifth day there would be a form with his initial on it saying he had known, and had left the gallery open.

"If I sign that," he said, "I am saying I believe you about the direction and I do not believe it is urgent. Both."

"Yes. That is what I am saying too."

He took the form back. In the box headed *Disposition* he wrote for some time, in a small hand, and then read it to them, since he wanted it heard: gallery to remain in service at present load; surveyor's rate entered daily for six days; seam to be opened at the scheduled window; gallery to close at once on any rise, at the surveyor's word alone, with no further reference to this office. He reopened the gallery to transit himself, from the panel at the hatch, an hour after he had closed it.

Then he initialled it. He did it in the manner of a man signing for a parcel he had not been allowed to open.

::: pov qiren-tal

The rate held.

Qiren had no duty in the gallery and went down on four of the six days regardless, at the surveyor's hour, and stood on the near side of the cold run and learned to see the line. It was not a thing a lattice could hear. It could be looked at. On the third day they could tell, unaided, that it had climbed, and said so, and the surveyor made a small movement that Qiren chose to read as approval.

Each day's form went up to the oversight office with the figure he liked at the top and the rate beneath. Each day it came back initialled. He did not come down. Qiren thought that was probably the hardest part of it for him, and the part he did best.

On the ninth day the maintenance crew opened the stretch. The seam was at the second saddle past the third junction, on the outboard side, where the surveyor had said. A gasket had been seated a quarter turn short when the pump was changed in the spring. It had carried its load from the first hour, and would have gone on carrying it for years, which was why no frame had ever complained of it. It had simply not been closed. Nobody had done anything wrong on purpose. The fitter who reseated it was the fitter who had seated it, and he looked at it for a moment and said, "Well," and did it again properly.

The surveyor read the stretch that evening and the next. Then she signed the certificate. Under *remarks* she wrote that the line on the cold run had stopped rising. She did not write where it had stopped.

The oversight officer wrote it up himself. Qiren saw the copy on the ring's board. It was short, and it blamed no one, and it had one sentence in it that he could not have written in the summer: *The refusal was correct in direction and the gallery was at no time unsafe; both were true for nine days, and the disposition was to keep them both on the form.*

It went up the line in the evening summary. The line belonged to a Line Captain who had stood at a junction on the ring for three transits, with her slate dark, and had since gone on along her circuit. She would read it when it reached her, or she would not. It did not need her. Qiren found that they had half expected it to, and that the station had not.

They went down to the ring at the end of the watch and struck the seventeenth point, for no reason the roster gave. It rang true.
