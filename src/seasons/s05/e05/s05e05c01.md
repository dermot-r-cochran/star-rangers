The ewes came down in the order they always came, the old grey one first and the two that did not get on last, and Órla counted them through the gap in the home wall at the hour she always counted them, which was a little before the light went.

Forty-three. She counted again, since a number you get once is only a number you got.

The count was the end of the day on the holding and had been for longer than there was anyone to ask. You counted them in. You saw the back gate shut on the fold behind the home pasture. You were in your own yard before dusk and not after. Her mother had done it, and her mother's mother, and none of them had ever stood at the back gate when the light was going to see what the hurry was about. It was a habit worth keeping regardless. Órla had said so for forty years and had not had to say more.

The letter was on the dresser, under the blue jug. It had come up with the post cart two days before.

Three lines. Her daughter's letters had been three lines for some years now. This one said that she would be at the council house on the fourth day of the week with some papers that were the Union's business and none of the holding's, that she had asked for leave after and had it, four days, and that she would be met at the council house door by an escort. *It is a courtesy and not a comment. Please do not feed them.*

Órla had read the last sentence a good many times. It was the only one in the letter that sounded like the girl who had left.

She had the gap hurdled and was turning for the yard when she saw the stranger at the road gate.

The woman had not come in. She was standing on the road side of the gate with her hands behind her, in the Guard's dress, the long coat and the plain cap with nothing lettered on either, and by the set of her she had been standing there since before the count began and would have stood there through another. The holding's gate was not locked. It had never had a lock. She had simply not opened it.

Órla went down. She did not hurry, with the light as it was, but she did not dawdle either.

"Tráthnóna maith duit," the woman said. "Órla Shepherd, the flockholder? Sergeant Ní Dhuibhir, of the Highland Guard. I have the detail for the Captaen Líne. I would ask your leave to walk your ground tomorrow, before she comes."

"My leave."

"It is your ground."

Órla looked at her. She was perhaps thirty-five, with a lowland voice and good boots, and the sort of face that had been taught to give nothing away and had not altogether learned it.

"And if I said no?"

"Then I would stand four of the Guard on the road for four days, and they would see a good deal less, and I would write down that the holder preferred it." The sergeant considered. "I would sooner not. It rains on the road."

"It rains in the yard."

"I had heard that."

It was nearly dusk. Órla found that she did not want to finish this at the gate, and that the reason was the hour and not the woman.

"Come up at eight," she said. "You'll have tea before you walk anything. I was asked not to feed you, and I have decided that tea is not food."

The sergeant did not smile, but she plainly knew where one would have gone. She said the Guard would be obliged, stepped back from the gate she had not touched, and went down the road toward the townland. Órla was in her own yard before the light went.

The Guard's order came to the gate office the next morning, as a copy, for the Corps' file. Catherine Galen read it at the counter between two manifests.

Under the standing agreement the Union told the liaison what it meant to do with any Ranger on its ground, and the liaison filed it. That was the whole of her part. She had no power to amend a line, and no business offering to. The gate was where the Corps stopped. She had stood on it long enough to know that it was not painted on anything, and that it was there all the same.

*Honour escort of four, with colours. To Captaen Líne Shepherd, T., na Maoir Réalta, on personal leave. From the moment she is met at the council house door until she is returned to Ranger-cleared transit. The detail to keep the officer within sight at all hours. Sergeant Ní Dhuibhir, of the four, to command.*

It was the usual form, and she had filed it forty times. The line in it that she always read twice was the fourth.

*Within sight at all hours.* It was written at a desk in the lowlands, by an officer who would not be on the hill, and it was not unreasonable. An escort that loses sight of the person it is escorting has stopped being one. But she had seen what that line turned into, once it had been carried up a track and set down at somebody's back door. She had passed Rangers out through this gate who had gone home to a mother or a brother and come back four days later having had a guardsman at the foot of the stairs each night, correctly, at attention, because the order said *all hours* and a kitchen has hours like anywhere else. Nobody had done anything wrong. The Rangers had come back through the gate like people who had held their breath since they left it.

And she had seen it go the other way. That depended on who was named in the last line, and she had no say in who was.

She did not know this Line Captain. The notice from the Corps' side had given the name and rank, the errand and the dates, and under *leave address* a holding in the Boirinn Uplands with the same name as the officer. So it was a Ranger going home. There were not many of those. The Union had been in the Corps' record for a generation, and its people did not, on the whole, go into the Corps.

The sergeant came through the office at noon to have the dates confirmed. That was the one thing Galen had standing to confirm, and she confirmed them. The sergeant was a tidy, unhurried woman with her cap under her arm. She read the Corps' notice through, and then she did what one in ten of them did. She asked a question that was on no form.

"You see them come back. What do they need, your people, when they are on leave?"

Galen thought about how much of an answer she was entitled to give. It was not her order, and it was not her hill.

"I can't tell you how to stand your detail," she said.

"I did not ask that."

"No." It was a fair distinction, and Galen took it. "Mostly they need to be off duty. That is not a thing I can ask of you, since you will be on it. But they give back what they are given, most of them, near enough to the grain. If they are given a wall, they will be a wall for four days, and very good at it."

The sergeant took that in without any sign that it had been useful. Galen had expected none.

"The order says within sight," the sergeant said.

"Yes. I read it."

"It does not say from where."

Galen looked at her. The sergeant looked back, perfectly correct. Then she thanked the liaison for the dates, put her cap on at the door, and went out to the road.

Galen logged the confirmation and filed the order with the other forty. She would not see what the fourth line turned into. She never did. She saw them go up, and four days later she saw them come down, and whatever had been made of the order in between she read off a Ranger's shoulders in the first four seconds, at the gate. It was not much to go on. She had found it was generally enough.

::: pov orla

The sergeant drank her tea standing, in the yard, and would not come under the lintel.

"You are not on duty until my daughter is met," Órla said.

"No. But I will be, from then, and I would sooner not have sat in your kitchen first and then stood outside it. It makes the standing look like a quarrel."

That was better sense than Órla had been ready for. She took the cup back when it was empty and they walked the ground.

It took the morning. The sergeant wanted to know where the track came in and where it went out, which windows gave on what, where the dogs slept and whether they would take against four strangers in long coats. They would, Órla said, for a day. She wanted to know where the colours could stand.

"Not over my house."

"They do not go over a house. They go with the detail, and the detail is not in the house." The sergeant looked along the home wall to the road gate. "There. Outside the wall, on the road side. So that anyone coming up sees them before they see anything of yours."

"And what does anyone coming up make of that?"

"That the Union knows she is here, and that she is here by the Union's leave." The sergeant said it plainly. It was the job. "And that it is a courtesy. It is both. It is meant to be read as both."

"It's my hill," Órla said.

"Yes. That is what we are standing there to say."

Órla turned that over as they went up by the byre, and did not find the fault in it, though she looked.

Four posts by day, the sergeant thought: the road gate, the yard, the track above, and one that moved with the Captaen Líne wherever she walked, twenty paces off and not nearer. Órla said her daughter would walk the whole holding the first afternoon, because she always had. The sergeant said that twenty paces would do for a hillside. By night, two awake and two asleep, in the byre loft if the holder allowed it, which the holder did.

Then they came round the top of the home pasture, and the sergeant stopped and looked down into the fold behind it.

It was a fine morning. The pale trees stood in the hollow of the ground as they always stood, with the light on them. The sergeant did not look at the trees. She looked at the back of the house from there, the scullery door and the two small windows, all of it in plain view and none of it visible from any post she had named so far.

"The night post would be here," she said. "At the back gate. It has the whole rear of the house."

"No."

The sergeant waited.

"Nobody stands at the back gate after dusk. Nor in the fold. Nor on this side of the pasture, once the count is in." It came out flatter than Órla meant it to. "That's the rule of the house. It was the rule before the house had this roof."

"Is there a reason I should have?"

"There may be one. I was never given it. My mother wasn't either." She had said that to a few people in her life, and each time had waited for a certain look. She waited for it now. "You keep the water clear, you don't cut the pale wood, and you're in your own yard by dusk. That's all there is of it. I won't have it broken by a visitor. I wouldn't break it for my daughter, and I'll not break it for her guard."

The look did not come. The sergeant was from the lowlands, but she was of the Union, and every townland she had ever walked had some field that was not ploughed or some stone that was not shifted, and nobody she knew had ever been the better for asking why.

"The order says all hours," she said. It was not an argument. She was reading it over to herself. "Within sight, at all hours. It was written by my captain, who is a careful man, and has not seen your back gate."

"Then he'll want to know why you weren't at it."

"He will read it in my note. I write the ground up tonight and it goes down in the morning, and anyone may see it." She walked back along the top wall, slowly, looking at the house. At the gable end she stopped. From there the scullery door could be seen, and one of the two windows, and the path from the back of the house to the yard. The fold could not be seen at all.

"Here," she said. "By night. It has the door. It does not have the gate, and it does not have the fold."

"So if she went down into the fold after dusk, your man would lose her."

"Would she?"

"No," Órla said. "She knows the rule. She had it before she could walk."

"Then the Guard will have her within sight at all hours," said Sergeant Ní Dhuibhir. "And the Captaen Líne will keep the hours. I will put it in those words."

She found a flat stone in the top wall's footing and set it on end against the gable, where a pair of boots would go. Órla watched her do it. She had been ready, since the letter, for four days of strangers who would do exactly as they were told in her yard. She had not been ready for one who would read her order on the ground and find that it had room in it.

"Your captain may send it back," she said.

"He may. Then I will stand at the back gate myself, by day, and he may come up and stand there by night." A pause, of the length the face allowed. "I do not think he will. It rains."

The detail went down to the council house on the fourth morning, in step, with the colours cased until the door. Galen watched them go from the office window. Four long coats, the sergeant's at the front. Her ground note had come through the gate office the day before, for the file, with her captain's initial at the foot and no query.

Galen had read it. It was hers to read and not hers to remark on. Three pages, most of it tracks and windows. One paragraph she had gone back over. *Back fold and back gate: by the holder's rule not entered after the evening count. Reason not given and not asked. Night post set at the north gable, which has the rear door. The Guard keeps the officer within sight; the officer, being of the house, keeps its hours.*

She did not know what was in the fold. It was not her business. The note did not know either, and said so in five words, and had gone up to a captain and come back signed.

The down craft was on the board for the half hour. In it, by the manifest, were a Line Captain, a sealed cache for the mediation council, and a kit bag. Galen would see her through the gate to the council house, in uniform, on the errand the invitation covered, and that would be all the gate saw of her until the fourth morning after.

What she was met with at the far door, Galen would not see. A wall, or four people who had been told where to stand by somebody who had walked the ground. She thought she knew which. She had thought so before, and had been wrong perhaps one time in five.

She would find out at the gate, in four days, in four seconds.

She squared the two manifests and logged the hour. Then she went out to the line where the Corps stopped, to wait for the craft.
