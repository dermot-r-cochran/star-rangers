The notice came down with the forenoon summary on the second day, and Lindahl read it at the watch desk, since the forenoon was his.

It was a line notice, on the line's form, and it was short.

*Threshold Station, Dock Seven. Field Triumvirate convened 0410, carried, log open. Aperture closed. Sufficient Cause not recovered. Eleven taken off in forty-one minutes, by shuttle and by courier under charter. All eleven fit. Line Captain Shepherd, T.: position logged at the failure point at the moment of closure. No further entry.*

He read it twice. He did not turn it over. He had done that once with a sheet that had her name on it, and there had been nothing on the back of that one either.

He had written a great many state sheets. Under *People* a sheet took a number and a condition, and the conditions were few and he knew them all. This notice gave eleven people a condition. It gave the twelfth a position and a moment, and stopped. The line had a word for an officer who was lost and a form to put it on, and whoever had made this out had used neither. They had written what a station's log held and had put nothing on top. He did not know who that was. He knew the habit.

A line notice went on the board by the hatch, where the watch read it coming on and going off. He put it there himself. He did not say anything about it. Six instruments agreed with one another, as they had every ninety seconds of the night, and he entered that.

The watch read it at the turn. Nobody spoke for about as long as it takes to read a bill. Then the Deputy, who had come with the spring stores and had known one Starwarden, asked who Shepherd, T. was.

"The dead Star—" said the Officer beside him, and stopped.

She had been the Deputy on a middle watch two autumns before, with a Senior on the other side and a new Starwarden between them reading the agreements. She stood there with the rest of the word in her mouth.

"She had the post before this one," the Officer said. "Before you came."

That was all anyone said. They went to their places.

The watch had called every one of them that. It had begun, each time, on the morning the hull cleared, and nothing had ever been meant by it. A Starwarden who went up did not write back to the post, and the post did not write after, and the watch had wanted a short word for an officer who was perfectly well and was not to be asked. It had been a good word. It had lasted through four of them in his time and had hurt nobody. It had lasted until the turn of that watch, and he did not think anyone would be able to use it again for her. Whether they could use it for the next one was not a thing he could tell from a desk.

He did not give them another. It was not his to give, and he did not have one.

What he did have was the log. He read summaries, his own and the line's, and he remembered what was in them. The notice had come down the line, to the posts and hulls that answered to it. That was what a line notice was. In the spring of her first year the stores hull had brought one sheet under a Frontier Corps frank, and the same week a request had gone up the relay in her own name and rank, in clear, for a file of that corps with its dissents. He had read it in the evening summary and had not asked. *Kane, G.* A Chief Ranger, by the frank. The Frontier Corps rode circuits. It answered to nobody on this line, and its post went by pouch, to the ends of passages.

So there was an officer she had written to, and sent for a file of, and the notice would reach him when his own corps got round to it, in a bag, some weeks on. Or it had reached him already. Lindahl could not tell which from here. A man told twice had lost nothing by it.

He worked it through, and then again. The post did not write after a Starwarden who had gone up. He had kept to that since her hull cleared and had not sent her a line, and he was not proposing to send her one now. This was a notice, passed to a desk that might not have had it, by a Chief who had read it. It came out the same both times.

He made it out in his own name and rank. The notice he copied as it stood, down to its last three words. Above it he put what the post's log held, the pouch and the request with their dates, and that he did not know whether the Chief Ranger's office was on the line's distribution. He read it over for anything of his own and found the word *regret*, which he had not noticed writing, and took it out. It was not that he did not. The notice had not said it, and he was sending the notice.

It went up at the end of his watch, in clear, and was logged at each desk it crossed. It would be in the evening summary, where the Starwarden read everything. Anyone who read summaries would know what the Chief of a quiet approach had sent, and to whom.

He let that stand.

:::pov galen-kane

The year's rosters were due at the turn of the season. Kane was at them, longhand, with his own name not yet written against anything, when the clerk put a relay form on the corner of the desk and did not wait.

It was not from his corps. The heading gave a post by its number, on a line he had never served, and a name he did not know. *Lindahl, E., Chief Ranger, general operations.*

He looked for a crossing-out before he read a word. It was habit, and a relay form has none. That was one of the things he held against them.

The Chief had written five lines. He did not know whether this office was on his line's distribution. The notice was below as it had reached his post, with nothing changed. Starwarden Shepherd had held the post from the late autumn of 2829. In her time its log held one pouch under a Frontier Corps frank, with the first stores hull of the spring, and her request to the Corps library the same week for a file of Kane's, with dissents, sent in clear in her own name. That was all the post held.

Then the notice.

He read it through, and then he read the Chief's five lines again. He was a slow reader of anything that mattered, and he had found that the top of a page usually said how the rest was to be taken.

The Chief had not said he was sorry. He had not said what the notice meant, or what the post had thought of her, or what Kane must be feeling. He had said what his log held and where it stopped. Kane had spent thirty years in a corps that kept cases for courts not yet founded, and he knew a docket handed over clean.

She had sent for the file. That was the second thing. He had told her where it was and which end to begin at, and she had asked for it within the week, up the relay in clear, where every desk between her post and the library could read what a new Starwarden wanted with the longest passage of a Frontier Chief. She had not told him she had. He would not have asked. Whether she had begun with the dissents was not on this form, and it was not a thing he had ever been going to be told.

The third thing was the notice, and he took it last because he had the least idea what to do with it.

In thirty years of dockets he had entered people as present, as absent, as held, and a few times as dead. He had never entered anyone as a place. *Position logged at the failure point at the moment of closure.* It was exact. It said where a station had last been able to say she was, and when, and it made no claim about the moment after. He could not have improved it and he did not think he could have written it.

He put it in his own book, under the date, in the notice's words. He would have noted it in any case.

His closures were in that book, each under its time, and each had a number. He did not give this one a number. The numbers were for mornings when a hall full of people had watched the thing happen and could all have said what it was.

Then he sent for the log. He made the request out in his own name and rank, in clear: *Shepherd, T., Line Captain. Field Triumvirate, Dock Seven. With dissents.* It was very nearly her wording. He had it from the Chief's five lines, and he saw no way to better it.

It took two days. He did rosters.

When it came he began at the end, as he had told her to begin with his.

*Dissent: none. Entered by the convening officer.*

A log with no dissent has, as a rule, no entry for one. Nobody writes down an objection that was not made. This one had the line, with the time against it, so that a reader who came looking for the argument would find that somebody had looked before him and had said so. He read it for some while. He had told one officer in his life to start from the dissents.

Then he read it forwards. Three conditions, and each was made to stand before the next was taken up, so that a man who wished to knock it over would have to say which leg. Two officers he did not know. A statement, in as many words, of what she was not acting for and had no standing to act for. He did not hold a boundary certificate and he could not read the part about the aperture. He could read the rest. It was the question he had been useful to her for, whether a record was complete or only defensible, and nobody had put it to him this time. He answered it anyway. It was complete.

He had written to her once that her corps would not have given her a clause. He had been wrong, and it was on the desk. This one had been set down beforehand, and not by her. It wanted two other officers, and it wanted the log run in the open while they decided, and she had given it all of that. The three things he trusted were in it.

But it was a clause for going in. His corps had written with great care how a Ranger is made to leave, and nothing about how he comes to be there, since the colonists settle that before he lands. Hers had done it the other way about.

The log stopped where the Triumvirate carried. What came after was in the notice. Forty-one minutes, and eleven people who had left because she told them to, and were fit. That was a leaving, and a sound one. For the twelfth there was a place and a moment. The notice did not say what order the twelfth had been under. She was the only officer there who could have given it.

He tried his three things against that, since they were what he had. Nothing had been set down for it beforehand that he could find. Nobody but her had said it. And of all the people the notice counted, not one had been standing where it could be seen. He had told her he never trusted a leaving that lacked one of the three. He had no rule for a thing that lacked all of them, and he was not sure that *leaving* was its name. His corps had arranged never to need one.

He knew a little about an order that is nobody's to give but your own. It was in the library with its dissents. He did not go into it at the desk.

*Ask again when you have a case in front of you. I answer at the ends.* He had written that from the back of a hall, with six days to wait for a hull and nothing to do in them. She had had a case in front of her at ten past four in the morning, with a log running, and no six days.

He took a sheet from his own book and wrote *Shepherd,* and stopped. The next thing a letter wants is somewhere to go. He had a position and a moment.

He did not tear the sheet out. He left it where it was, with the name and the comma.

To the Chief he wrote three words, *received as sent*, and his name. It was all he could sign to. He had received it, and it had been sent as the Chief said. What it came to, neither of them had been told.

Then he drew the rosters back across the desk and wrote his own name against the long passage, as he did every year, and went on down the list.

The acknowledgement was in the forenoon summary on the sixth day.

*Received as sent. Kane.*

He read it at the desk. He had handed over a great many sheets with three printed words under his initial and a space beside them, and officers had signed that space on the sill, with a bag still in the other hand. One had left it empty and told him why. This man had not used the form's words. He had written his own, and they claimed a little less, and Lindahl could find nothing in them that the man had not been in a position to check.

He filed it with the copy of what he had sent. There was no query and no note. He had not looked for one.

The autumn drill of the withdrawal bill had fallen due on the fourth day. He had not asked to have it moved, and the Starwarden had not moved it. Line one was given at the office hatch at ten. The hatches were shut in their order and the logs were carried, and the parties stood in the lock, with the watch at the desk last. Fifty-three minutes, by the post's clock. He entered it under the others. Nobody on the watch set the figure beside the one on the board. He knew each of them had. Eleven people off a hull is not a post, and the two numbers measured nothing in common, and he did not say so, because nobody had said the other thing aloud either.

The Starwarden hung the bill back on its hook. It had forty lines, as it had when Lindahl first read it, and there was nothing written under the first.

At the turn the Deputy came by the desk, for no reason the bill gave. He had the agreement log open at a date two autumns back, which he had no call to be reading, and Lindahl did not ask him why he was.

"The Officer says Shepherd read all of these. Every one, the whole watch."

Not *the Line Captain*. That was a rank she had never worn through this lock. Not *the Starwarden*, which was somebody else now. The watch had been without its word for four days and had not found another, and somewhere in the four days it had taken to using her name. It had not done that before for one who had gone up. A name said who she was. It said nothing about how she was, and a watch could say it at any hour without having decided anything.

"It is in the log," Lindahl said.

The Deputy looked down at the page he was holding. A hundred and sixty agreements, and one initial at the close.

"Yes, Chief."

He took it back to its place. Lindahl wrote up the forenoon: the hour, six instruments in agreement throughout, nothing inside the approach. He put his initial under it and nothing else.
