The evacuation took forty-one minutes, or thirty-eight, or on one occasion an hour and six.

She had the figures because she had started writing them down. There was nothing to write on. She kept them the way she had kept a watch bill as a cadet, before she was trusted with the slate: in order, said over, with the last one said twice.

It had begun with the lane. Below the house the lane went down between two walls to the road, and it had done that every day of her childhood without once being remarked on. On most mornings it still did. On some it did not. She would come to the place where the walls ran out, and step through the gap where the road should have been, and be standing on the bridge of the *Sufficient Cause* with the boundary going along its grain twenty kilometres off and eleven people still aboard.

She did not find it frightening to arrive there. She noted that, as she had noted it on the day. What she found was that her hands were already moving, and that she was partway through an order.

"Shuttles first. Full loads. No personal effects."

The first time, she had taken it for memory. It had the detail of one. The science department went out with the instrument logs, because the logs were the only part of the day anyone would be able to argue about afterwards. The couriers stood off where three Farline hulls had stood off. Her Archwarden took a direct order and then a second one. It ran forty-one minutes and she was alone on the bridge at the end of it, and she did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said hold. She held. Then there were more doors than a ship has, and then the grey came up over the wall, and her mother said she was up early.

The second time it ran thirty-eight minutes, and the Archwarden went on the first order.

She had let that pass. Three minutes was inside what a tired officer misremembers. She was not sure she was tired. She was not sure the word applied.

The third time the arithmetic came out the other way.

She worked it standing at the plot, as she had, with the edge of the aperture on one side of the sum and four shuttles and three couriers on the other. The margin was there. It was not a large margin. It was large enough that a Line Captain who stayed aboard to widen it would have been staying for some reason other than the sum. She checked it. It was still there. So she went down to the lock and out on the last lift, sitting on a bench in the *Ninth Draft* between a surveyor and a crate of logs. The hulls cleared on their own, all of them. Nobody on the courier's deck would look at her, and she understood why. The grey came up over the wall anyway.

After that she kept the list.

There was a version in which the Combine's operations lead answered her question. *Not permitted,* he said, and consulted somebody, and came back and closed the aperture himself inside the hour. In that one she convened nothing. She wrote a finding in four lines and the day ended in paperwork. There was a version in which the Archwarden refused the second order as well, and stayed, and the bridge had two officers on it at the end and neither of them said anything about it.

There was a version in which the relay was live.

She had known on the day that it was not. That had been the third condition, and she had said it slowly, on the log: waiting for the chain of command would cost the thing the decision was meant to save. The High Captain was eleven hours out. In this version the High Captain was on the channel, in a voice with no lag in it. *Countermand nothing,* it said. *I have the boundary desk. Go on.* She gave the rest of the order with that at her back, and it was the lightest of all the times she gave it, and she wrote that down too.

And there was one she had reached once and did not go back to. She kept it on the list as a number. The number of people in it who were fit at the end was not eleven.

Six endings, then, that she could tell apart, and the grey after each. She tried to order them and could not. None would yield first place to any other.

She set the finding out for herself as she would have set it out for a board.

*The marker is the order. State whether it resolves.*

It did not. That much she had expected, since nothing on the hill did. What she had not expected was which part would fail to hold still. The minutes varied. She could have stood that. The officers varied. But the arithmetic varied, and the arithmetic was the order. She had not held the aperture's edge because it was brave. She had held it because the sum said the hulls would not clear otherwise, and she had never in her life given an order she could not defend afterwards.

This was afterwards. She had the order, and six sums.

She was aware of the easy reading and she looked at it squarely. Two of the six let her live. One let her off the convening altogether. A mind with nothing to work on but its own wanting would be expected to produce exactly those. She could strike them on that ground and keep the one in which she had stayed.

She did not strike them. To trust the version in which she had paid most, because she had paid most, was a line of reasoning she had seen before on other people's paperwork. It made a very handsome document. It was not arithmetic either.

The log would have settled it. She went back up the lane on the next morning that offered the bridge, and gave the order, and when the bridge was empty she went to the log before she went to the plot. It carried the Triumvirate. It carried three conditions, each made to stand before she took the next. It carried the lifts, by load. For the last sum it carried, on that morning, one line in her own hand, and on the morning after, nothing, and on a third morning a different line.

She could not remember writing one. She thought she had not. There had been nobody left to write it for.

That left her with an order given to one officer, by the same officer, with no second and no log, on working she could not now reproduce. She had sat on boards. She knew what a board did with an order like that.

She went back down to the house to find somebody to say it to.

::: pov wender

Shepherd made the report in the yard, standing, to a High Captain sitting on an upturned feed bin.

The made Wender had not chosen the bin. She found herself where Shepherd needed her, and this morning that was the yard, in a graphite jacket that had never been near a sheep. She had stopped minding it. Nobody chose the conditions of a post. A hen went past her boot. She let it.

It was a good report. Six endings, told apart by what differed. The minutes. The officers. The sum. The log, which carried the last decision three ways on three mornings. One ending held back, with its number given and nothing else. Shepherd went through it in the flat voice and stopped where it stopped.

Then she asked for the one thing the made Wender had been waiting, for some time, to be asked for.

"I want it read," Shepherd said. "By somebody who taught the doctrine it was given under. I gave an order on working I can't recover. I'd like to know if it was sound."

"No."

Shepherd waited. She was good at that. The made Wender had been built by somebody who knew exactly how good.

"You want it countersigned," the made Wender said. "You'd take it from a board if you had a board. You haven't, so you've come to me. And I'm made of what you know about me, Tiss. Every bit. If I say it was sound, that's your initial on your own order, twice, in two inks. You'd fail a Senior for bringing you that."

"I'd fail her for presenting it as a second signature. I'm not presenting it. I'm asking what you'd say."

"That's the same thing with better manners."

A dog barked somewhere below the house, or the hill produced a bark. Shepherd did not turn her head. The made Wender noticed that she had stopped turning her head for things, lately, and filed it as a change and not as a loss.

"The relay," Shepherd said. "In one of them you were on it."

"I heard."

"You said to countermand nothing."

"She was eleven hours out." The made Wender used the pronoun without difficulty. She had settled some time ago which of them was *she*. "You knew that when you convened. You said it on a log. That was your third condition, that waiting for her would cost the thing. You were right, and you don't get to bring it to her now because it's gone quiet enough to ask."

"I'm not bringing it to her."

"No. You're bringing it to the nearest thing." It was not said hard. "I don't know what she'd have said. You don't either. I know you'd like to. It's on your list as the lightest of the six, and you wrote that down, which is the only reason I'm still sitting here. Leave it on the list. You don't strike a reading because you'd have liked it, and you don't keep one for that either."

Shepherd took that. The made Wender watched it go in, once for what it said and once for what it was for, as everything went into that woman.

There was a thing the real one would have done here. The made Wender knew it, and knew that she knew it only because Shepherd did. The real one would not have asked what her officer was carrying. She would have asked what was currently known. She had built four years of a command on the difference.

So the made one asked that.

"What's currently known. Not on the day. Now, standing where you're standing."

"That an order was given. I have that every time. That the working behind it can't be reproduced here. That here isn't an instrument."

"And the order itself. Sound or not."

"Not established." Shepherd said it slowly. "Not from inside."

"And."

A pause. The made Wender let it run. She would not supply the other half. It had to come from the officer whose order it was, or it was the citation again in a feed yard.

"Not disestablished either," Shepherd said. "I've six sums. None of them is the one I did. I can't show the one I did was wrong any more than I can show it was right."

"Then that's the entry. It's a poor entry. I've signed off worse from officers who'd lost less." The made Wender got up off the bin. "You keep going down that lane to see how it ends. How it ends was never in the order. You didn't have the ending when you gave it. Nobody does. An order is correct given what's known at the time, and you've been trying to mark it against six things that weren't known by anybody."

"I was trying to check the arithmetic."

"I know what you were trying to do. It's what I'd have done." She almost left it there. "You said no second. No log. Nobody else on the bridge."

"Yes."

"You did that sum more than once. I know you. Why did you do it again?"

Shepherd did not answer. Her face did what it did when a forty-page bulletin turned out to have something on page thirty-one.

The made Wender did not know the answer. She had not been given it. Whatever it was, it was something Shepherd had never put where a High Captain could see it, and so it was not in the stuff the made one was built from. She found that she could tell where the gap was by the shape of what stood round it, and that she was not going to be told.

She did not ask.

"Go on, then," she said. "I'll mind the hen."

::: pov tissadelle

She had done it again because she was told to.

*Do the arithmetic again.* He had said it on the bridge, in the tone he used for a thing that had only been got once. She had done it, and it had come out the same, and she had not thought of it since as anything but the sum.

It was a second. It was the only one the order had ever had. A Line Captain had given herself an order, and before she acted on it somebody had required her to show the working twice, and had watched it come out. Arithmetic done once was an opinion. That was not her rule. She had borrowed it from him years ago and had long since stopped noticing where she had got it.

She stood at the gap where the walls ran out, with the road on the other side of it this morning, and reached inward.

*Tobble. The sum on the bridge. Did it come out the same.*

She listened for some time.

She could hear *yes*. She could hear it in his voice, with the small weight he put on a figure he had seen twice. She could also hear herself supplying it. The two were not distinguishable. She had known they would not be before she asked, and she had asked anyway, because a witness is owed the question whether or not the answer can be used.

So she had it complete now, and she set it out.

*Order: given. Working: not recoverable from here. Log: none that holds. Second: one. Not presently confirmable. Soundness: not established, not disestablished.*

It was the worst entry she had ever made about an order of her own. Every line of it was true. She read it over for anything that claimed more than she had, and found nothing, and let it stand.

She noticed what it did not have. It did not have a line for how the thing ended. She had gone down that lane more times than she could count, to six different ends, and not one of them had found its way into the entry. There was nowhere in it they could have gone.

On the next morning the walls ran out onto the bridge again. She had half expected that they would stop, now that she had no question to take there. They did not stop. The boundary was going along its grain, and eleven people were aboard, and her hands were already moving.

She could have turned round. The gap was behind her and the lane was in it.

But there were eleven people aboard. They were made of logs and memory and of whatever she had left, and they did not know that, and the boundary in front of them was going along its grain all the same. Whichever morning this was, they were owed the order.

"Shuttles first," she said. "Full loads. No personal effects."

She did not go to the plot afterwards. She did not go to the log. She stood on the bridge until the last lift was clear and did not ask how it would end.

It took forty-four minutes that time. She was pleased with it, in the small flat way she permitted herself. Forty-four was not as good as forty-one, but everybody was off.
