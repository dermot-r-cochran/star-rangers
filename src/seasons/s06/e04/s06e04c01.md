::::: scene 1

::: pov dorrit-hale

*Cause (select one).*

The work order had been closed four times, and every time the same box had been ticked. Hale read the history on the walk out along the harbour wall, with her kit on one shoulder and the rain coming in flat off the water.

*Dock Seven, outer gallery. Complaint: gallery warm. Cause: heat exchange. Action: valve replaced. Closed.* That was the first, on the twenty-first day. It had come back in a week. *Cause: heat exchange. Action: outlet sensor replaced. Closed.* Six days. *Cause: heat exchange. Action: loop rebalanced, setpoint verified. Closed.* Nine. The fourth technician had replaced nothing. He had run the loop for an hour, written *within tolerance on departure*, ticked the box and gone to lunch. She did not hold it against him. A form that asks for one of eight causes will be given one.

She had not had the order before. It had come to her because it had been round everyone else on the section.

Dock Seven was the furthest out. The gallery ran along its seaward side, forty paces of bare deck with one bench, and a viewport the whole length of it, and nothing else. Nobody had ever fitted it with instruments. It had a robot in it. The robot stood at the glass near the far end with one hand flat against the pane, and it turned its head when she came in and then turned it back.

She knew what it was. The Constabulary's analyst had been standing in this gallery, on and off, since the autumn. People on the section said it was grieving. Hale thought that people on the section said a good deal.

"Maintenance," she said. "I've the heat exchange."

"The panel is behind the bench," the robot said. "The previous four used it."

She did the exchanger first, because it was on the order and because she would not write a word about anything else until she had. It took an hour and ten minutes. Inlet, outlet, flow, the new valve, the new sensor. She put her own probe in beside the station's sensor and the two agreed to a tenth. She cycled the loop and watched it settle. It settled like a diagram.

It was in order. It was better than in order. With a new valve, a new sensor and a rebalance in thirty-six days, it was very likely the best-kept heat exchanger on Threshold Station, and there had never been anything the matter with it.

Hale closed the panel and stood up, and then she did the part that was not on the order. She took the hand thermometer off her belt and walked the gallery with it.

At the panel it read setpoint. At ten paces, setpoint. At twenty. She walked slowly and let the reading settle at each stop, since a thermometer hurried is a thermometer guessing. At thirty paces it read setpoint.

At about thirty-six it read a degree and a half over.

She stopped. She stepped back one pace. Setpoint. She stepped forward. A degree and a half, coming on over the length of a stride.

She had expected a slope. Warm air from a fault spreads, and thins as it spreads, and a technician can walk up the slope to whatever is making it. This did not thin. Nothing was upstream of it. The ducts ended at the bench.

She put the thermometer against the glass. The glass was cold. It was late autumn, and the sea was on the other side. She put it on the deck. Setpoint. She held a strip of sealing film up by one corner and watched it hang straight down, and there was no draught.

Then she got the second thermometer out of the kit, the old one with the scratched face that she trusted, and did the whole walk again from the panel.

It came on at thirty-seven paces that time, and it read a degree and two tenths.

So the air at the far end of the gallery was warmer than the air at the near end, and warmer than the glass beside it, and it was not the exchanger. She had two instruments saying so and neither had said it the same way.

She looked at the robot. It was standing four paces inside the warm, with its hand on the cold glass.

"You've been here weeks," she said. "Is it the exchanger?"

"I have no instrument for a heat exchanger. Yours reads it in order."

"That's what I make it."

"Then that is what it is," the robot said.

She waited to see if it would go on. It did not. She wrote down her two readings and the times, and the paces, and went back along the wall to get something to eat.

**Field Analyst Rook-7. Log open. Dock Seven, outer gallery, fifty-seventh day. A fifth technician attended the heat-exchange work order between 1005 and 1150 station. Logging the attendance. The previous four are also logged.**

Rook-7 had assessed on the nineteenth day that the maintenance crews were wrong about the heat exchange. It had reached the assessment with non-standard arrays and eleven prior cases. The fifth technician had reached the same place in under two hours with two hand thermometers, one of them scratched.

**Note. The comparison does not flatter the arrays. Logging it regardless.**

She had asked a direct question, and Rook-7 had given the part of the answer that rested on her instruments. It had not given the part that rested on its own. Its assessment of Dock Seven was flagged as interpretation, at low confidence, for the High Captain and no one above her. A technician was not above her. Rook-7 had examined whether that settled the matter and found that it did not, quite. The flag said who the assessment was for. It did not say what a technician was owed.

What she was owed, Rook-7 assessed, was an entry that would stand. An entry that said *warm, exchanger in order, source not found* stood on two thermometers anybody could pick up. An entry that said *warm, and the Constabulary's analyst believes a process is running at the failure point* stood on arrays whose origin had been flagged twice and asked about zero times. The first could be checked by the next technician. The second could only be believed or not.

**Assessment: withholding the interpretation left her finding stronger than sharing it would have. Rook-7 logs that this is also the more comfortable course, and that the two being the same course does not make it wrong. Flagging for own review.**

She came back at 1340 with a sealed cup and the work order on a slate. She did not open the panel. She sat on the end of the bench with the slate on her knee and did not write on it.

At 1352 the dock hand came in.

Rook-7 knew him. Lund, I., cargo handling, outer docks. He was one of the nine who had stood in this gallery on the morning and given statements at 0549. He had not wept. He had asked Rook-7 a question, and Rook-7 had said, *I don't know*. He had been on the gallery, off shift, on thirty-one of the fifty-seven days since. He sat at the far end of the bench, which was inside the warm by about a pace. He had always sat there. He brought a flask.

He looked at the technician's kit and then at the closed panel.

"Is it broken again?"

"It was never broken," the technician said.

"They've had it open four times."

"I know. I've read them."

He considered that. He was a large man, and he was careful with the bench, which was not built for two.

"So what's warm?"

"I don't know."

Rook-7 logged the three words and the time.

**Note. This is the second occasion on this gallery on which a person asked a question about Dock Seven and was given those words. On the first, Rook-7 gave them. It notes that it has waited fifty-seven days to hear somebody else do it, and had not known it was waiting.**

"I was here," Lund said. "On the day. There were nine of us at this glass."

"I heard."

"You'll have heard what we said."

"I heard a word."

"Dragons." He said it flatly. He had been asked to say it a number of times, by people with slates, and it had not changed. "I said it then and I'd say it now. I don't know what else a man would call it. I'm not asking you to believe it."

"I'm not asking you to call it anything else," the technician said. "I've a thermometer. It doesn't do dragons. It does a degree and a half, about there." She pointed with the slate at the deck under his boots.

He looked down at them.

"I thought it was me," he said. "I thought I just liked this end."

"It might be both."

Rook-7 observed that neither of them had asked the other to move toward anything. The technician had a reading and no account of it. The dock hand had an account and no reading. For most of an hour they shared a bench on that basis. In fifty-seven days of logging the inquiry file Rook-7 had not seen two parties hold their positions that close together without one of them leaning.

At 1431 Lund asked the other question. He did not ask Rook-7. He had asked Rook-7 once.

"The ones who came. Whatever they were. All of ours got home and none of theirs did. You'd think that meant something."

"I do heat exchangers," the technician said.

"I know you do. I'm asking anyway."

She took some time over it.

"I don't know that either," she said. "I'd want to. I'd want it badly, if I'd stood here. That's about the only thing I'm sure of, and it isn't a reading."

He nodded. He seemed to Rook-7 less disappointed than he had been at 0549, though Rook-7 had no instrument for that. It logged that he poured the cap of the flask full and put it on the bench between them, and that she took it.

**Observation. The true answer to his question is long. Rook-7 filed it on the day, and it has been read zero times. It would not have helped him then. Rook-7 assesses it would not help him now. The technician's answer was shorter, and it claimed nothing, and he took it. Rook-7 logs that it does not fully understand why he could.**

**Log held open.**

::::: scene 3

::: pov dorrit-hale

The form would not take a blank.

She tried it. She filled in the test of the exchanger, every figure, and attached the trace of the loop settling. She filled in *Action: none required.* Then she left *Cause* empty and pressed to file, and the slate gave the field a red border and waited.

Eight causes. Wear. Calibration. Installation. Heat exchange. Duct. Sensor. Setpoint. External damage. Hale read down them twice. She had a warm patch of air with nothing behind it, and none of the eight was that.

She could see how it had gone, four times. A technician stood out here with a sound exchanger and a warm gallery and a red border, and the nearest word was fourth on the list. Nobody had lied. They had each picked the closest of eight and then, being honest, done some work to go with it. That was how a sound exchanger came to have a new valve.

There was a free-text line under the box. It was meant for part numbers. She wrote in it.

*No mechanical cause identified. Exchanger tested and in order; test attached. Air at the outer end of the gallery, last four paces or so, above setpoint: 1.5 at 1118, 1.2 at 1141. Glass colder than air. No draught. Source not found. Recommend the order stay open and the exchanger be left alone.*

She read it over. It did not say what the warm was. She had nothing that would let her say, and she found she did not much want to guess.

Then she filed it with the box empty. The slate filed it as *open, incomplete*, and put a mark beside it that would show on the section's sheet at the end of the week.

Her section lead came out along the wall at four. He did not send for her. He walked it, in the rain, which she gave him credit for. He had the sheet with him.

"You've left Seven open."

"Yes."

"Four people closed it."

"Four people mended an exchanger that wasn't broken. If I close it there'll be a fifth, and they'll have to find something to replace. There's not much left."

He looked at the panel, and then down the gallery to where the robot was, and the bench.

"An open order sits on my sheet every week until it's shut. Somebody upstairs reads that sheet."

"I know."

"What do I tell Operations when they ask why Seven's gallery is warm?"

"That it's warm. That it isn't the plant. I can tell them where it starts, near enough, and that it doesn't start in the same place twice."

He was quiet for a bit. He was not a man who liked an untidy sheet, and she had worked for him long enough to know it was because he had once been blamed for somebody else's. He read her free-text line. He read it again.

"Two readings," he said.

"Two thermometers."

"If it's going to sit on my sheet, it's going to sit there with more than two. Take it morning and evening for the week. Times, paces, both instruments. If anyone asks me, I want to be able to hand them a page." He gave her back the slate. "And put a tag on that panel. *Do not service against this order.* Somebody will, otherwise. It's on the list."

He walked back along the wall without waiting for an answer. She had not had one ready.

She tagged the panel. Then, since she was there and it was nearly evening, she took the first of the week's readings.

Lund had gone on shift. The bench was empty, with a ring on it where the flask had stood. The robot was at the glass, and outside the rain had stopped, and there was nothing on the water.

Thirty-six paces. One and four tenths on the new thermometer. One and three on the old.

She wrote them under the others, with the time. Four readings, and no two alike.

Behind the bench the exchanger ticked over, in perfect order. She left it alone.
