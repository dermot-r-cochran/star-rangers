::: pov tissadelle

She had never taken the reading, so she set out to take it now.

It was the oldest fault in survey work and she had made it. An instrument is read when it does something. Nobody writes down what it shows on the days it shows nothing, and then one day a silence turns up and there is no page to hold it against. She had known Tobble for longer than she had known how to file a report. She could have described his voice to a board. She could not have described the quality of an hour in which he was there and saying nothing. She had never needed to.

She needed to now, and she went about it as she would have at a desk. The first step was to find one case where she knew he was there, and one where she knew he was not, and set them side by side.

She had neither. She had a great many mornings, and in every one of them the quiet after his name was the same quiet.

So she tried to make a case.

The plain test was a question with an answer she did not have. She thought about that for most of a morning, on the wall, with the flock moving below the house, and it came apart in her hands. Anything she could think to ask, she could think to answer. If she asked him how many chronometers hung on the wall of Dock Seven, she would know it before the question was finished, and then *two* would arrive in his voice, and she would have learned nothing except that she remembered a wall.

She tried the other way round. She would ask for something she had never known. What had he made of her board at Chief, the long afternoon of it, when he had said nothing for hours and then told her at the end? She had his account of that. But the hours themselves, what he had been thinking while he said nothing, she had never had. If an answer came with something in it she had not possessed, that would be a reading.

She put the question. She waited.

What came was plausible. It was in his manner. He had been counting the board's water glasses, it suggested, and had got the figure twice. She turned it over. It was exactly the sort of thing he did. It was also exactly the sort of thing she knew he did. A person who had known him thirty-one years could have produced it without him, and she was such a person.

She saw then that it was a general result. A plausible answer proved nothing, because plausible was what she could make. An implausible one would prove less. She would only conclude that the hill had got him wrong.

She tried, last, the thing he was best at. She asked him for a number.

*The top course of the wall. How many stones.*

A count was not a remark. A count could be checked. If he gave her a figure, and she then counted, and the figure was right, and she had not known it beforehand, that would hold.

Nothing came.

She did not count the course herself. That was the discipline of it: if she counted first, the number would be hers, and anything that arrived afterwards would arrive second.

She waited through that day and the next, or through one day twice. The quiet did not alter. It did not seem to her to be an empty quiet. She distrusted the impression at once, since it was the one she would have chosen.

On the third morning she gave up the test.

She did not do it bitterly. A method that cannot fail cannot find anything either, and she had run this one long enough to see that it could only ever return what she put in. She wrote that down as a result.

What she had left was smaller. She could not tell him apart from her memory of him. Very well. She could still take the reading she had come out to take, without knowing what it was a reading of. An entry does not have to say what a thing is. It can say what was observed, and when, and leave the heading blank until somebody is in a position to fill it in.

*First entry,* she said, inward, to whoever kept the count now. *Morning. Grey, not yet gold. Name said. Quiet after it. Quality of the quiet: not known. No prior entry to compare.*

It was a poor first line. First lines of a baseline always are. They only become anything by the fortieth.

::::: scene 2

::: pov tobble

Fifty-eight.

He went back to the gatepost and started again, because once was an opinion. The top course ran from the gatepost to the corner where the wall turned downhill, and he took it stone by stone, and he did not hurry. He had never hurried a count in his life.

Sixty-one.

Tobble sat down. He was where he had been since the first grey of the first morning, which was in the middle of it. He had no particular place on the hill. He did not have a place on a ship, either, when there had been a ship. He was not seen, and had never expected to be.

It was the stone by the gap. He had run it down weeks ago, if weeks were what these were. Three along from the place where the third course had shifted, there was a stone that was one long stone when he counted from the gatepost and two short ones when he counted back. That accounted for one of the three. He had not found the other two. He suspected the corner.

He had counted the course every morning. He had had fifty-eight a good many times and sixty-one a good many times, and other figures besides, and he had never had any of them twice running. So he had no number. This morning he had two opinions.

That was why he had not answered her. She had waited for a count on the first morning, at this wall, and he had known she was waiting, and he had been in the middle of the second pass and had got a different figure. A person did not report that. A person reported a number, and that he had got it twice, or kept quiet until he could.

He had been quiet a long time now. He was professionally good at it, and he did not like it at all.

*Good morning,* he had said, every morning. He was fairly sure of that. Some mornings she got his name out first and some mornings he was ahead of her. What he could not tell was what arrived. He said it. Something reached her. He had watched her hold it and turn it over and decline to put his name after it, and he could not fault her, because from where he sat it was a fair question. Everything in this place had come through him. The wall, the kettle, the gold. He had put them in with his own structure, in a great hurry, off a ship's log. A voice that came out of the same place might be him speaking, or might only be more of what he had put in. He could not think of a way to check that from his side either.

It was a thing they now had in common, and he had no means of telling her so.

He had gone over the tests before she had. He had got there some time before she did, having less to do. He knew the question with the answer she did not have. He had one.

He had opinions about Karla Wender. He had formed them over some years, and he had kept them to himself, which he almost never did with her about anything. She did not have them. She knew only that they existed, and that he was keeping them.

He could tell her now. They would arrive with something in them that she had never possessed. That was the reading she wanted.

He looked at that for a long while, with his feet under him.

It would not work. That was the first thing, and he made himself take it first, because it was arithmetic and the other thing was not. She had never had the opinions, so she had nothing to check them against. An opinion that could not be checked was only a plausible remark in his voice, and she had already found out what those were worth.

The second thing was not arithmetic. He had kept them for a reason. The reason had to do with two people and not with him, and it was a decent one. Nothing about it had changed because a ship was gone. He would be handing over a private matter between two people to prove a point about a third. It would not even be a kindness. He would be doing it to be believed.

He had never needed that from her before. He found that he wanted it now very much. He did not think the wanting gave him any licence.

So he kept them.

That left the wall.

He considered the wall. He had been treating every morning as a failed count. He had been waiting for the hill to settle, so that he could get one figure twice and report it properly. It had been a great many mornings. He was a cat who kept records, and somewhere in the night it had come to him that he had a record. It was just not the one he had been trying to get. The count did not come out. It had not come out once. This morning it was fifty-eight on the pass out and sixty-one on the pass back.

That was not a number. It was still a finding, and he had been sitting on it because it was untidy.

She had started saying entries aloud, inward, to nobody. He had heard the first one. *No prior entry to compare.* It was the sort of line he would have written, and he noticed that he could not tell whether that was a compliment to her or a fact about where he was sitting.

There was a place at the edge of the clearing where the air was warm. He knew it was there. He was a cat, and he had not gone and sat in it, not once, and he had noted that about himself as unusual and had not accounted for it. It was not his.

He got up, and went back to the gatepost, and did both passes again, so as to be sure of what he was about to say.

Then he said it.

::::: scene 3

::: pov tissadelle

*Fifty-eight. And sixty-one. I got each of them once.*

She was at the wall when it came, with the gold not yet on it, and she kept still.

It was in his voice, and it was not what she had expected. That was the first thing, and she set it down before she did anything else, because it would be the first thing lost. She had asked for a count four mornings ago, or one. She had expected a number, or nothing. She had not expected two numbers and an admission.

Tobble did not report a figure he had only got once. In thirty-one years he had not done it. He would sit on a count all day sooner than hand over an opinion with a number's coat on. Either he had changed his practice, or the hill had made a Tobble who did not keep it.

She looked at both of those. She could not choose between them.

Now she could count, since the figure had come first. She went to the gatepost and took the top course stone by stone to the corner where the wall turned downhill.

Fifty-eight.

She came back along it.

Sixty-one.

So the numbers were right, and they were hers as well. She had built this wall, or it had been built out of what she had, and anything it could tell him it could tell her. The count she had been keeping in reserve as the one clean test had come back checked, correct, and no use. She almost laughed. It would have been the first time on the hill, and she noted that too.

She stood at the corner and went through it as she would have gone through a return from a sweep.

*Received: two figures, each stated as got once. Verified: both, by own count, after receipt. Expected: no. Source: not established.*

The third line was the one she kept coming back to. *Expected: no.* She did not know what it was worth. It was not proof. A mind that has run out of tests is exactly the mind that would hand itself a surprise and call it company. She would not put more on it than it could carry.

But it was not nothing either. She had a great many mornings of *Good morning*, and none of them had told her anything, because each was what she would have supplied. This was the first line that had come from that quarter with a fault in it. He had not got it twice, and he had said so.

That was the entry, then. She added it to the baseline, under the others, with its heading still blank.

There remained the matter of what was owed. She had settled that on the first morning, and it had not changed. If he was there, he had just done something he disliked a great deal, for her. If he was not, it would cost her two words.

*Thank you,* she said.

She did not wait to see what came back. She had found that the waiting was where she did her supplying.

She went up instead to the edge of the clearing, as she did on most mornings now, and stood where the air was warm. It was warm. She did not ask it anything. She had never been able to mistake that for something of her own, and she did not know why not, and she had stopped requiring herself to know. She stood in it for a while. It was the one place on the hill where she was not keeping a count.

Then she went down to the wall and began the next entry.

*Morning. Gold on the top course. Fifty-eight stones, or sixty-one. Quiet after the name.*

She stopped there, and then added the line the form required.

*Quality of the quiet: not known. One prior entry.*

It was a poor second line as well. She kept it.
