Two papers had come in overnight, and Arashobal put them side by side on the desk before the High Captain was in, because they were one paper.

The first was from the station administration. It ran to a page and it was courteous. The Dock Seven approach had been shut to traffic for sixty days. The outer docks were working round it, at a cost in handling time which the administration gave, to the hour. The inquiry was expected to close within the week. The administration would be grateful if the approach could be returned to the traffic plan before the inquiry's people left, so that the station they left would be an ordinary one.

The second was from the outer docks. It had no letterhead. It had forty-three names, in forty-three hands, and four lines above them. The crews understood the approach might be reopened. They asked that it not be. Hulls ought not to be run through that water, they wrote, as though it were any water.

One asked for the lane open. One asked for it shut. Arashobal read them for what they stated, and then for where the stated terms ran out, since that part did not wait to be looked for. Each had been written to say what Dock Seven was. An ordinary approach. A place apart. Both had been sent to the one officer on the station who had spent sixty days declining to say.

Neither was hers to answer, either. That was the material part. Traffic Control had shut the lane on the morning, on its own authority, and Traffic Control would open it. Before it could, the pilot department had to fly an approach survey and certify the lane. That had been on the Chief Pilot's list since the second day. It had no pilot's name against it.

She had the replies drafted when Wender came in. Wender read them standing.

*The High Captain's office holds no view on what the Dock Seven approach is for. The lane is Traffic Control's. Its certification is the Chief Pilot's. Your paper has been passed to both.* The same four sentences, twice.

"Yes," Wender said. "Send them." She did not put them down. "Why is the survey sixty days unflown?"

"The Chief Pilot has not assigned it."

"That's the fact. I asked why."

"I can give what I have been told, and what I register. They are separate."

"Both."

"Told: weather, and the inquiry's calls on the department. Registered: the Chief Pilot will not order a pilot onto it. Whoever flies it flies into both of those." Arashobal indicated the papers. "A pilot who reports the lane sound has opened it, in the reading of forty-three people. One who reports a doubt has shut it, in the administration's. The survey is a short flight, and the report is three lines. The Chief Pilot judges it is not an order that can be given to a Principal as though it were only that. I think the judgement is correct."

Wender looked at the wall for a moment. It was the pilot department's wall, a deck below.

"Ask the Chief Pilot whether the list will take a relief pilot. Boundary plate current. I've kept it up."

Arashobal did not answer at once. She had no need to look for the drift. It was the first thing in the sentence.

"A High Captain states what an order is for and what it must not cost," she said. "She does not state how. You said so, in the office across the deck, when you asked me to this post. A High Captain who flies a Principal's survey has stated how, in as much detail as it can be stated. And the Chief Pilot cannot refuse you."

"No."

"Then it is not a request. It is the theatre running on what its commander would prefer. You asked me to say so first."

"I did. You have." Wender put the replies down at last. "Now tell me the terms on which it isn't that. If there aren't any, I don't fly."

It was a fair return. Arashobal took it as one, and worked it, since that was what her desk was for.

"The replies go before the flight, not after. Then nothing you do in the lane can be read as the answer to either paper. The request goes to the Chief Pilot from this office, in my name, as an offer of a plate, with one line: that a refusal will not be acknowledged or asked about. The flight is under the Chief Pilot's certification. The report goes to the Chief Pilot alone, in a pilot's form, with no rank on it. It recommends nothing. You do not see the certification before the station does."

"And I don't decide the lane."

"You were never going to decide the lane. I am arranging that it will also look that way."

Something went across Wender's face that in a human was near to laughing. Arashobal noted it.

"The form has a line for purpose," she said.

"Approach familiarisation. I haven't flown it since I held the department."

Arashobal weighed that as she weighed anything. It was a stated term, and it was true. The conduct would match it. There was something under it that had not been stated, and she could tell roughly how large it was. It was not small. It had not been put into the order, and nobody was being asked to carry it.

"That is the material part," she said.

"It's the part that goes on the form."

The Chief Pilot's answer came back in nine minutes. *Slot at 1400. Cutter Four. On my list a pilot is a plate.*

::::: scene 2

::: pov wender

She had forgotten how small a cutter was. She found she liked it.

It was a single seat and a plot and a screen, and the instruments were where she would have put them, because for some years she had been the one who decided. She ran the checks aloud, to nobody, as she had made every pilot in the department run them. The berth let go. The harbour wall went by on the left with the rain on it.

"Cutter Four, Traffic. You have the Seven approach, survey, sole use. Station time fourteen-oh-six. Reference to follow."

They gave her the second clock. They had given her both when she was a Senior, and she had been irritated by it then, as young pilots were. She read both back.

She was aware of what she was doing. It was better to have that out in the first minute than to find it at the far end. Two papers had asked her what this water meant, and she had said, correctly, that it was not hers to say. Then she had found a way to be out on it within the day. It was a clean way. Her chief of staff had built it and it would stand. But a clean route to a thing you want is still a route to it, and she did not pretend otherwise in an empty cutter.

She had the old arithmetic with her as well. Eleven hours. She had been eleven hours out when the log reached her. She had run it every day since, to see whether there was a version in which she was nearer, and it came out eleven. It always came out eleven, and that had not helped.

The wall ended. The approach began.

It was not a hard approach on paper. It was nine legs out and nine back, over shallow water, toward a dock at the end of everything. What made it a pilot's approach was the middle of it. On the fourth leg the plot and the water stopped agreeing. They always had. The plot put the cutter a little to one side of where the water plainly had it, and then, a few kilometres on, quietly took it back. No instrument was at fault. Each of them, checked alone, was sound. A pilot learned to fly the water through that stretch, and to let the plot say what it liked, and the department had failed good candidates for trusting the wrong one.

She came onto the fourth leg and her hands took the cutter off the plot before she had decided to.

It was twelve years since she had flown this, or near it. She had sat in briefings since. She had commanded the people who flew it, and then the people who commanded them. Her hands had kept it all the same. She was more pleased by that than a High Captain should have been.

The disagreement was where it had been. She marked its start and its end. It was the same width as on the last survey, to the limit of what a cutter could say. The water was grey and flat and shallow. She watched it as a survey pilot should, for anything it was doing that it had not done before. It was doing nothing.

On the sixth leg the plot showed her a mark.

Somebody in the department had entered it, in the sixty days. It was not a wreck mark. It was not a hazard mark. It was a plain position, with a time against it and no name. It lay off to the north of the lane.

Four kilometres.

She had known the figure from the chart. It was different to have it at the end of her own arm. Four kilometres was nothing. At this speed it was the lane's width a few times over, and no rule on any sheet said a survey pilot might not look at the water beside her lane. She could put the cutter over that mark and be back on the leg inside three minutes. The report would have one line in it that nobody would query.

She looked across, at the grey water and the rain.

Nothing was there. It was not that she saw an absence. There was the sea, exactly as it was on the other side of the lane and as it had been on every leg out.

The cutter carried a pilot's instruments. They read depth and set and the drift of the plot. They said the water over that mark was water. They had not been built to say anything else, and she did not ask them. Somewhere behind her, on a gallery, a robot had arrays that said the place was still ringing. Her cutter had no opinion. She was not sure which of the two she was more grateful for.

The lane did not go to the mark. She stayed with that for the length of the leg. An approach was a line to a dock, and it passed whatever lay beside it. If she turned off it now she would not be flying a survey. She would be paying a visit, in the Chief Pilot's cutter, on a form that said something else. She would also have answered both papers, in the one way she had told them she would not.

She thought she could at least slow, and did not do that either. She held the leg and the speed. The mark came abeam, and stayed abeam for about as long as it takes to read a line twice, and went behind.

She did not say anything. She had half expected that she would. There was a thing she had never said to that officer in sixteen years of knowing her. It was not a thing for a survey channel. She noted, as honestly as she noted the other, that she was relieved to find it had not come out by accident.

It was the nearest she had been in sixty days. It had not been near.

The seventh leg, the eighth. The dock came up out of the rain, low and long, with the gallery lit along its seaward side. She took the cutter past it at survey height, as the form required, and there were people at the far end of the gallery. Two of them, and the robot. One had an arm up, holding something small into the air and looking at it. The other sat on the bench with a flask. Neither looked out at the cutter. She did not know what they were doing, and it was plainly not for her.

She turned at the mole and flew it home. The fourth leg did what it did. Her hands kept the water.

The report took her less time than the checks had.

*Dock Seven approach, survey. Flown out and back, 1406 to 1511. Flyable to the standard of the last survey. Plot disagreement on leg four, as charted, extent unchanged. Nothing observed at or near the logged position, on the instruments carried, from the lane.*

She read it once for content and once for what it was for. It was for a Chief Pilot, who would certify the lane or would not. She took out *from the lane* and then put it back. It was true. It was also the only line in the report that was hers.

::: pov arashobal

The report reached the pilot department at 1519. It did not reach the staff office at all. Arashobal had arranged that, and she noted that the arrangement held.

What reached her was the station's traffic sheet, at the turn of the watch. *Cutter Four, survey, Seven approach, returned.* There was no name. On the Chief Pilot's list a pilot was a plate.

The replies had gone at nine. The administration had acknowledged, in a line. The outer docks had not, and she did not expect them to. Forty-three people who had signed in their own hands had not been writing to get an answer. She had filed the paper where the inquiry's copies went. Then she had filed a second copy in the staff's own record, under no heading. She kept poorly anything that had no present use. She had learned to do it deliberately, on the days it seemed to matter to the people around her.

Wender came up from the berth at half past three with rain on her collar. She did not sit.

Arashobal read her, as she read anybody who came through the door. The term had been approach familiarisation, and the conduct had matched it. There was no drift. What had been under the term this morning was still under it. It was the same size. It had not been spent on anything, and it had not been set down.

"It flew," Wender said.

"I have the traffic sheet. I do not have the report."

"No. You fixed that."

"Was it familiarisation?"

A Krenyi's questions came out. Arashobal had long since stopped apologising for it, and Wender had never asked her to.

"Yes. Leg four is where it was. My hands had it." Wender looked at the window. "The lane passes the position at four kilometres. I stayed in the lane."

Arashobal considered whether more was owed. A human would have added something there. She had learned that much in a good many years of human rooms, though never what it was they added. She decided that it was a complete report and that she would take it as one.

"Then the Chief Pilot has what a Chief Pilot needs," she said.

"Whichever way it goes, somebody will say I flew it to get that."

"They will. The replies are timed at nine, and the slot at fourteen hundred. The order is in the record for anyone who reads the record."

"Hardly anyone does."

"No," said Arashobal. "It is there for the ones who do."

Wender nodded. She went through to her own office, where the citation lay on the left of the desk. Arashobal heard the chair, and after it nothing.

She returned to the list.

She had made it four days ago, earlier than anyone needed. It was a list of what the High Captain's office would be asked for on the day the inquiry closed. A statement: declined, wording agreed. Attendance at the reading: yes. The draft citation: no change. And the sixth item, which she had queried when it was given her, and had drafted anyway.

*Countersignature of the closing report. Not required by the form. Refusal prepared, at the High Captain's word.*

A closing report was not countersigned by the body it reported on. Arashobal had said so. Wender had said she knew, that she had known it for nineteen years, and that she wanted it drafted. So there was a paragraph on file refusing a thing nobody was going to ask for. It was a good paragraph, and Arashobal could find no use for it. She had kept it because the human it was for had not finished needing it.

She added a seventh item, since the day had produced one.

*Dock Seven approach: not this office's. If asked, say so.*

It was a Tuesday. She did not find that meaningful, and she understood that on this station it had become a thing one noted.
