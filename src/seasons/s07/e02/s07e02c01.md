::: pov wender

*Received. Placed on the incident file as the account of the holding officer. The Council makes no finding upon it.*

That was the reply. It had a paragraph of grounds under it and one question under the grounds, and a closing line, and it had taken the Council nine days.

Wender had used the nine days to prepare. She did not think of it as bracing. She had commanded long enough to know that a High Captain who sends a civilian council an account it has not asked for, in place of a signature it has asked for, should have her answers in order before the answer comes. So she had them in order. She had an answer for a summons. She had an answer for a letter from the Triumvirate's secretary, which would be courteous and would mean the same thing. She had three sentences ready for the room's most likely objection, which was that an officer of her seniority had sent correspondence into a boundary failure on her own authority, and she had checked the three sentences for the place where they stopped being right.

None of it was needed. She read the reply once for content.

The grounds were short. The account's statements about the persisting signal, and about what the signal carried, rested on instruments for which the Council could identify no certification. The Council did not doubt the good faith of the account or of the desk whose note stood under it. It could not adopt as a finding what it had no means to test. It declined, for the same reason, to reject it. The account was therefore placed on the file as the holding officer's, at the holding officer's own grade, and the file remained open in her name.

Then the question. The Council could not command a Ranger and did not try to. It could ask, and what it asked the Corps was bound to answer.

*State the certification status of the instruments on which paragraphs nine to fourteen rest.*

Then the closing line. *The Council notes that its draft citation remains before the holding officer.*

She read it a second time for what it was for, and that took longer, because she kept arriving at the same place and not trusting it.

It was not a rebuke. She looked for the rebuke with some care. It was not agreement either. A room had been handed a claim it could not check, by an officer it had reason to be tired of, and it had written down exactly how far it could go and stopped there. It had not rounded her account up into a finding because she was a High Captain. It had not rounded it down into an error because it was inconvenient. *Could not test; makes no finding.* She had built four years of doctrine on officers who could write that sentence. She had not expected to receive it.

There was a consequence in it, and she noted that too, since nobody else was going to. A stalled process of that kind became somebody else's business, somebody a great deal larger than a shore station, at the moment anyone in authority could prove it was occurring. The Council had just recorded, with care, that it could not. So the matter stayed where an unproven thing stays, which is on the desk of whoever is unable to prove it.

The draft citation lay at the left of that desk. She did not look at it. She keyed the internal line.

"Rook. My office, when you're free. I've been asked a question about you."

::::: scene 2

::: pov rook

**Field Analyst Rook-7. Log open. Summoned to the High Captain's office, 1020 station. Stated reason: a question about this analyst, from the Oversight Council. Rook-7 has been the author of every filing it has made concerning Dock Seven and the subject of none. Logging the change of column.**

Rook-7 did not sit. The High Captain did not suggest it. She turned the reply on the desk so that it could be read from where Rook-7 stood, which was a courtesy most people offered a robot by reading aloud.

It read the reply once. Its assessment was short, and it filed the assessment before the question, because that was the order the document came in.

**Assessment: the Council's reply is consistent with the instrument record. It states what the Council received. It states what the Council cannot test. It does not describe the arrays as faulty, which would be a claim, and it does not describe them as sound, which would be another. Rook-7 has now been able to file that sentence about two documents concerning Dock Seven. Both came from the Council. Rook-7 notes that it did not predict this, and is adjusting the weight it gives its own predictions about the Council.**

"They want the certification status," the High Captain said. "I know what I think the answer is. I would rather it came from you."

"Uncertified," Rook-7 said.

"That's the status. Is there a second sentence?"

Rook-7 examined the question, because it could be answered at two lengths and the shorter one was true and incomplete.

"There is. They cannot be certified on the present record. Certification of a field instrument requires three things. A specification from the maker, stating what the instrument is built to read. A reference source of known value. And a test in which the instrument reads the reference and returns the value. Rook-7 holds no specification for the arrays. That is the first failure and it is sufficient. The second is that there is no reference source. Certified instruments file this class of signature as noise, so no certified instrument can supply a known value to test against. The only source Rook-7 has ever found for a signal of this kind at this amplitude is the failure point at Dock Seven."

"Which is the thing in question."

"Yes. An instrument cannot be certified against the reading it is being certified in order to support. Rook-7 would flag that in anyone else's filing."

The High Captain was quiet for a moment. She was looking at the reply and not at Rook-7.

"So the room is right," she said.

"The room is correct that it cannot test the account. Rook-7 would add that this analyst cannot test it either, in the sense the room means. Rook-7 has the reading. It does not have a second instrument that agrees with the reading. The Analyst's desk has certified instruments that agree the failure point is warm and has not decayed. They do not agree about structure, because they do not read structure. They are silent on it. That is not the same as disagreeing, and Rook-7 would ask that the answer to the Council not let it sound the same."

"It won't."

**Note. The Council has asked whether the arrays are certified. It has not asked where the arrays came from. These are different questions. Rook-7 has flagged the second in its own records twice. Count of times it has been put to Rook-7 by anyone: zero. The count is unchanged by today.**

**Prediction, logged so that it can be checked later: a body that has been told an instrument cannot be certified for want of a specification will ask, in its next letter, who made it. Confidence moderate.**

**Note to the prediction. See the adjustment above.**

"You are on a Council file now," the High Captain said. "As *no finding*. I would have understood if you minded."

"It is written down like that in Rook-7's own log," Rook-7 said. "The entry in the tenth week says *consistent with an invariant*. The entry on the night the Senior Archivist read it says *under the assumption that the arrays are reading*. The Council has used more words and reached the same rung. Rook-7 has no objection to being graded where it graded itself. It would have had an objection to being graded higher."

She looked up at that.

"Higher."

"A finding adopted by the Council, on these arrays, would be a finding Rook-7 could not support. It would then be on a file, under the Council's name, resting on this chassis. Rook-7 assesses that as the worse outcome of the two. It did not expect to be spared it."

**Observation, not sent. The High Captain prepared for nine days for an answer of a different kind. Rook-7 has no instrument for preparation. It logs that she had three folded sheets at the right of her desk when it came in, that she has not referred to them, and that at 1034 she put them in the drawer.**

::::: scene 3

::: pov wender

She wrote the answer after Rook-7 had gone. It took four minutes, most of which was the second sentence.

*Uncertified. Not certifiable on the present record: no maker's specification is held, and no reference source exists apart from the signal in question. The station's certified instruments confirm that the failure point is warm and has not decayed to schedule. On the signal's structure they are silent, which is not disagreement.*

She read it over. It gave the room nothing it could use against the account and nothing it could use for it. That was the correct amount. She signed it, which the form required, and found she did not mind her name under a sentence that said what she did not have.

The weather came in the afternoon, and it did not come from the Council.

The reply had been copied, as the form required, to the Council of High Captains. By 1500 she had two notes from the table she sat at. One was from an officer she had served under and liked. It was four lines, and it was kind, and it said that she had made her point and the room had heard it as far as a room could, and that there was a time to let a good officer rest. The other was from a man two seats along who had never liked her doctrine. It was one line. *No finding. You have had your answer, Karla. Sign it.*

She read them both twice. They wanted the same thing, the kind one and the short one, and they wanted it for reasons she could not call bad. One of them had lost officers and knew what a file left open did to the people who had to walk past it. The other believed a High Captain's signature was not hers to withhold from a civilian council on the strength of a robot's shoulder. He was not wrong that it looked like that.

Neither of them had read the reply the way it was written. She was fairly sure of that. They had read *no finding* and heard *no*, because that was the shorter word and most people rounded to it. The room itself had not. Somebody at the Council had sat with her account for nine days and declined to say one thing more or less than they could stand behind. She did not know who. She would have liked to. It was the first time in this whole matter that the Council had written to her in what she could only call her own hand.

It made the citation harder, not easier, and she was honest with herself about why. She had been refusing a document written to make a thing easy to stop thinking about. It had been simple to refuse while the people who wanted it were only tired. Now they had shown they could be exact when exactness was asked of them, and they still wanted it signed. The citation was no truer than it had been in the autumn. But she could no longer tell herself that the room did not know the difference.

She drew the draft across the desk for the first time in some weeks and read it again. Every sentence was true. The sum still said *this is finished*.

She put it back where it had been.

To the kind note she wrote three lines of thanks and nothing about the substance. To the other she wrote *Noted*, which was accurate. Then she took up the next thing on the desk.

Neither note was the last. She had known that when she sent the account. There would be more of them, at long intervals, for as long as the file stayed open. They would not all be kind.

The room had been fair to her. She could not see what she was owed beyond that.
