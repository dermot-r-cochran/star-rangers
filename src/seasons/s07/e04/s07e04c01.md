The dock crew rang the boundary desk at ten past three in the morning to ask whether it was them.

"Dock Seven, night hand," the voice said. It was a human voice and a tired one. "We're reading warm on the inner bulkhead again. Before I pull the exchanger, is that ours or is that yours?"

Toval had been alone on the desk for five hours. It was the first night Syra had left him every line on the sheet, and he had spent a fair part of it finding out that the Boundary Operations Room made a number of small sounds he had never heard while she was in it.

"I can tell you what the desk has," he said.

"Go on."

"The failure point is warm. It has been warm since the autumn. It has not decayed to the schedule a failure decays on. It is inside its band tonight." He stopped there, because that was where the instruments stopped.

There was a pause on the line.

"So is it the exchanger?"

"I can't support that it is," Toval said. "I can't support that it isn't. The desk reads the failure point. It doesn't read your bulkhead."

"We've changed that exchanger seven times."

"Yes. It's in the maintenance returns."

"It wasn't the exchanger the first six."

Toval considered that. It was a reasonable inference. The man had reached it without help, and it was not Toval's to confirm for him. He was briefly aware that a human on a desk might have said something friendly here, and that he did not know what it would be.

"I have no reading that says changing it does harm," he said.

The night hand laughed, once, and not unkindly. "That's the most anyone up there has ever given me. I'll change it." The line closed.

Toval wrote the call into the watch log, with the time. Then he looked again at line nineteen, which he had been not looking at since ten o'clock.

He had taken it at the change of shift, as the sheet required. Rook-7 had come in at 2200 and stood at the end of the desk with one hand on the wall and given him the arrays' column, and he had copied it. *Variation continuing. Invariant held. Nothing addressed. None detected.* He had copied those four phrases under Syra's eye for most of a winter, and tonight they had cost him nothing. Rook-7 had waited until the stylus stopped, which it did not usually do, and then gone.

The desk column was his own to take. He had taken it from the certified instruments, and the figure had come in one division under the top of its band.

It was inside the band. He had checked that before he wrote anything. It was also higher than any figure he had copied onto that line since the deep winter. He knew this because he remembered them, as he remembered most figures. He had gone back down the column anyway, to be sure he was remembering and not preferring.

He had written the figure and the band and nothing else. That was five hours ago, and he had been arguing with it since.

The trouble was that he knew what he was. Syra had told him in his first month, without heat, and he had found nothing in it to dispute. He was a person who wanted a figure to sound as certain as the reading had felt. He had spent a winter learning not to round a seven up to an eight. Nobody had warned him that a line could sit perfectly flat for a season and then give him a seven that was real.

The sheet had no column for *higher than before*. He had looked. The desk did have a mark, a small one, made in the margin, for a thing to be raised aloud at the end of the shift. It was correct procedure. He would not be writing anything the instrument had not carried. He would only be saying that he had noticed.

He held the stylus over the margin for a while.

What stopped him was not a rule. It was that he tried to say, in order, what he would raise. *The figure was near the top of the band.* That was already on the sheet, in the figure. *It had not been so high before.* That was already on the sheet too, for anyone who read up the column. When he took those away there was a third thing left over, and the third thing was that he wanted it to mean something. He found, examining it, that he did not much mind what. Up would have done. Down would have done. He had sat beside that line for a season while it said the same four phrases and the same band, and he was tired of it not settling.

That was not something an instrument had carried. He put the stylus down.

At four the figure was two divisions lower. At five it was in the middle of the band, where it had been all winter. Nobody would ever log those readings, since the line was taken at the change of shift and not between. He watched them anyway and wrote none of them down. He noticed he was a little disappointed, and that it was the same disappointment whichever way he turned it.

A band was the claim. He had been taught that as a sentence and had agreed with it. A figure inside the band was not a second, smaller claim that the instrument was making to him privately. He thought he had probably only known that as a sentence until tonight.

::: pov syra

She came in at ten to six and read the sheet before she spoke to him. That was her habit, and he had learned not to fill the silence.

Forty lines taken and entered, in his level hand. The watch log had one call in it, from the dock, and she read what he had told the night hand and found nothing in it she would have changed. Then line nineteen.

The arrays' column was as given. The desk column was a figure one division under the top of its band, with the band beside it. Nothing else. The margin was clean.

She looked at the margin for longer than at the figure.

"You saw where it sat," she said.

"Yes."

"You did not mark it."

"I wanted to." His questions came out before he had decided whether they were safe to ask, and so did his answers. "I held the stylus over it. I couldn't say what I would be raising. The figure is the figure. What was left was that I wanted it to be news."

"Was it news?"

"It was in the middle of the band by five."

"That is not what I asked."

He took the correction. She watched him take it. "No," he said. "It was a figure inside its band. It was never news. I knew that at ten o'clock, and I spent five hours wanting to be wrong."

"Good," said Syra. "The wanting is not a fault in the entry. The entry is correct. I knew you would want it. I have known since the autumn. It is why you are being trained and not why you are being doubted." She initialled his watch. "You may take line nineteen at every second change from now on. We will have it turn about, your hand and mine."

He did not ask why. There was nothing in it to ask about. Every other line on the sheet had gone turn about as he was cleared for it, and this was the last. He gathered his things and stood, and then he asked a different question, because he was Toval.

"Does it get easier? Not wanting a figure to move?"

Syra considered what she could support.

"It gets more familiar," she said. "I would not say easier. Go and sleep."

He went. She took the six o'clock reading herself, since it was her turn and the first of them. Rook-7 gave her the arrays' column. *Variation continuing. Invariant held. Nothing addressed. None detected.* The desk column was in the middle of its band. She wrote both.

Then she sat, with the sheet filed, and did the other thing she had come in early to do.

She had caught it nine days ago, and she had caught it by being annoyed.

It had been the end of a day watch. She had reached for the sheet and it was not there. Toval had filed it, on time, as he had been taught. She had felt, quite distinctly, annoyance. Syra did not experience fury, and she did not often experience annoyance at a Cadet doing his work correctly, and so she had stopped and looked at it.

She had wanted the sheet in order to read line nineteen. She had already read line nineteen. She had written it herself, at 1400, from Rook-7's own words. There was no cause to read it again. No instrument had been recalibrated. Nothing had been queried. The entry at six in the evening would be the entry she had made at two.

She went back over the previous weeks as honestly as she could. She found that she had been doing this at the end of most shifts, and that she could not say when it had started. Some time after the thaw. Three weeks was her estimate, and she marked it as an estimate.

A reading taken twice is a check, if the reader expects the same result. She had asked herself what she had expected. Then she had asked the better question, which was what she had felt each evening when the entry was the same. The answer was small and entirely clear. She had been disappointed. She had looked at *none detected*, in her own hand, and been disappointed by it.

In the early winter she had been given the boundary data because she wanted nothing from that signal except its value. She had said so to a room. It had been true.

It was not a catastrophe. Syra did not experience deep fear, and what she felt on finding it was the anxiety proper to a fault in a gauge, and the anxiety was useful. She had gone through her own entries for the three weeks, every one, against Rook-7's log of what it had given her. They matched. Nothing she had written leaned. The drift was in the reader and had not yet reached the reading. That was the best time to find one, and nobody could count on it.

A desk that finds a lean in an instrument does not remove the instrument. Mostly it cannot. It writes the lean down, with its direction, so that the next reader knows which way to distrust it. And it stops that instrument being the only one on the line.

She had spent the nine days on the second part. The Cadet was not her opposite. He leaned too, though not in a direction. He wanted the line to settle and did not care which way. She wanted one particular thing from it. Those were different errors, and two readers with different errors were better than one reader who claimed to have none. He had needed to be ready, and she had not been willing to clear him a day early to suit her own repair. Last night he had been ready. This morning's margin had confirmed it.

Now the first part.

The drift log was kept under the instruments, in the low drawer. It was a plain book. Each entry gave the instrument, the date found, the direction, the magnitude, and how it had been detected. The certified gauges were in it many times over. No person was in it. She checked, because she would have liked a precedent, and there was none.

She wrote the date. Under *instrument* she wrote *Analyst, boundary desk*, and her name, since an entry that did not say which analyst would be of no use to whoever held the desk after her.

*Direction: toward an answer from the failure point.*

*Magnitude: not measurable.*

*Detected by: second reading of line nineteen at end of shift, without cause, estimated three weeks. Entries for the period checked against the Field Analyst's log. No discrepancy.*

*Correction: line nineteen entered turn about with the Cadet, from this date.*

She read it over. It was accurate. There was a preference in it, and the preference was hers, and she noted that she did not wish it gone. That belonged in the entry too, and she added it, in six words.

The High Captain would have to be told. A desk whose analyst had acquired a preference should say so before somebody else found it in the readings, and it should say so to the officer who had given her the data on the opposite understanding. She would go up before the week was out. She did not know what the High Captain would do with it. The reasonable course was to move her off the line, and she had the handover notes written, because it was better to have them written than to be asked for them.

She closed the drawer.

On the sheet, line nineteen had two entries for the day so far, one above the other, the Cadet's and hers. The figures were different and both were inside the band. The four phrases beside them were the same.
