::: pov sen

"I would like to see what I wrote," the visitor said, "where you put it."

She had a request slip, correctly filled, and the cutter's salt still on her coat. Under *standing* she had written *counsel to a party to the inquiry*, and under *documents*, a single reference. The desk knew the reference. It had filed the thing itself, in the late autumn, and had done nothing else to it.

*Counsel,* Cael said. *The submission. That is who drafted it.* There was no alarm in it. Cael had no alarm. There was the close attention Cael gave a document's author, which authors seldom enjoyed.

Sen brought the binder to the reading table. Binders did not leave the Archive and a reader did not need them to. She sat, and thanked them, and opened it at her own first leaf.

She read the way a person reads their own work a season later, quickly and with small stops. It was a good hand to have written in. Sen had thought so in the autumn.

"I'm on the station three days on other business," she said, without looking up. "My clients review their standing claims every autumn. This is one. I could have been sent a summary. I have read a number of summaries of my own work."

"The desk does not write summaries," Sen said.

"No. I know what the desk writes." She reached the end of the submission and stopped with her hand flat on the last leaf. "That's why I came."

Then she turned the page.

The next page was the Triumvirate log, the whole of it, where the desk had put it. The survey flag was in the log. All four lines of it were there in the Line Captain's hand, the fourth included: *grounds, phase-lag consistency with item fourteen.*

Sen had waited most of a year, in the manner of the desk, to find out what a reader did on reaching that line. Sen had not expected the first reader to be this one.

She read it. Her face did not change. She had the look of somebody confirming a figure.

"You filed it adjacent to the log," she said.

"That is where it belongs."

"And no note."

"No."

"I looked for one at the foot. I'll be honest with you, I looked for it before I read a word of my own." She sat back. "My clerks pulled sixteen years of this desk's notes when we were drafting. Every reconciled file we could reach. Unsigned, two halves, the gap and then what the gap cost somebody. We drafted against it. I told the room, if we leave the fourth line out, the Archive at Threshold will put it back in a note within the week, and we had better know what we say then."

*She knew,* Wyn said. Wyn had been silent since the slip.

*She quoted three lines of four on purpose and expected to be caught,* Cael said. *I said at the time that it was tidy.*

"And what would you have said?" Sen asked. It was not the desk's question. Sen asked it anyway, because the visitor had come a long way to be asked.

"That the fourth line goes to whether the flag was justified, and nobody ever said it wasn't. The grounds are good. I have read them a great many times, and they are very good grounds." She said it without grudging. "The claim goes to something else. An officer can have sound grounds and an interest she did not disclose, both at once. The first doesn't dispose of the second. Your note would have said *see line four*, and I would have said *line four answers a question we did not ask*. It's a fair answer. The inquiry more or less gave it for me, nine leaves' worth, and gave neither side the last word."

"Then you have had your answer."

"I have had the inquiry's. I never got to give mine, because nobody wrote to me." She looked at the foot of her own last leaf, where the desk's hand was not. "Do you know how a claim reads when it stands on a file with nothing under it? It doesn't read as an argument. An argument has two sides on the page. It reads as a thing somebody said about an officer who wasn't there. I did not draft a smear. I drafted half of a dispute, and then the other half declined to turn up, twice. Once upstairs and once here."

*A submission is a reading of the record,* Cael said, inward, quite evenly. *Not a gap in it.*

"A submission is a reading of the record," Sen said. "The desk annotates gaps. If it began annotating readings it would be an editor."

"Yes. I worked that out about a month after the inquiry closed. It's a perfectly good rule." She closed the binder with some care. "It's hard on the people you don't answer, that's all. I don't imagine anyone has told you so. They wouldn't know where to send it."

Sen had nothing the shelf held to say to that. Sen waited for Wyn, who usually had the sentence for what a silence cost and whom, and Wyn did not offer one.

"Is her status on the record?" counsel said. "The Line Captain's."

"The closing report remits it to her command. The index carries no closing date against the incident."

"And there's an account on the Council's file, under the High Captain's name. I've seen the index entry. Not the account. The entry says *no finding*." She stood and put her coat straight. "The Council's registrar told me the account is the holding officer's and I should ask the holding officer. So I am going upstairs to ask her one question, and I expect she'll decline to answer it. I would like, this time, to be in the room when she does."

::: pov wender

Counsel did not sit until she was asked, and then sat like someone who intended to be brief.

Wender had read her twice in the autumn and had thought then that she would have liked to argue with her. In person she was smaller than her prose and no less exact.

"I'll say what I'm for first," counsel said, "so you needn't work it out. My clients run folds for a living. Every one of them holds a licence that a survey flag can end. I watched that happen to a concern in three days last autumn. I'm not here for that concern. It was working an aperture it had no business working and I've never said otherwise. I'm here for the ones who weren't. They are entitled to know that a Ranger flag is clean. If one of mine had flagged a competitor's ground and said nothing about owning the field next to it, you'd want it looked at. You'd be right."

"I would," Wender said.

"Then my question. The claim has stood most of a year against an officer who has not answered it. I'm told, and I put it no higher than told, that the Corps has sent something to wherever she is. If anything can be put to her, put this. *Did she know whose land it was.* One line. If she says no, I withdraw before the autumn. If she says yes, you have a problem you ought to have, and I think you would rather have it than not know."

It was well made. Wender looked for the place where it was unfair and did not find one. She had been offered a second message before, by people who loved the officer in question. This was the first time anyone had offered it to her as a duty, and the first time it had been one she half owed.

She answered in order.

"An account went to the failure point in the deep winter. That is on the Council's file and I won't pretend it is not. I can't tell you that it arrived. I have no instrument that reads arrival. If it did arrive, it asked one thing, and the thing was hers to answer or leave. I am not going to send a second after it with a claimant's question in it."

"Because you think she's owed peace."

"No. I have no finding about what she is owed. Because the first was sent as an account, and the second would be a summons. I don't have the standing to summon her. Nor do you. An officer under a claim may be asked to answer it. I can't see that anyone may put the question down a channel she cannot use to refuse."

Counsel took that in. It was plainly not the refusal she had prepared for.

"Then we're where we were in the autumn," she said. "You did not ask her, you do not know, and you won't say either way. I have that sentence by heart."

It was the easy place to stop. Every word of the autumn's sentence was still true. Wender had not asked. She would not say what Shepherd would have said. She could let counsel carry that back down the stairs, and nobody would ever show it to be false.

She had declined to sign a citation once because every sentence in it was true and the sum was a lie.

"No," Wender said. "We are not where we were. I want that understood before you leave, because you will write it down, and you should write down the right thing."

Counsel waited.

"In the autumn I declined to answer your first claim because I did not know the answer. I had never asked her about that coordinate. That is on the file and it was the whole of my position. It is not the whole of it now. I know more than I did."

"You asked her."

"I did not. I have still never asked her about it. What I know came unasked. It wasn't given to me so that I could settle a claim, and it wasn't given to me alone." She kept her voice level. It took more effort than it had in the autumn. "I am not going to tell you what it is. It is hers. If she wants it on your file she can put it there, and until she can, nobody is going to put it there for her. I include myself in that."

For a moment counsel said nothing.

"That's worse," she said. "You do see that. In the autumn you were ignorant, and I could do very little with ignorant. Now the holding officer tells me, in her own office, that she holds something bearing on a claim against her subordinate and declines to produce it."

"Yes."

"I can put that in front of the Council."

"You can. It's accurate. I would ask you to quote all of it."

Counsel looked at her a little longer. Then she laughed, briefly and without any malice at all.

"I turned the page, you know. Downstairs, just now. I had turned it last autumn as well. We left that line out because it was the best thing on the file and it wasn't ours." She stood. "I will quote all of it. The room can make what it likes of a High Captain who volunteers that her position has got worse. I've not met that before and I don't know where it goes."

"Does it stand?"

"The claim? It stands. I can't withdraw on a thing I haven't been shown. You wouldn't, in my place." She put her coat straight, which seemed to be how she finished things. "I'd have advised her to do what she did, for what that's worth. State the grounds and nothing further. It's nearly unanswerable. I would have liked very much to cross-examine her."

"She would have stated her grounds and said nothing further."

"Yes," counsel said. "I had gathered that."

When she had gone Wender sat for a time with nothing in front of her.

The first refusal had cost her in public and had been simple. She had not known, and she had said so. This one would cost more, and she had made it harder herself. She could have left the old sentence standing. It would have protected Shepherd just as well. It would also have meant using *I did not ask* to cover something she had since been told, and Shepherd had drawn that line more carefully than anyone Wender had commanded. A thing left unsaid was allowed. A true sentence put there so that somebody would believe a false one was not.

She knew now what had been under the silence over item sixteen. Three short clauses, water and wood and dusk, and a family that had kept them for six generations without writing them down. The officer had known exactly whose land it was. She had flagged it anyway, on the marker, in four lines, with her own family's ground under the coordinate, and had said nothing more because the flag required nothing more. That was the answer to counsel's question. It was a better answer than counsel could have hoped for or feared. But it was not Wender's to give.

She wrote a short minute of the meeting for the file, in order, leaving out nothing she had said. Then she took the next item.

::::: scene 3

::: pov sen

The binder went back on the shelf as it had come off it. The submission, with three lines of a four-line flag. The log beside it, with four. No note.

*Neither withdrawn nor answered,* Cael said. *Unchanged. I have checked.* Then, because Cael did not leave a thing half-reported: *She had read the fourth line before she ever wrote. So in the autumn the desk declined a note that would have told its author nothing she did not know. It would have told every later reader something. I record both.*

*She is the first person who has ever come here to say what one of our silences cost her,* Sen said. *And it was one I chose.*

*Yes.*

Nobody said anything further for a while. That was unusual enough that Sen noticed it and looked, inward, for the one who would ordinarily have spoken by now.

*I am not going to say it,* Wyn said.

*You could.*

*She said it for me. She wanted somebody to write back. She came across the water for it and sat at our table and said so, in better order than I ever have.* Wyn was quiet for a moment. *And she was wrong about the note. You were right in the autumn. A reading is not a gap. If we had answered her we would have been in her dispute, and after that nobody would have read anything else on this shelf without thinking of it. I can see that. I have always seen it. I wish it weighed less, that is all. It does not make me want the name any less tomorrow.*

*I know.*

*I thought you should hear that I can hold both.*

*I never thought you could not,* Sen said.

There was nothing to enter. Nothing had been filed, and the index had no column for a reading. Sen put the request slip in the day's tray with the others and squared the tray. Then, having no entry to make, Sen stayed at the desk a while with the binder's spine in view.

The light went late, in summer. The three of them stayed until it had.
