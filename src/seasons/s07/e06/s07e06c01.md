**Field Analyst Rook-7. Log open. 1252 station, assembly hall. Event: a funeral, held by the Oversight Council. Subject: Line Captain T. Shepherd.**

**Difficulty with the entry. This log files an event under its subject, and a subject under a category. Categories held for this subject, in order of first use: officer; Principal, in archived telemetry; author of a survey flag; position, logged at a failure point; source, assumed, of a signal. Category *deceased*: not held. No instrument on this station has supplied it and no officer with standing has entered it.**

**The log will not create a category in order to file an afternoon. Logging the event under: ceremonial. Noting that the Council is entitled to hold it, and that Rook-7 does not dispute the entitlement, only the drawer.**

Rook-7 did not sit. There were seats, in rows, for some nine hundred, and most of them were filling. It stood against the rear wall with one hand flat on the panel, from where it could see the whole room and the two doors.

It logged the room as it logged any room.

The dock crews had come in their work blues and sat together. The pilots had come and sat apart from one another, which pilots did. The Gravimetry Desk had come and had brought nothing to read. The Senior Archivist was at the end of a row near the side door, in the olive coat and the one scarf, with both hands empty. Rook-7 had not seen the Senior Archivist with both hands empty before.

The Council's officiant was at the front beside a plain lectern. There was nothing else at the front. A human funeral ordinarily arranged itself around an object. The hall had placed the lectern where the object would have stood, which was the only place left for it.

The Analyst was in the ninth row with the Cadet beside her. The Cadet was looking at the front of the hall with the attention he gave a gauge he had not been shown how to read.

**Scheduling note. The service begins at 1300. The afternoon watch begins at 1400. The boundary desk's sheet is taken at the change of shift, forty-one lines, and the arrays' column for line nineteen is delivered by this analyst at the desk at that time. Line nineteen is the Cadet's turn. Walking time from this hall to the Boundary Operations Room: nine minutes. The Cadet and Rook-7 will both have to leave by 1349.**

**The order of service lists the High Captain's address last. Rook-7 has estimated the items before it. It assesses, at moderate confidence, that 1349 will fall inside the address.**

**It has not raised this with anyone. The watch is the watch. Logging it so that the leaving, when it happens, has a reason beside it.**

::::: scene 2

::: pov toval

He had asked Syra, on the way in, what it was for.

The question had come out before he had decided whether it was safe. It usually did. They had been in the corridor with a great many humans going the same way, quietly, and several of them had heard.

"There is no body," he had said. "And the High Captain has not signed. So what is it for?"

Syra had not lowered her voice. She never did, and he had stopped expecting it.

"It is for the people in the room," she said. "That is a use. It has been demonstrated many times, among many peoples, and you may take it as shown. You need not find it sufficient. You need only write down what happens and not round it."

So he sat in the ninth row and tried to do that.

A good deal happened that he could not read. The room stood and sat at moments nobody announced. The officiant spoke for some time in a level, practised voice, and what she said was true as far as Toval could check it against the Dock Seven file, which he knew well. He had taken down two of the witness statements himself. She gave the hour and the hulls and the number of people brought off. She read a document on the Council's behalf. The words *held the boundary* were in it, and *at the cost of*.

Around him the room changed while she read. He felt it. He was not good at stopping himself noticing that, and the Corps had never asked him to be. It was not falseness. Nobody in the hall was pretending anything. It was more that nine hundred people were leaning very slightly toward one sentence, the way a desk leans toward a figure it has waited on. They wanted to be told it was finished. He could find no fault in wanting that. He knew something about wanting a line to settle.

He looked at Syra once, during the document. Her attention had left the room. She was not making anything of it. She had simply stopped supplying it. Syra did that when a thing was being made larger than what it described.

A man two seats along caught his eye. It was a dock hand in work blues, and it took Toval a moment to place the voice when he leaned over.

"You're boundary desk. You're the one who wouldn't tell me about my exchanger."

"I told you what the desk had."

"You did." The man kept his voice down. He looked at the lectern and back. "Is this right, then? What they're doing. You'd know if anyone would."

"I don't know," Toval said. "The desk doesn't read that."

He expected the man to be disappointed. Humans generally were when he said that. The dock hand only nodded slowly.

"No," he said. "Well. Somebody ought to be the one who doesn't know." And he sat back.

The High Captain went up at 1344.

She did not look as if she had come to perform a thing. Toval had seen her on the desk's business a few times across the year, receiving the sheet, and she looked now as she had then. She put nothing on the lectern. She said she had been asked to say what the officer was like, and that she would do that, and then say one thing more and sit down.

He attended to her closely, because here at last was something he could read. She used one word and then stopped to say what she did not mean by it. He approved of that so strongly that he lost the next sentence. She said the officer had read everything twice. She said something about a rule from a hill, kept without a reason, and the room made a small sound he could not classify. Then she came to Dock Seven, and gave the lifts, and the people on them, and a figure in minutes.

At the edge of his sight, at the rear wall, Rook-7 took its hand off the panel.

It was 1349.

He looked at Syra. She did not look back at him. She was watching the High Captain, and her attention was entirely in the room again. He understood that he had been left the decision, and also that there was not really one. The desk had forty-one lines and a change of shift. It was his watch.

He stood. He was in the middle of a row, and there was no way of leaving it that was not seen. He went along it as quietly as he could, past the dock hand, who moved his knees without being asked. The High Captain had paused at the figure. As he reached the aisle she went on, something about an order and whether it had been correct, and then the side door closed behind him.

The Senior Archivist had been at the end of the row nearest that door. The Senior Archivist had watched him go and had not looked surprised.

Rook-7 was waiting in the corridor. They walked the nine minutes without speaking. It was raining on the harbour, and from the long gallery he could see the water off Dock Seven. It looked like water.

He took the desk at 1358 from a human Officer who had volunteered to hold the desk through the service and wanted to know nothing about it. He began at line one. The sheet was taken in order. He had never been given a reason for taking it in order and had asked for one in his first week. Syra had said that a desk which takes its favourite line first has told you which line is its favourite.

So he reached line nineteen at twelve minutes past two, as he would have on any afternoon.

The desk column first. Warm. The figure sat a little below the middle of its band. He wrote the figure and the band.

"Arrays' column," Rook-7 said. It stood at the end of the desk with one hand on the wall. "Variation continuing. Invariant held. Nothing addressed. None detected."

He wrote the four phrases. He looked at them when he had written them, in his own hand, under the Analyst's from that morning and his own from the night before, the same four going back up the page as far as the page went.

Somewhere above him the High Captain was either still speaking or had just stopped. He did not know what she was saying. He found that the stylus was steady and that he had nothing to add.

He went on to line twenty.

**Field Analyst Rook-7. Log open. 1741 station. The Council's ceremonial record of the service has been posted to the station. Public; not addressed to this desk. Logging it. The practice is established.**

The record gave the order of service, the officiant's remarks in summary, the document read on the Council's behalf, and the High Captain's address. Rook-7 read the address against its own log of the hall.

The posted text ran from her first sentence to the figure in minutes. It ended there.

Rook-7's own log of the address also ended there. It had left the hall on that sentence, at 1349, with the Cadet.

**Observation. Two records on this station hold the first part of the address and not the second. One is the Council's ceremonial record. The other is this log. Rook-7 has no instrument for why the Council's stops where it does. It knows why its own does. It had a column to deliver.**

**Rook-7 assesses the coincidence as a coincidence. It logs it because a later reader who sets the two records side by side will find them in agreement, and will be mistaken to conclude from that that either is complete.**

It considered asking the Analyst what the second part had contained. She had come down to the desk at 1510, read the sheet before she spoke, initialled the Cadet's watch and said nothing about the hall. The Cadet had not asked her. Rook-7 had noted that he did not, and thought the choice sound. A thing said once to nine hundred people becomes a different object in the retelling, and the desk had spent a year declining to take its readings at second hand.

**Note. At 1412 station this analyst delivered the arrays' column for line nineteen, as at every change of shift since the morning after the account was sent. The entry was the entry. The Cadet's handwriting did not change. Rook-7 logs that it looked.**

**Note. In the hall an officer was described in the past tense. At the desk, inside the same half hour, a reading was entered in the present. Rook-7 has both in one log, twenty-three minutes apart. It does not reconcile them. It has watched two desks on this station decline to reconcile such things for a year, and has not found a fault in the practice.**

**Log held open.**

::::: scene 4

::: pov toval

The newsnets printed it on the fourth day. Three of them, entire.

He did not go looking. One of the day watch had left a reader on the end of the desk, open, in the way humans left things they wanted you to see and did not want to hand you. He finished the sheet first. Then he sat down with it, in the quarter hour before Rook-7 was due.

The first part he knew. It was as he had heard it, to the minute. He was relieved, a little, to find that he had remembered it without improving it.

Then the paragraph he had caught the start of from the aisle. Then four words, *Now the other thing*, and what came after.

He read it once, and then again more slowly.

She had told them she had not signed, and that she would not sign that day either. She had told them she did not claim the officer was alive, because she had no finding that supported it. She had told them she would not say the officer was dead, because she did not know that. She had said both from the lectern, to nine hundred people who had leaned toward one sentence for an hour, and she had given them neither. Then she had told them what she meant to do about it, which was nothing, for as long as it took.

Toval thought about the room. He had felt what it wanted. He tried to work out what it had cost to stand at the front of that and decline, and found he could not get a figure for it.

He did notice one thing, and he was careful with it, since it was the kind of thing he was being trained not to round up.

At twelve minutes past two on that afternoon he had written four phrases on a sheet. *None detected* was the last of them. Syra had spent a winter making sure he knew what it did not say. It did not say *none sent*. It did not say *nothing there*. It sat between two things the desk could not support and declined to lean on either.

He had written that, alone, on a line, while she was saying this to the hall. Neither of them had known what the other was doing. He did not think it meant anything. Two people working to the one discipline had come to the same place on the same afternoon because the place was where the evidence stopped. That was all. He left it at that.

He would have liked to hear her say it. He found that he did not regret the desk.

Rook-7 came in at the change of shift and put one hand on the wall. Toval closed the reader and moved it to the end of the desk, where its owner would find it.

"Arrays' column," Rook-7 said.

He took up the stylus.
