::: pov syra

The boundary desk audited its own shift sheet once a year, in the deep winter, when the thin water was quiet and there was time. Every line on the sheet had to say what it was for. A line that could not was struck, because a reading nobody used was a reading somebody would eventually stop taking carefully, and a careless reading on a sheet was worse than a gap in one.

Syra had given the audit to Toval. He was poor at keeping anything whose use had not been shown to him, and she had noted that as the second thing to train, after the rounding. An audit was the one job on the desk where the fault was a qualification.

He brought it back in two days. Forty-one lines. Thirty-eight kept, with the use stated beside each in his level hand. Two struck, both correctly; she had been meaning to strike one of them for three years. And one flagged.

"Line nineteen," he said. "Dock Seven, failure point, each shift. I can't state a use."

"State what you found."

"A year of it. A thousand and ninety-some entries. The desk column says the failure point is warm and has not decayed to the schedule a failure decays on. It said that on the first day and it says it today, inside the same error bars. The arrays column is Rook-7's, and Rook-7's arrays log continuously whether anybody copies them onto our sheet or not." He did not hurry and he did not soften it. "No decision on this desk in a year has depended on line nineteen. No decision on the station has. If it had been struck last winter, everything that happened this year would have happened. That's the test you gave me for the other forty."

"It is."

"So by the test, it's struck." He stopped. His questions came out before he had decided whether they were safe to ask, and she had told him that was a habit he might keep. "But you put it on the sheet. Nobody ordered it. I looked. There's no order. It's in your hand, the morning after the High Captain's account went, and there's nothing beside it."

"There is nothing beside it because I had nothing to put there that was true."

He waited. He was learning to.

Syra took the audit from him and read line nineteen as he had written it, which was accurately. Then she told him what she could support, in order.

"The account was sent. That is verified; it is in the High Captain's hand with a time on it. I cannot support that it arrived. I have no instrument that reads arrival. The High Captain asked a question in it, and nothing addressed to this station has come back in a year. I cannot support that nothing was sent. I can support that nothing was detected. Those are three sentences and you have been taught the difference between each pair."

"Yes."

"Then here is what line nineteen is. A process is running at the failure point. A person may be in it. She was asked something. The line is the place on this station where an answer would first be written down, if one came, by somebody who was looking and had been looking the shift before." She put the audit on the desk between them. "That is grounds. It is not a use. You are correct that no use has been demonstrated, and you should not write one in to make the line look like the other forty."

"Then what do I write beside it?"

"The grounds. And under *use*, what is true."

He took the stylus. She watched him consider the column, and she watched the part of him that wanted a figure to sound as certain as a reading felt look for a word that would round the line up into something that had earned its place. He did not find one he could stand behind. He wrote *none shown*, and his handwriting did not change.

"The line stays," Syra said. "That is my decision and my name goes against it in the audit, not yours. Your finding stands as you made it. Both go to the High Captain."

"She'll see I recommended striking it."

"She will see that a Cadet tested the one line on this desk that everyone else had stopped testing. I would rather she saw that than a sheet with forty-one uses on it."

He looked at the arrays column for a while.

"I have never asked what that column rests on," he said.

"No," said Syra. "Ask the one who fills it. It will be in at the change of shift."

::::: scene 2

::: pov rook

**Field Analyst Rook-7. Log open. Change of shift, boundary desk, 1400 station. Arrays column for line nineteen delivered, entry one thousand and ninety-six. The Cadet did not copy it down. The Cadet asked a question first. Logging the order of events, because the order is unusual.**

Rook-7 did not sit. It stood at the end of the boundary desk with one hand flat on the wall, which was preference, and gave the Cadet the reading it had given at every change of shift for a year. Then the Cadet asked where the arrays had come from.

**Query received from Cadet Toval, Survey Corps: origin of the non-standard arrays. Count of times this query has been put to Rook-7 in its service on this station, before today: zero. Count now: one. Rook-7 has flagged the question in its own records twice. It notes that being asked is a different event from flagging, and takes longer.**

It answered with what it had.

It had a chassis whose classification was either out of date or understated, and it had flagged both. It had arrays above the shoulder of a different make from the chassis, on a newer collar, fitted before its posting to Threshold by a hand its own records did not name. It could say what the arrays were not. They were not a Corps pattern, a Survey pattern or a constabulary one, and they were on no register it had been able to query. It could say what they did. They carried signatures that certified instruments filed as noise, and they carried them more cleanly near one kind of marker than near another, and that had been useful for six years without being explained.

"So the answer is that you don't know," the Cadet said.

"The answer is *unknown, flagged twice,*" Rook-7 said. "They are not the same answer. The second one tells you somebody looked."

The Cadet considered that with the stillness of his kind. Then he asked the next question, which Rook-7 had assessed at high confidence that he would.

"Then how does the desk know they're reading? Line nineteen has two columns. If the second one rests on something nobody can name, I've spent a year copying it."

**Observation. The Cadet has reached in one exchange the place the Senior Archivist reached in the deep winter of last year, and stated aloud, in front of the High Captain: that the arrays' column is what a reading expects, under the assumption that the arrays are reading. The assumption has stood at the head of that column for a year, in the Senior Archivist's wording. Rook-7 assesses that it has been copied onto the sheet one thousand and ninety-five times and read, as a heading, by fewer people than that.**

"The desk does not know," Rook-7 said. "Read the head of the column."

He read it. He read it the way the Line Captain had read things, which Rook-7 logged and did not remark on: once, and then again more slowly.

"It says so," he said. "It's said so the whole time."

"Yes."

"Does it bother you? That it all rests on that."

Rook-7 examined the question. It had no instrument for *bother*. It had the thing its logs sometimes did, whose status it had resolved by ceasing to require a name for it.

"The column was always resting on it," it said. "What has changed today is that a second reader of the column knows what it rests on. Rook-7 assesses that this makes the column better read. It does not make the arrays better. Those are different improvements and only one of them was available."

Then it gave him the rest, because he had asked what the column rested on and was owed what the column held.

The signal at the failure point still phrased. It repeated and varied and returned to its one fixed element, the string and the figure and the three short clauses, and that element had not changed in any iteration the arrays held. Nothing in the variation was addressed to the station, as far as the arrays could tell an address from anything else. It was the entry Rook-7 had given the day before, and on the first day, and on every shift between.

**Logged each shift. Rook-7 notes that an unchanged entry is still an entry. It has logged eleven boundary failures going quiet in its service and knows what a line looks like when there is nothing left to write on it. This is not that line. This one has the same thing to write every time, and the same thing is not nothing.**

The Cadet had the stylus over the sheet. Rook-7 observed the stylus not move.

"So she hasn't answered," he said. "A year. That's what the column says, if you read it straight down."

"That is what you would like the entry to settle," Rook-7 said. "The column says none detected, a thousand and ninety-six times. It does not say none sent. Rook-7's logs would say the shorter thing if they were permitted to round. They are not, and the Analyst has spent a year on the proposition that you are not either."

"Rounding down is still rounding."

"Yes," said Rook-7. "It is the kind that sounds like discipline."

**Note. The Cadet wrote the entry as given. Variation continuing; invariant held; nothing addressed; none detected. He then made, in the margin, where the sheet has no column, a single mark of the kind the desk uses for a thing to raise aloud. Rook-7 does not know what the thing is. It logs that the mark is correct procedure, and that it has, at low confidence, a guess, and that a guess is not an entry.**

**Log held open.**

::::: scene 3

::: pov syra

He raised it aloud at the end of the shift, as the mark required.

"I want to ask something that doesn't go on the sheet."

"Then ask it."

He closed the sheet first. She noted that, and approved of it.

"Do you want her to answer?"

It was the question nobody on the station had put to her. The humans had not asked because they assumed the answer, and the High Captain had not asked because the High Captain did not ask. Syra considered declining it, and found she had no ground for declining that was not a preference for being thought steadier than she was.

"In the early winter of last year I wanted nothing from that signal except its value," she said. "That was true, and it was the reason I was given the data. It has become less true. I noticed it in the spring. I prefer that she answers. I do not know what an answer from her would look like, or whether she owes one, and I think she does not. But the preference is there, and it is mine, and it points one way."

"Does that — "

"It disqualifies nothing yet. I entered it in the desk's drift log, under the instruments, since that is where drift goes, and I told the High Captain the same week, because a desk whose analyst has acquired a preference should say so before somebody else finds it in the readings. She read it twice. She left me on the desk. She said an instrument that reports its own drift is the one you keep, and then she asked me nothing further, which is her habit."

Toval took that in.

"So neither of us is neutral about line nineteen."

"No. You would like the column to settle it, one way or the other. I would like her to answer. That is two readers leaning, and the sheet has to be written so that a third, who leans the other way or not at all, can check every entry and find only what the instruments carried." She stood. "That is what the line is for, if you still want a use. It is not for her. She cannot read it. It is so that if anything does come, the people who wanted it are not the only evidence that it did."

He opened the audit again and looked at *none shown*, and she saw him weigh whether what she had just said was a use he could write down.

"It hasn't been demonstrated," he said.

"No."

He left the entry as it was.

"Good," said Syra, and put her name against the line.
