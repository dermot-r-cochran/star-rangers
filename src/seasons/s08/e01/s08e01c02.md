:::pov brother-fintan

He packed the smoker before he opened the door. That was the order he did things in that morning, and he had cause to notice it afterward.

He had slept well. A man with a post to go to generally does. He had woken before the light with the day already laid out in him the way a watch bill used to be: the third hive, at the first hour warm enough to lift a cover; a little smoke; the combs out one at a time and read, from the outside in. He had stood beside the thing through the dusk and carried it through the office, and had asked nothing of it. Now he would ask. That was not a breach of the discipline. It was the other half of it, and he had never held that attending meant not finding out — only that the attending came first, and was not hurried to make room.

Then he opened the door, and the hill was white.

Not snow. Frost, the first of the year, and a hard one: every blade of the grass between the lodge and the hive-yard stood separately, the boulders had gone the colour of the sky, and the three hives on their stand were so nearly the white of the ground they stood on that for a moment he found them by their shadows. It was two below on the lodge wall.

The forecast had given frost for the end of the week. The hive-yard sits where the cold air comes down off the slope and lies, and it has been known to run a night or two ahead of any forecast. He had known that for ten years and had not thought of it once between the dusk and the Amen. He did not make much of that. There had been nothing to do with the thought if he had had it.

He went down anyway. The smoker was in his hand, unlit, and he carried it because his hand had it.

The grass broke under his boots. At the stand he did what there was to do, which was to listen, and he went along the row in order so as to have the two sound ones in his ear first. A colony in frost does not hum as it hums at dusk in a mild week. It has drawn in — all of it, into a ball on the combs not much bigger than a man's head, shivering by turns to keep the middle warm — and its voice goes inward with it, closer and smaller, a thing heard through the wood with the ear nearly on it and not from a pace away. The first hive had one voice. The second had one voice. He bent to the third, with the cold coming off the wall of it onto his temple, and the seam was there.

It was where it had been. No lower in the chord, no wider, no worse; drawn in with everything else and keeping its place, as steady as at dusk. He could not have said it had changed at all. That was everything the morning had to tell him, and he had known it the night before.

He straightened. He knew the arithmetic, as any keeper does, and he went through it regardless, because going through it was owed. A cluster is all a colony has between itself and the winter, and it is made of nothing but the bees' hold on one another. Lift the cover and the heat they have spent the night making goes up past your face. Lift a comb and you have taken a wall out of the house. They cannot fly to mend it; a bee that leaves the cluster at two below drops in the grass and does not get up. A keeper who goes in after an answer on a morning like this one will have his answer, and it will be one he made.

So the smoker stayed in his hand. He noticed that. At dusk he had set it down, and the setting down had been something: a man with a smoker and a quarter-hour of usable light and perhaps ten degrees, declining. He had been ten years learning to do it. This morning there was nothing to decline. He could have set the smoker on the grass, or carried it to the lodge and back forty times, and none of it would have been the discipline, because the frost had got to the stand before him and settled the matter without reference to what kind of man he was.

He stood a while longer. It was the same ground and the same hive and the same half-tone, and his hands were at his sides as they had been. He could find no difference in the standing itself. He looked for one.

Noon, he thought. A white morning will sometimes make ten degrees by noon, this late in the year, if the sun gets on the yard and the wind keeps out of the north. It would not need long. A quarter of an hour with the cover off.

He went up to wait for noon, and found at the lodge door that he was still carrying the smoker.

:::pov hive-bee

The cold had come all the way in.

It had been at the wood before. Now it was in the comb, at the edges, in the cells where nobody stood, and the thousands had drawn closer than close: heads in among the cells, backs under bellies, sisters touching sisters on every side and no air between. The warmth was in the middle. She was not in the middle. She was some way out, where it thinned, and she beat without opening her wings, as all of them did, and the beating made warmth, and the warmth went inward and came back.

After a time the ones behind her pressed, and she was let in by a body's width, and one from the inside came out past her to take the cold. That was how it went. Nobody stayed in the warm and nobody stayed out of it.

The saying was closer too. All of it came through her now, with so little comb between, and the wrong place came with it: down-comb still, keeping time still, the same gap in the same place. The cold had not closed it. The drawing-in had not closed it. It had come in with them, like the smell of the stores.

Light came at the door, hard and white, with no pull in it. Nobody went.

Then the tread. It was not the dusk tread. Each step broke something, and the breaking came up through the stand into the wood, sharp — tread and break, tread and break. Far along the wood it stopped. Nearer, it stopped. Then it was here. The small weight was still on the wood above, where it had settled in the dark. The world would open now, or the smoke would come. That was what followed the tread when the tread came in the light.

Something leaned on the wall. Not above: at the side, low, at one place, no wider than a few sisters, and it stayed. A slow thing moved in it, in and out. It did not knock. The wall did not lift.

It went. The tread broke away, up the hill.

It came back when the light at the door was strongest, and leaned at the same place, and went. It came back when the light was going. Before the cold it had come at dusk and stood off. Now it came at every light and leaned.

The thousands did not turn toward it. There was the warmth to keep. She was let in another body's width, and beat, and the wrong place kept its time beside hers.

:::pov brother-fintan

Noon made four degrees.

He read it off the lodge wall at twelve, and again at one, when it was still four and the wind had gone round into the north. The sun was on the yard and made no difference to it. The frost had lifted from the open grass and stayed, white and exact, in the shadow of the stand: a long rectangle of the morning that the day had not reached.

Ten degrees is the figure. It is not the hill's; it is older than the hill, and every keeper Fintan has read or met has had it within a degree or two. Below that, a winter cluster is not taken apart. He went down at one and put his ear to the third hive and heard what he had heard at first light. He came up again.

That was the day he had been expected at.

He took the commonplace book down in the afternoon, because a reading that is not entered has not been taken, and ruled the page as he ruled every page: two columns, the left for what was confirmed and the right for everything else. The left took four lines. *Third hive: a half-tone, low in the voice, from dusk yesterday. Steady. First frost overnight; two below at first light, four at noon. Did not open.*

He looked at the right column for some time. Thirty-one years of reports had taught him that anyone can enter a reading, and that the officer is known by whether he enters beside it the size of what he does not know. He wrote: *Cause: not known.* Then, because the entry was not complete without it and nobody else was going to complete it: *Not knowable without opening. No opening under ten degrees.*

He did not write the date of that day, since he did not have it. He had ten winters of the hill to go on. In a kind year there is a soft noon or two before the turn of the year, and a keeper may get his quarter of an hour. In most years there is not, and the first day of ten degrees comes in the spring. Three months. It might be four.

So that was what the dusk had cost him, and he put the figure on it as he would have for another man's decision. Yesterday, with the light going, the cover could have come off. A keeper in a hurry would have had it off. He would know by now what the half-tone was. He might not like what he knew; there might have been nothing to do about it this side of the winter, or a good deal; and he would know which. Instead he had the sound. He would have the sound tomorrow, and the day after, through the wood, with his ear near it, and it would tell him each time exactly what it had told him at dusk, and he would not be able to ask it anything.

He had offered the attention, last night, as the thing he had to give. He had been particular about it. It had not occurred to him that by morning it would be the only thing he had.

The psalter has a line for the day, and it came to him without being fetched. *He gives snow like wool, and scatters frost like ashes.* And a line on: *Who can stand before his cold?* The psalm means that nobody can. He had been standing before it since first light, and had not been asked whether he would.

He did not think the dusk had been wrong. He took that down to the stand with him at the end of the light and looked at it there, with his breath showing and the frost already coming back on the cover, and it held. A sound is not a finding. A keeper who takes the roof off a hive at nightfall, in a hurry, with a cold night coming, for a sound he cannot name, has put his own wish to know ahead of the hive, and he had not spent ten years on the hill to do that at the first half-tone. He would stand the same again.

He put his ear to the wood. The seam was there.

He had gone to sleep like a man expected at a post in the morning, and he had been. He had only mistaken the post. He had thought it was the opening. It was this: the square yard of grass he had stood on at dusk of his own accord. It would be this tomorrow at first light, and there was no morning now on the far side of it that he could mark a hive for.

The cloth was on the rail where he had hung it, stiff, white along its top fold. He had meant to take it off today. A mark comes down when the reading it stands for has been taken.

He left it where it was, and went up to say the office.
