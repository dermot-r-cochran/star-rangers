:::pov brother-fintan

The first warm day came on a Thursday, twelve degrees on the lodge wall by ten in the morning, and Fintan went to the third hive first.

He had known since the autumn that he would. The cloth he had hung on the stand rail the night the hive went wrong was still there — faded on its weather side, stiff with a winter of rain, the keeper's annex that nobody else would read. He had read it every day for four months. That was all he had done about it.

The morning he had marked the hive for had come up white. Frost on the stand, frost on the grass, the thousands drawn into their winter ball inside, and a keeper who lifts combs out of a cluster in that cold to find out what is wrong with it will generally have made it true by the time he knows. So he had not opened. He had stood, that morning and most mornings after, with his ear near the wood, and the half-tone had been there each time — a little lower by midwinter, a little thinner by the turn of the year — and he had told himself that standing was what the season allowed. Which it was. He had noticed, somewhere in the second month, how readily he believed it.

He used very little smoke. He opened the hive.

It did not take long to read. A colony in order, on the first warm day, has a small bright patch of brood at its centre: sealed cells, the year beginning. The third hive had none. It had stores, more than it had needed, because there had been fewer and fewer mouths to need them. It had dead on the floor in the ordinary winter number and then a good deal past it. And it had, on two combs near the middle, what was left of the living — a cluster he could have covered with his two hands, old bees, autumn-born, their wing edges worn pale, moving on the comb with the unhurried aimlessness of a watch that has not been relieved and has stopped expecting to be.

No queen. He went through twice, comb by comb, the way he had once gone through a reading he disliked, and did not find her, and had not thought he would. She had failed in the autumn, by the sound: after the last eggs were too old to make another of, after the last drones were gone. A colony that loses its queen then cannot mend it. It knows, and says so, in the only register it has, and goes on keeping the middle warm for as long as there is a middle.

That was the half-tone. The hive had told him in the autumn, and he had heard it, and had not known what he heard.

The chaplain the rota had sent that fortnight was at the wax-house door with her sleeves rolled, because nobody stays idle at Cnoc na mBeach near an open hive, and she had the sense not to come closer without a veil. She watched him put the cover back.

"Dead," she said.

"Not yet," said Fintan. "There's a handful. They'll not see the month out by themselves."

He knew what to do with a handful; any keeper did. The remedy was older than the hill. You carry what is left of a queenless colony to a colony that has a queen, and you put a sheet of paper between the two so that they cannot reach each other all at once, and by the time they have chewed through it they smell alike, and there is no fight. He would do it that afternoon, while the warmth held.

What he stood over, with his hand flat on the cover, was that the remedy in spring was also the remedy in autumn. In the autumn it would have meant opening a cluster at the edge of frost on the strength of a sound, and he might have killed a hive that was only out of sorts. Or he might have carried four thousand bees across the stand instead of four hundred. He did not know which. He would not know. The instrument that could have told him was the hive, opened, and the opening was the question.

He had spent ten years unlearning the reach for the smoker. It was possible — he set it down plainly, the way he would have set it in a report — that he had unlearned one thing too many, and had been calling it the discipline.

:::pov hive-bee

The middle was small now.

It had been the thousands. It was not the thousands. She did not count; counting was not a thing done. But the warmth had edges where it had not had edges, and the touching of sisters on every side was now sisters on two sides and comb on the others, cold, with the stores in it that nobody came to.

The saying was mostly the wrong place. That was how it had gone, through the dark: the gap that kept time had widened along the comb, wing by wing, until the saying was a thin thing around a silence instead of a silence inside a saying. It was still carried. Everything was still carried. There were fewer to carry it.

The light had lengthened. She knew it through the wood, and by the old pull in her that said *cells, small ones, warm them* — and there were no small ones. The cells at the middle were clean and empty, and had been cleaned again, and were empty.

Then the world opened.

Light, and a little smoke, thin, not the choking kind. The big slow warmth was over them, the one that had come at dusk and stood, all through the cold. It lifted the comb beside hers and set it back. It lifted hers. She held on, because holding on was what there was, and the air moved across her worn wings, and for a moment she was in the whole brightness of the day with nothing over her at all.

The comb went back. The world closed. The heaviness stayed above for some time, pressing a little on the wood, the way a sister rests.

::::: scene 2

:::pov brother-fintan

He told them first. He kept to the form, because he did better with one form than with a new one each time.

To the third hive, with his hand on it: "Your queen is dead. Since the autumn. You told me, and I didn't know what I was told. You're going in with the second, this afternoon. You're to know it."

To the second, which was flying well and paid him no attention: "The third has lost its queen. What's left of them is coming in over you. You're to know it."

He did not tell the first hive anything, and then went back and told it, because it was the same household.

The chaplain held the box. She was young for the rota, between a station parish and a ship, and she had been at the hill nine days and rendered a great deal of wax, and the rota had trained her as it trained all of them, to ask the plain question and not the polite one. She let him get the two combs across the stand before she asked it.

"Would it have lived, if you'd opened it in the autumn?"

"I don't know."

"Would more of it?"

"I don't know that either. It might. It might be I'd have chilled it and had nothing to carry." He was pricking the paper with a nail, a few small holes, enough to let the smell through and nothing else. He had taken the sheet from the back of the commonplace book, a blank leaf. He had thought, for about as long as it deserved, of using the page with the autumn's entry on it, and had not, because that would have been making a gesture, and the bees do not read. "I've written down what I know. Queen failed before the first frost, by the sound. Did not open. Those two are confirmed. Whether opening would have served — that goes in the other column."

"You keep columns."

"I kept them for thirty-one years. They don't stop being right because the instrument has wings."

She watched him lay the paper over the second hive and set the box on it. Below the sheet the second hive's voice went up a little, the sound of a colony that has noticed something overhead and has not decided about it.

"I'll tell you what troubles me," he said, since she had asked plainly and was owed it. "It isn't the hive. A hive is a hive; they're lost every winter by better keepers. It's that I had a whole office built on standing beside the thing. I prayed it. I offered the attention — I'd have told you in the autumn that the attention was the offering. And the hive went on dying under it, at exactly the rate it would have died if I'd stayed in bed."

"Is that true?"

He considered it, because she had not said it to comfort him. "No," he said. "Not exactly. If I'd stayed in bed I'd not have known which to open first today. I'd have gone down the row in order, and it's turning cold again by four." He looked at the box. "That's the size of it. Four months of watching, and what it bought is that four hundred bees were carried at noon and not at three."

"That's not nothing."

"It isn't. I'd want it weighed at what it is, though, and not at what I felt it was at the time."

The chaplain was quiet. She had a chaplain's sentence available — the rota has several, and one of them is that the record is kept, and so is the person — and he watched her think of it and decline to say it, for which he thought the better of her.

"I'm not asking to be let off," he said.

"No," she said. "I wasn't offering."

She went back to the wax. He stayed by the stand until the light began to go, and it was the same standing it had been all winter, and he could not have said from inside it whether it was the discipline or the habit. He stood anyway. He did not know a way to find out that did not involve being there.

:::pov hive-bee

The world had gone up into the air and come down somewhere else.

The comb was the same comb. The stores smelled of home. But under her feet, where the floor should have been, there was a thing that was not wax and not wood — thin, dry, giving a little — and under that there was a saying.

It was not their saying. It was the thousands. She had not been in the thousands since the cold came, and here it was, close as a sister and not reachable, the great unbroken sound of everyone, coming up through the thin dry floor into her feet. It had no gap in it. She stood on it and it beat through her, whole.

It did not smell of home. That was the difficulty. The sisters around her went still and tight, and from below there came the small hard sounds of others at the thin floor, working at it, and nobody could have said whether what was coming through was welcome.

It took the dark and part of the light after. The thin floor went the way everything soft goes — a mouthful, and another, carried off. The smell came up through the first holes a little at a time, and their own smell went down, and by the time the holes were wide enough for a body there was no longer one smell and another. There was the smell of where they were.

A stranger came up through the floor and touched her, feelers to feelers, and was not a stranger.

She went down. The comb below was warm past anything she remembered, and in the cells at the middle of it there were small ones, curled and white, a great many, and the old pull in her that had had nothing to answer it since the light lengthened had something now. She turned to the nearest cell. She was old for the work. Nobody was keeping count of that.

The wrong place did not close. She carried it down with her, as she had carried it all the cold; it was in how she beat, a half-beat behind, and in the sisters who had come with her. But the saying here was very large, and it took the half-beat in the way the comb takes one more wing, and went on.

:::pov brother-fintan

The night office at Cnoc na mBeach is said alone, except when it is not. The chaplain asked whether she might sit in, and Fintan said the door did not have a lock and never had.

He lit the one lamp. The psalter fell open where it always fell, at the watchers and the morning, and tonight he turned it back by hand, three leaves, to a psalm he did not usually read at that hour because it is not kind to his trade. *Unless Yahweh watches over the city, the watchman guards it in vain.*

He read it aloud, and let it stand. He had built ten years on the watchman. He had prayed every night for the ones posted to a small steady wrongness, and he had never until this winter put the plain question to the prayer itself: whether a watch that changes nothing is a watch, or a man keeping himself company with a hive. The psalm did not tell him. It does not say the watchman should go home. It says in vain, *unless*, and leaves the watchman on the wall with the condition.

He found he could stay on the wall with it. That was less than an answer and he did not dress it as one.

He prayed for the watchers, as on every night. He did not take back a word of it. But he added, where he had not before, the ones who had stood beside a thing and should have opened it, and the ones who had opened a thing and should have stood — and that the two might be forgiven for not being able to tell, from inside, which they were. He asked it of someone. That was the difference between this hill and some others, and he had never found a way to say it that was not simply saying it: that he did not think the attention went nowhere. He thought it was received. He could not have put that in either column, and did not try to.

Before the Amen, he kept the forty seconds.

The chaplain kept them with him. She kept them well, without fidgeting, which is rarer than it should be in a chaplain. Forty seconds for the discrepancy: the one at Threshold, which was still the record's business and not his; the one in the third hive, which was not a discrepancy any longer but a finding, read and entered and carried across the stand in a box; and the one in him, which was where it had been. He noticed that the first two had moved since the autumn and the third had not. He noticed, too — it came late in the count, around the thirtieth second, and he let it come — that he had now been shown what it looked like when a thing was attended to for four months by a man who did not open it.

He did not open it. Not tonight. But he knew, as he had not known in the autumn, that standing beside it was a decision and had a cost, and that the cost was being paid by something whether he looked or not.

The silence ended. He said the Amen into it, and the chaplain said hers a half-beat behind his, as people do who are not used to each other's time.

She asked him one thing at the door, plainly. "Was there a third?"

"There's a third."

"Is it mine to ask about?"

"No," said Fintan. "But you're the first to count them."

Two days later the paper was on the grass in front of the second hive — grey shreds no bigger than a fingernail, carried out and dropped off the landing lip, as a colony carries out anything that is finished with. He stood and listened. There had been a seam in the second hive's voice the first evening, and a fainter one the next morning, and now there was not: one voice, and a little more of it than before.

He picked the shreds out of the grass so the wind would not have them. Then he took the cloth off the third hive's rail, where it had hung since the autumn, and folded it, and did not put it away.
