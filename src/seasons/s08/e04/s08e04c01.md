::::: scene 1

:::pov hive-bee

She had found a place, and she was saying so.

It was a long flight: over the wall, over the low ground where the water stood, to a dead tree with a split in it the height of a sister's short flight above the grass. Inside the split it was dry. It was dark all the way up, and wide enough for comb, and it smelled of old wood and of nothing that lived there. She had walked the whole inside of it, up and across, the way a place is measured, and come home with the size of it in her legs.

Home was not home today. Home was the thousands hanging in the open from a thorn branch, sister holding sister, with the old mother somewhere in the middle of the hold and the light on all of them. There was no comb. There was no saying of the kind that lives in comb. There was only the hold, and the backs of sisters, and on the backs of sisters the ones like her, walking.

She walked it. *So far. That side of the light.* A straight run across the backs with her body shaking, a turn, the run again. Each run said the same thing, because the place had not moved. Sisters followed her on the run with their feelers on her, and some went off to see, and she kept on.

There was another walking near her. It was short, hardly a run at all: *close, close, this side.* She knew what it meant, because she had been. Everyone had been. It was the box in the row, a few wing-beats from the old door, that smelled of stores and old wax and of no one. It was a good smell. It was a strange thing for a place to smell so much of home and have no one in it.

More were walking the short run than the long one. That was not a thing she counted. It was a thing in the hold: a lean in it, the way the warmth leans. She did not stop for that. A scout says what she has found for as long as it stays in her legs, and it was still in her legs.

Under the thorn, a heaviness had come and was standing. There were two of them. One was the big slow one that came at dusk. The other was lighter on the ground and did not know how to stand yet.

:::pov brother-fintan

The cadet came up the lane a little before eleven, and the second hive went at noon. Fintan would have said afterwards that the hill did not arrange things, and he held to that. He noted the order of events anyway.

She gave her name at the gate the way they all did the first time, name and rank, as though the hill kept a duty officer. Hanley. Cadet. She was out of uniform and carried a Corps kit-bag, and she had the look he had seen on a number of them over ten years: somebody who has walked a long way uphill to ask a question and has understood, in the last hundred yards, that she does not know what it is.

"Fintan," he said. "There's a veil on the hook inside the wax-house door. You'll want it in about an hour."

He knew that because he had opened the second hive ten days before. He opened hives now. He had opened this one on the first fair morning in a fortnight, with very little smoke, and found it packed wall to wall, and along the bottom edges of three combs a row of cells built long and downward, each the size of the end of his finger, which is how a colony makes queens. A colony does that when it means to go. He had taken in four hundred bees in the spring and they had raised their share of the brood, and the hive had done well on it, and the hive doing well was the reason it was about to divide.

There were things a keeper could do about that. He could cut the cells out and buy a fortnight. He could split the hive himself, by hand, and call it management. He had stood over the open hive with the comb in his hands and gone through them, and had done neither. He had closed it up, and cleaned the third hive to bare walls, and put back two frames of its own old comb, and left it standing empty in the row with its door open.

It was not the standing of the winter. He had been clear with himself about that, because since the spring he no longer took his own word for which kind of standing he was doing. This one had a box in it, and a sheet, and a date.

Scouts had been at the third hive's door for three days. That was confirmed. What they would make of it was in the other column.

At noon the second hive came out. It came the way they do, all at once and without hurry, a brown pouring off the landing lip that went up and became a noise and then a weather. The cadet stood in the wax-house door with the veil in her hand, not on, and watched ten thousand bees fill the air over the yard and turn there, slowly, a wheel thirty feet across with nothing at the middle.

"Is that wrong?" she said.

"No," said Fintan. "That's a hive in order. Put the veil on."

They settled on the thorn above the yard in a quarter of an hour, a hanging weight the length of his forearm, and went quiet.

::::: scene 2

:::pov brother-fintan

A swarm on a branch is the gentlest thing a keeper handles. It has no brood and no stores to defend, and nothing it owns but itself. He told the cadet so, and she nodded as people nod when they have been told a thing is safe by the man who is not holding the box.

She held the box. Nobody stays idle near bees on that hill.

He did not shake them at once. He stood under the thorn for some minutes and read the outside of the cluster, where the scouts were walking. There were two walkings that he could make out. One was short and tight, low on the near side. The other was a long straight run that pointed east of the lodge, well out over the wall, and the bee doing it was going at it like a junior hand who has found the fault and cannot get the watch officer to look.

"What are they doing?"

"Deciding where to live. Each of those has found somewhere and is giving the bearing. The rest go and look, and come back and agree or don't. When enough of them are giving one bearing, the whole lot lifts and goes there."

"And where's that?"

"The short one's my empty hive, I'd say. Twelve feet from where they started. The long one I don't know. East, and a fair way. There's a dead ash on the far side of the low ground with a split in it. It might be that."

"Which one's winning?"

"The hive, by a little. I wouldn't sign for it."

She was quiet. He could feel her doing the sum: that if he waited, they might walk into his hive by themselves, on their own bearing, and he would have had no hand in it. He had done the same sum. On the other side of it there was rain coming by evening, and a dead ash he could not climb.

"Hold it right under," he said. "Higher. They'll be heavier than you think."

He took the branch in both hands and brought it down once, hard, and the cluster let go of itself and fell into the box in one piece, like something poured. The cadet said a word the Academy does not teach and did not drop it. He laid a board across, leaving a gap, and they set the box in the grass, and the bees that were still in the air came down to it over the next half hour, because the queen was in it and that was the only bearing that mattered to them now.

They sat on the stand rail and waited. This was where a visitor usually got to the question, when both of them had their hands empty.

"I asked for a posting," she said. "A year ago. It came through eleven days ago. I haven't acknowledged it."

"Is it the one you asked for?"

"Exactly the one."

He waited.

"I thought you'd say something about them." She nodded at the box. "The ones that go and the ones that stay. I thought that was the kind of place this was."

"I can tell you about them," said Fintan. "I'd not hang much on it. The half that went has no comb, no stores and an old queen, and if I hadn't a box it would have a wet night on a thorn. The half that stayed has everything except a queen. It's sitting in there on a dozen sealed cells, and it needs one of them to hatch and fly and mate and get home past the swallows, and if she doesn't, that's my third hive over again by the autumn." He looked at the second hive, which was flying thinly and steadily, as if nothing had happened to it. "There's not a safe half. They were one hive this morning. That's all I can confirm."

"That isn't advice."

"No. I kept instruments for thirty-one years. I can give you the reading. I never had the certificate for the other thing."

She very nearly laughed. He thought that was the first easy breath she had drawn since the gate.

"Two columns," he said. "What you know and what you don't. Say them."

She took her time over it, which he thought well of. "I know it's the posting I asked for. I know when I have to answer by." She stopped. "I don't know if the person who asked for it is the one who'd be going."

"That's a well-kept second column," said Fintan. "I've seen thirty-year officers who couldn't have written that line."

He did not tell her what to put under it. He had not been asked that, and in any case he did not know.

At dusk, before the rain, he spread the sheet from the grass up to the third hive's landing lip and tipped the box out at the bottom of it. They went up. They always do: a slow brown tide walking uphill over white cloth toward a dark slot, and at the slot the first of them turned and stood with their tails raised, fanning the smell of the place back down the sheet to the rest.

:::pov hive-bee

The world fell.

There was the hold, and then there was no branch in it, and then there was a floor, and walls, and the thousands in a heap in the dark with the old mother under them somewhere. The hold made itself again. That is the first thing done. Sister found sister and the heap became a hanging, from a roof this time, in a place that smelled of the big slow heaviness and of nothing else.

She had the far place in her legs still. She walked it once, across the backs, in the dark, *so far, that side of the light*, and no one followed. There was no light to say it against. The short walking had stopped too. Nobody was saying a place. The mother was here, and the thousands went where the mother was.

Later the floor tipped and there was grass, and cloth, pale, going up. Ahead, up the pale slope, there was the smell that sisters make with their tails lifted when they mean *here, it is here, come in*. She knew the door before she reached it. It was the close place. Old wax, old stores, no one.

She went in with the rest. There was not another thing to do, and she did not look for one. Inside there was comb already, two pieces, standing, clean. The mother went by her in the dark, in a crowd of sisters, toward the middle of it.

The far place stayed where it was. She had measured it and it was good, and that did not stop being so. It was in her as a bearing and a size: the dry dark, all the way up. She would not walk it again, because a walking is for a hold that has not chosen, and this one was housed.

By the time the rain came on the roof, she was hanging in a chain of sisters with the wax starting in her. A scout is only a scout until there is comb to build.

:::pov brother-fintan

The cadet asked whether she might sit in on the night office, and then said she was not sure she was of the Communion, or of anything, and Fintan told her what he told all of them: the Communion's whole test was turning up, and she had walked four miles uphill.

He lit the one lamp. The psalter fell open at the watchers and the morning, and he left it there and took down the other book, because the day had been about something else. He read one verse. *By faith, Abraham, when he was called, obeyed to go out to the place which he was to receive for an inheritance. He went out, not knowing where he went.*

He let it stand. He was careful with it. It was a text a man could lay across a young officer's shoulders like a yoke, and he had not read it at her. He had read it because he had spent the afternoon with ten thousand creatures who had gone out not knowing where, and had ended the day twelve feet from where they began, in a box he had chosen for them. He did not think the verse was about that. He noticed, though, that the verse says *called*, and that nobody had called the bees but the bees, and that the cadet had asked for her posting herself, a year ago. He did not know which of those the verse would count. He set it in the second column.

He noticed also that the text has nothing to say about the household that stayed where it was, and that somebody stayed.

He prayed for the watchers, as on every night. Tonight he added the ones gone out, and the ones sitting on sealed cells waiting on a thing they could do nothing to hurry. He did not pray that the cadet would choose well. He did not know which choice that was, and he would not ask for an outcome he could not name. He asked that she be kept, whichever it was. He believed that was heard. He kept that where he kept it.

Before the Amen, the forty seconds.

She kept them badly, the way most do the first time. She breathed in around the twentieth second as if to speak, and thought better of it. He did not mind that. The clock at Threshold was still the record's. The hive was a finding, and now a full box besides. The one in him was where it had been in the spring. Three months. He counted that now. He had not used to count it.

The silence ended. He said the Amen into it, and she said hers after a moment, in a low voice.

It rained until three. In the morning the third hive was flying. It was a thin traffic at the slot, and here and there a bee stood on the lip facing inward and made short flights backward, up and around, learning the look of the door. Whether they would stay was not confirmed. A hived swarm can change its mind for a week. He would know when there was brood, and not before.

The cadet was at the gate with her kit-bag at half past seven. She had scrubbed the box and hung the veil on its hook. She thanked him, and he said there was nothing to thank him for. She said, "I wrote the second column down," and he said that was the right place for it. Neither of them said which way she was going from the bottom of the lane. He found he had no opinion to hide. That surprised him a little, in a man who had spent thirty-one years having opinions about postings.

He told them after she had gone. He started with the third hive this time, since it was the newest of the household.

"A guest has gone down the hill. Hanley. A cadet. I don't know where to, and I'd say she doesn't yet. You're to know it."

He told the first hive the same. Then he stood a while at the second, with his hand flat on the cover, and told it too. It had the longer wait.
