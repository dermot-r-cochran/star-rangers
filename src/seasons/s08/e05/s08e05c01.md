::::: scene 1

::: pov orlagh-dunne

Ten past six in the evening, the second day of the thaw. Orlagh Dunne wrote the time first, as she always did, and then the place, which was the drowned ground, sixty yards past the wall.

The Sorley girl had come to her door. She had said that Eoin Brack had gone through the gap with a lamp, and then she had stood there, having said it, and Dunne had not stopped to ask her anything else. She took the long ladder from the hall and four people from their suppers.

He was in to the chest. He had kept the lamp up out of it, which was how they found him so quickly, and he had stopped shouting by the time they reached the wall, because shouting moved him and he had worked out that moving was the wrong thing to do. He was twenty, and not a fool on an ordinary day. They laid the ladder flat on the ground and sent the lightest of them out along it with the rope. It took forty minutes. She wrote that down. He came out without his boots, grey to the collar and very cold, and by eight he was beside the stove in the hall with a blanket on him, drinking something hot and not looking at anybody.

The gap in the wall had been stopped with two hurdles since the autumn. He had lifted them out and leaned them against the stones, side by side, tidily. She noted that. It was what a man does when he expects to come back the same way and put them in again.

She asked him why. She asked it plainly, in front of the others, because it would be asked behind her if she did not.

He said the ground was kept. He said everybody knew it. Three children had walked it in the dark without a lamp, and the house on the hill had sat up all that night, and the old captain had said herself that somebody walked them home and that it was permitted. He had thought that if it was permitted for them, a grown man with a lamp might at least go and look at the line they took.

"Who told you she said that?"

His aunt. She had it from Rhona Maddren.

Dunne did not ask where Rhona Maddren had it. Rhona was in the hall, at the back, with her coat still on, and Dunne had known her forty years.

She sat up with the book after they had gone. *Cause*, the form said. She had left a line empty on the page for the autumn, the one after *route*, and she had been content to leave it. This one she could fill in. She wrote: *believed the closed track passable, on report.* She could stand over that. She looked at *on report* for a while. A wall was a thing she knew how to mend, and she would have the gap built up in stone by the end of the week. She did not know how to build up a sentence. But she knew where this one had come down from, and in the morning she had the pony put to the cart.

::: pov peran-slade

Peran had the oil can in one hand and the door in the other when the cart came into the yard, and they knew what it was about before the woman had climbed down. It was the hour. Nobody drove two hours up the lane to arrive at ten in the morning with good news.

They had seen Orlagh Dunne twice, across the settlement's hall, and never heard her speak. She spoke now without raising her voice and without any preamble, standing in the hall of the house with her hat still on, to the chapter leader, who had come out of the small room and stood with one hand on the back of a chair.

"A man went onto the drowned ground last night. He's out, and he'll do. He went because he'd been told your house said the children were walked home, and that it was permitted. I've come to ask whether your house said it."

Peran felt the sentence arrive whole, like a tool they had lent out and now saw in a stranger's hand. They had said it once, to one person.

It had been midwinter, at the settlement's store, where the house sent for lamp oil. Rhona Maddren had been at the counter. She had turned round and looked at the novice's grey coat and said, without any greeting: *your door lamp was lit that night. I saw it from the yard. All night. What does she say it was?* Peran had looked at the woman's face. Two boys, nine and eleven, nine weeks home. And Peran had said: *she thinks somebody walked them home.*

That was all. They had not said *permitted*. That word had got out some other way, or been added on the road, and it made no difference. They had been told, on the morning itself, that what the chapter leader thought was not what the record would carry, and that they were to know the difference for the rest of their life. They had known it. They had stood at a shop counter knowing it, and had handed a mother the half that was warm and kept the half that was true.

The chapter leader had not yet answered. She was looking at Dunne, and she had drawn breath.

"It was me," said Peran. "I said it. Not her. She said it to me, and I carried it down."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The oil can was heavy and they did not put it down.

"Thank you," said Asteria, in the voice she used for a report properly made. She did not look round. To Dunne she said, "It's accurate. I'll come down and say so in your hall, and say the rest with it. Will the cart take two?"

It would take two. It was not Peran she meant. She told them to keep the house, and the door lamp as it always was.

"And no brighter," said Peran.

"No," she said.

::: pov asteria tier=contemplative

She had drawn the breath to say *it was mine*, and the novice had got there first. She let them have it. It was a thing a person should be allowed to say for themselves, at that age, in front of a witness. She had seen a good many officers who were never given the chance and went on owing it for years.

It was hers, all the same. She had told Peran what she thought, and she had told them how to label it. Then she had sent them downhill for lamp oil with the thing in their pocket, as though a label were a kind of lid. She knew better than that. She had spent a century in a service that did not let a reading leave the room without its error written on the same line. She had given a novice the reading on one line and the error on another.

A mother had asked. She thought about that as the cart went down, with the pony picking its way and Dunne beside her saying nothing, which she was grateful for. If Rhona Maddren had asked her, at a counter, with that face, she was not certain what would have come out. She had a great deal of practice at saying nothing. She was not sure it would have been enough.

"I need to know what your house did that night," Dunne said, a mile down. She said it to the pony's ears.

"Nothing," said Asteria. "I sat in a chair. I thought of lighting every window, and did not, because it would have drawn them uphill into the wind. I kept the door lamp as it always is. That is all of it."

"That's what you'll say."

"That's what I'll say first."

Dunne drove for a while. "I left a line empty in the book, in the autumn. After the route."

"So did I."

"I'd as soon it stayed empty."

"It will. I'm not coming down to fill it in for you." She looked out over the wall at the long curve of the low ground, the colour of the hill, with the thaw standing on it in grey pools. She had known this ground the way she had once known a boundary zone, and the comparison held further than she liked. Once, in a zone, a ship of hers had come through a place it had no business coming through. It was a single transit. She had logged what the instruments gave, and had thought what she thought about it. The Corps had never entered it on a chart as a lane, and no captain who read that log afterwards had taken it for one. That was not because they were more sensible than Eoin Brack. It was because the log said, on the same line, *not repeatable; cause not established.*

She had not changed her mind about the autumn. She noticed that she was checking, and she checked properly, as she would have checked a reading she was fond of. Three children, a mile of that, in the dark, by the one line. She still thought somebody had walked them. She still thought it had been permitted. She found that neither of those thoughts had a second half. Permitted, that night, to those three. It said nothing about the next night, or the next person. A permission is not a road. She had known that perfectly well in the autumn and had not said it, because in the autumn nobody was proposing to walk on it.

"He leaned the hurdles against the wall," Dunne said. "Tidily."

"Yes," said Asteria. "He would have."

::: pov orlagh-dunne

They filled the hall. Dunne had not called it for any hour, and they filled it anyway, by five, because the cart had been seen on the lane with two in it.

She had offered the old woman a chair, and it had been declined. Asteria stood at the stove end, with one hand on the bench back, and did not wait for quiet. She got it.

"Three children came home in the autumn across the drowned ground," she said. "I am glad of it and so are you. That night my house did nothing. I sat up in a chair. The door lamp was lit because it is always lit. Nobody in my house went out, or sent anything, or knows how those children found the line. I have been asked what I think, and I told one person, and it has come down the hill without the rest of what I said. So here is the rest of it. What I think is that they were helped. I do not know it. I can't show it to you. I would not walk on it, and I would not let anyone I had charge of walk on it. The ground is the same ground it was in your grandmothers' time. That is all I have."

It was Brack's aunt who asked. Somebody had to. "But you believe it. You believe they were walked home."

"I do."

"Then why not again?"

"Because I believe it of one night," said Asteria. "I've believed a good many things in a long life and been wrong about a fair share of them. If I'm wrong about this, nobody is hurt, as long as nobody stands on it. Your nephew stood on it."

Eoin Brack, on the side bench, looked at his feet, which were in borrowed boots.

Rhona Maddren had not sat down. She was by the door with her coat on again, and her face was the face of a woman working out how much of a night on a ladder was hers. Dunne saw it, and said, to the room and not to her, that a report had been passed on in good faith by several people and acted on by one, and that the book would say so and name nobody. Nobody was going to be blamed in her hearing. She had not allowed it for the shepherd in the autumn either.

Then old Sorley, who meant no harm by it, turned on the bench to his granddaughter. She was sitting on the floor by the stove with her knees up. He said, "You led them, girl. Could you do it again?"

The hall went quiet.

"No," said the girl.

She thought about it some more. She was ten, and she gave it the time it needed.

"I wouldn't," she said.

Dunne wrote down both. They were not the same answer, and she did not know which of them the child meant. She found she did not need to. Either one would do to build a wall on.

Brack was at the gap at first light with a barrow, before she was. She had not asked him. By noon it was stone to the height of her shoulder, and the two hurdles were back in the hall yard where they belonged.

::: pov peran-slade

There was nothing to do at the house all day, and it took all day to do.

Peran swept the hall. They set the three other novices to the work the three other novices would have done anyway. They filled the lamps from the can. In the afternoon they stood in the small room off the hall for a while and looked at the chair against the wall, and did not sit in it.

They had wanted to go down. They knew that, and knew why. It was their sentence, and it was being answered for in a hall two hours away by a woman who should not have been in a cart at her age. They had spent two years and more asking the house whether they were one who stays or one who goes. Today somebody had needed to go, and somebody had needed to stay, and nobody had asked Peran which they were. They had been told. It was a strange thing to be given an answer for one day only.

At dusk they lit the lamps. The hall, the stair, the long room. They went to the door lamp last. They trimmed it after the others were lit, which was the custom, and their hand stayed on the wheel.

The lane was two hours of dark, and the cart had one small lamp on it, and she was very old. A brighter door would show from the second bend. It would be a kindness. It was the same kindness, they understood, standing there with their fingers on the brass, that they had done at the shop counter: a little more light than the facts had in them, given to somebody one cared about, on a road where other people would see it too.

They took their hand off the wheel. The lamp burned as it always did.

It was full dark when the other light showed, low on the lane, small, and moving at a pony's walk. Peran stood in the doorway. They did not go down to meet it. The door was the post, and they had been given it.

The cart came into the yard. A boy from the settlement was driving. The chapter leader climbed down by herself, slowly, and stood a moment with her hand on the wheel-rim, getting the lane out of her back.

"House kept?" she said.

"Kept."

"Anything to report?"

"No."

"Good. That's most nights." She came up the step, and she looked at the lamp as she passed it, and Peran saw her see that it was no brighter. She said nothing about it. In the hall she stopped.

"You gave her a true sentence," she said. "I want that understood. It was true when I said it and when you did. It went down the hill without its weight, and that was my packing before it was yours." She began to unwind her scarf. "The child was asked tonight if she could do it again. She said no. Then she said she wouldn't."

Peran waited for the rest. There was not any.

"Which did she mean?"

"I've no idea," said Asteria. "Put the kettle on."

Afterwards they opened the day-book. They turned back, first, to the autumn. *Three children lost on the high ground; home before first light.* The space beneath it was as she had left it. Peran looked at it for some time. They were, they found, no longer waiting for it to be filled.

They turned to the day and wrote, under the date the house used: *Chapter leader to the settlement and back. House kept. Door lamp as usual.*

It did not look like much. They left it as it was.
