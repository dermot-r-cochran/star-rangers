:::pov brother-fintan

The order from the circuit came to two hundred and sixty candles: sixty each for three way-chapels, and eighty for a ship. Fintan wrote it on the wax-house wall in chalk, where he wrote every order, and under it the sum he always did next. He had wax in hand for about a hundred and ninety.

So it was a rendering week. The honey had come off the first hive in the last days of summer, three boxes of it, and the cappings were waiting in a tub. There was the burr comb he had scraped all season and thrown in a bucket. And at the back of the wax-house, stacked in a crate with a board over it since the first warm day of the year, there were the combs of the old third hive.

He had not rendered them in the spring. Two had gone back into the box for the swarm. The other six he had put by, telling himself that old brood comb yields poorly and was not worth a fire by itself, which was true. It was also the comb the colony had wintered and died on, and he had not been ready to melt it. He knew that was the reason. He did not pretend otherwise now.

He put it in the pot with the rest.

Wax is rendered in water. The comb goes in, and the water is brought up slowly, and the wax lets go of everything it has been holding and rises. What it leaves behind stays in the cloth: old cocoons, a winter's dead, the dark papery lining of ten generations of cells. Old comb is mostly that. He pressed the cloth and looked at what six combs had given him, floating gold on the top of the pot, and it was more than he had allowed for. It was not a great deal. It was clean.

The chaplain came up the lane on the second day, a little after noon, with a hand-cart for the ship's eighty. She was the one the rota had sent in the spring, who had held the box. She had her posting now. The ship went out on the ninth, and she had asked to fetch the candles herself, because she knew the way.

She rolled her sleeves at the wax-house door without being asked. He set her to dipping. It is slow work and cannot be hurried: a row of wicks on a rod, down into the wax and up, and a wait while it sets, and down again. Thirty dips make a candle. She found the rhythm inside an hour, and after that they did not need to talk, and for the most part did not.

She asked after the hives. He gave her the reading. The swarm had stayed in the third box. There had been brood in three weeks, and it was going into the cold small but in order. The second had taken longer. Its first queen had flown and not come home. It had raised another from the last of the cells, late, and she was laying. But the hive was light, and he was feeding it, and he would not sign for it before the spring. The first was as it always was.

"And the cadet?" she said. The rota passes news along, even when it is only that someone called.

"Went down the hill in the morning. I never heard which way after that. I wasn't owed it."

She nodded and dipped.

She did not ask about the third. He noticed. She had counted them in the spring, in the forty seconds: the clock, the hive, and one more. She had the kind of memory the rota trains, and she had not forgotten. She did not ask because he had told her it was not hers to ask about, and she had taken him at his word. He found, over two afternoons of dipping, that being taken at his word was harder to stand next to than being pressed would have been.

It was ten months since the autumn. He counted that now. Four months he had stood beside the third hive, and he knew what that had bought and what it had cost, to the handful. Then he had carried what was left across the stand, and hived a swarm, and seen a cadet off. He had opened every hive on the hill when it wanted opening. He had done all of that in the same six months in which he had gone on standing beside the other thing every night, for forty seconds, with his hands at his sides.

On the second evening, the last rod of the ship's eighty was hanging to set. He was scraping the pot. He thought about what was in those candles, which was the comb of a hive that had told him something in the only way it could. And he thought, for no reason he could trace, about the form. *You're to know it.*

He told the hives every death, every birth and every departure. He had done so for ten years. He kept the custom on the old reasoning, which he neither claimed nor dismissed: that a colony left uninformed about the household it belongs to will sicken or leave. He had told them about riggers he had never met.

There was one thing in the household they had never been told. By his own form he was a decade in arrears.

He put down the scraper. He told the chaplain he would be a while at the stand, and she said she would bank the fire.

::::: scene 2

:::pov hive-bee

The light was going, and she was at the door.

That was the work now. She had been a cleaner of cells and then a feeder of small ones and then a maker of wax, and now she stood on the lip with the others who stood there, and touched what came in. A sister, home late, heavy. A sister. Something that was not a sister, striped the same and smelling of nothing, turned back. The cold was coming up out of the grass. Behind her the thousands were drawing together for the night, and the saying came out through the door past her, warm, one saying, with no gap in it that she had ever known.

The tread came. It was the big slow one, and nobody at the door lifted for it. It came most dusks and stood.

Tonight it came close. The roof took a weight, flat and still, as it sometimes did. And then the sound began.

She knew that sound. It was the low one the heaviness makes, that is not wings. It came now and again, down through the roof into the comb: a short thing, a few beats of it, the same shape each time, and then it stopped and the weight went. It had no smell. A saying with no smell is not for the thousands. They let it through, as they let through rain on the roof.

It did not stop.

It went on past where it stopped. It went low, and there were holes in it. Once there was a hole so long that the sister beside her turned back to the door, taking it for finished, and then it came again, lower. It was not the same shape over and over. It was one long shape, with a beginning. It went on while the grass got colder and the last of the late ones came in over her back.

She did not know what it was. Nobody did. But it was in the wood, and from the wood it was in the comb, and comb does not choose. Comb takes what is put into it, warmth and stores and every wing's small saying, and holds it. The low sound went in among the rest. The thousands' own saying did not change for it. It did not push it out, either.

Then there was the short shape, the one she knew, at the very end. A few beats. The weight came off the roof.

The tread went along the row. Later, fainter, through the ground and the stand, the long sound came again from where the next ones lived. It was as long as before, and the holes were in the same places. Later still it came a third time, fainter again.

Then the tread went away up the hill. It was the same tread. She could not have said it was lighter. She touched the next one in, a sister, and the dark came down, and the door was closed with bodies for the night.

::::: scene 3

:::pov brother-fintan

He came up to the lodge in full dark. The chaplain had banked the wax-house fire and was sitting on the step with her coat on. She did not get up, or ask.

He had thought he might feel lighter. He noted that he did not, and he noted also that he had half expected to. That would have been a poor reason for doing it, if it had been the reason. His hands were cold. It had taken longer than a telling takes. He had said it three times, once to each hive, in the same words, because he did better with one form. The words had been hard to find the first time. They had been harder the second time, when he knew what was coming.

The words are not set down here. He did not set them in the commonplace book either.

He lit the one lamp. She came in and sat where she had sat in the spring. The psalter fell open at the watchers and the morning, and he turned it back a long way, to a psalm he had read past for ten years without ever stopping in it. He read one verse aloud. *I was mute with silence. I held my peace, even from good. My sorrow was stirred.*

He let it stand. *Even from good.* He had called the silence a discipline, and for the hive it had been one, in part. He had weighed that in the spring and would not weigh it again. But the psalmist does not say he kept silent from harm. The psalmist says that there was a good to be had from speaking, and he held his peace from that as well, and called it guarding his ways.

He prayed for the watchers, as on every night. He did not take back a word. He added the ones who have kept a thing so long that the keeping has become the post, and who can no longer tell which of the two they are standing guard over. He asked that somebody trouble them. He believed, as he had always believed, that the asking was heard. Tonight there was a thing beside the belief that had not been there before, and it was small, and he looked at it squarely. He had done something that afternoon. He had not been given to know anything about it. He did not join the two.

Before the Amen, the forty seconds.

She kept them with him. The clock at Threshold: the record's, still. The hive: a finding, and a box of bees in order, and eighty candles hanging on rods in the dark of the wax-house. The one in him.

It had moved.

It had not moved far. He was exact about it, since he could be exact here if nowhere else. It had been said aloud, once, in the open air, to three boxes of insects that could not read it back. In thirty-one years of watches he had learned what that was worth. A reading called out on an empty deck has been called. It has not been acknowledged, and nothing has been corrected by it. But it is no longer only on the instrument. He had spent ten years calling the thing patient, like an unread instrument. He had read it. He could not call it unread again.

The silence ended. He said the Amen. She said hers with his this time, on the beat.

At the door she asked the one question, plainly, as she had in the spring.

"You were a long time at the stand. Was it a death?"

"No. Not a birth or a departure, either. The form doesn't cover it. I used the form anyway."

"Is it mine to ask about now?"

"No." He banked the lamp. "But it wants saying to something that can answer back. I know that now. I didn't in the spring."

"Will you?"

"I don't know. I didn't know I'd do this until I'd put the scraper down." He thought about it. "I'd not sign for it."

"I wasn't asking you to sign," she said. "I'm through again when the ship is. The rota comes round."

She did not say anything else, and he did not want her to.

In the morning they packed the ship's eighty in straw, in the hand-cart. He could not have told her which of them had the third hive's wax in it. All of them, probably, a little. It had gone into one pot, and wax does not keep the name of its hive. They would be lit on a ship he would never board, for people he would never meet, and whatever they were burning for, the hive that made part of them had never been asked.

She went down the lane at eight. He wrote the day's line in the commonplace book: *Told the hives. All three. The same words each time.* He put the date on it and did not add to it.

Then he went down to the stand, since a departure is a departure.

"The chaplain's gone down to her ship," he said, to the first, and the second, and the third. "You're to know it."

It took no time at all. He stood a moment longer at each hive than the telling needed.
