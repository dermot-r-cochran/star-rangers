::::: scene 1

:::pov brother-fintan

"Is that all of it?"

The carrier asked it of the crates, not of him. She had three on the cart already, sixty candles to a crate, packed in straw and tied, and she was looking at the wax-house floor for a fourth.

"That's all of it," said Fintan. "The ship's went down in the summer."

"Anything else to go?"

She asked that every time. She had asked it every second Thursday for nine years, with one boot on the wheel-hub and the pony already leaning. It was not a question. It was the last thing a carrier says before she takes the brake off, and the answer was a jar of honey for somebody in the settlement, or a note for the rota, or nothing.

"Not this time," he said.

She took the brake off.

He stood at the head of the lane and watched the cart go down, which he did not usually do. It was a clear cold afternoon near the end of the year. A year, to the week, since the third hive had gone a half-tone wrong at dusk and he had hung a cloth on the rail and climbed to the lodge to pray about it.

He had been asked a plain question. He had given the usual answer, and it had not been true. There was something else to go. It had been ready to go since the end of summer, when he had said it aloud three times to three hives and told a chaplain at the lodge door that it wanted saying to something that could answer back. He had said he would not sign for whether he would do it. That had been honest. It had also been ten weeks ago, and he knew by now what he thought of a man who stands beside a thing and counts the weeks.

The cart went round the second bend. The carrier would be back on the Thursday fortnight.

That gave it a date. He had done better, in the spring, with a box and a sheet and a date than he had done all winter with an open evening. He went in and took the commonplace book down. He did not open it. He took a clean sheet from the back instead, and put it on the table, and went out to see to the hives while there was light. He knew better than to sit down in front of it cold.

::::: scene 2

:::pov brother-fintan

There is a way of reading a hive in the cold that is neither standing beside it nor opening it. He had known it for ten years and used it every autumn, and it had not once occurred to him, the winter before, that it was a third thing. You put two fingers under the back edge of the box and lift it an inch off the stand. The weight is the stores. A hive that goes into the frost heavy will generally come out of it. A light one will not, however well it sounds.

He hefted the first. It did not want to come up. Good.

He hefted the third, the swarm's box. It was fair. It had started late with nothing, and built, and it would do if the winter was ordinary.

He hefted the second, and it came up as easily as a kettle.

He had half known. This was the hive that had sent out the swarm, lost its first queen on her flight, and raised another late from the last of the cells. He had fed it through the end of summer and told the chaplain he would not sign for it. It sounded well. He put his ear to the wood to be sure of that, and it did: one voice, no seam in it. A year ago that would have satisfied him. The third hive had taught him what a sound could and could not carry, and this was the other half of the lesson. The third had sounded wrong and he had not known what it meant. This one sounded right, and it was starving.

He did not stand over it. There was nothing to weigh. He went up to the lodge and boiled sugar hard, and poured it in a slab on a board, and let it set while the light went. Then he carried it down, lifted the cover on the second hive for as long as it takes to say so, laid the slab flat across the tops of the combs above the cluster, and closed it. He had let cold in. He knew to the degree how much. He entered it: *second, light, fed, cover off a count of ten.*

Then he went in to the sheet of paper, with his hands still smelling of sugar, and began.

He began it four times that evening and three the next. It was not that he lacked the words. He had found the words at the stand in the summer, and he had them by heart, having said them three times. It was that the words he had found were for bees. They had needed no beginning and no account of himself, because a hive does not ask who is speaking or why now. A person would ask both. A person could also write back, or not write back. He found that every opening he tried was built to steer which of those it would be.

On the third evening he stopped trying to write a letter. He ruled the sheet the way he had ruled sheets for thirty-one years: date, post, what was observed, what was done, what was not done. He wrote it as a filing. It took an hour. It came to less than a page, which astonished him, after the room it had taken up.

He had been able to do that all along. He saw it plainly when it was done. He had been keeping the thing in columns in his head for years. What he had not had, any of those years, was the line at the top where the addressee goes.

He filled that in last.

:::pov hive-bee

The cold had come and the thousands had drawn in. They were not quite the thousands. She did not count. But the hold was small for the box, and there was a long way of empty comb between the outside of the warmth and the wall.

The saying was good. That was the strange part, and nobody found it strange, because nobody compared. It was one saying, close and whole, every wing in it. The mother was in the middle. She was young and narrow and she had come home late in the year, with the smell on her that meant she would lay. There had been small ones after that, enough of them. The sisters around her now were mostly those, born into the cold, fat with it, made to last.

What there was not, was much above them.

A hold moves in the cold. It goes up the comb slowly, a cell's height and another, eating as it climbs, and what it eats is what was put by in the light. She was near the top of the hold. She could feel with her feelers how little way there was to go before the comb ended and there was only roof.

The world tipped.

It was a small tip. The floor went up at the back and everything that hung leaned toward the door. The saying rose a tone, all together, and then the floor came down again, gently, and it was level. Nothing came in. No light, no smoke. The saying settled.

Later the roof came off.

Cold fell straight down onto the top of the hold, and light with it, and the sisters at the top flattened and held on. It was very short. Something came down where the roof had been, wide and flat and heavy, and lay across the tops of the combs. Then the roof was back and the dark was back.

The thing above them had a smell. It took a while to come down through the cold, and when it came she knew it, though she had never met it in that shape. It was the smell of stores. It was not flower stores. It was plainer than that, and hard. But a sister reached up and put her tongue to it and stayed there, and then another did.

Nobody had gone out for it. It had not been there, and then it was. The hold did not ask about that. It moved up a cell's height toward it and went on beating.

::::: scene 3

:::pov brother-fintan

The rota had sent nobody that month, so the night office was said alone again, as it had been for most of ten years. He found he noticed the empty place where a chaplain had sat. He had not expected to.

He lit the one lamp. The filing lay on the table, folded once, with the old cloth from the third hive's rail set on top of it to keep it from the draught. The cloth had been on that table since the spring, folded. He had not put it away and had not known what it was for. It turned out to be for holding a sheet of paper down for a week. He thought that was probably as much as a thing ought to be asked to mean.

The psalter fell open at the watchers and the morning. He left it, and took down the other book, and read one verse from a prophet he had always thought of as a colleague. *I will stand at my watch, and set myself on the ramparts, and will look out to see what he will say to me, and what I will answer concerning my complaint.*

He stopped there. He knew what the next verse was. The next verse begins with an answer, and he was not at the next verse. He was a man on a rampart who had, after a great many years, finally sent something out from it. The text was right about what came after that, which is looking out.

He had prayed for the watchers every night for a decade. He had been thinking of them as people who stand beside a wrongness that will not explain itself. It had not occurred to him that there was a second kind. There is the one who has made the signal and is waiting to learn whether it will be read. He had been on the other end of that all his working life. Every instrument he ever kept had been putting out a true reading, steadily, for whoever would look. He had spent thirty-one years being the one who looked, and ten more praying for the lookers. He had never once stood where the instrument stands.

He prayed for the watchers. He added the ones waiting to be read. He did not ask that the letter be answered. He did not know that an answer was owed, and he was not sure that it would be good for him to get one. He asked that whoever opened it be kept, in the opening. He believed that was heard, as he believed it every night. It was no more than he had ever believed and no less.

Before the Amen, the forty seconds.

The clock at Threshold. It was the record's, still, and a year older.

The hive. He had three in order and one of them fed, and none of them signed for.

The one in him. He turned to it, as on every night for a decade, to stand beside it. It was on the table under a cloth, in his own Corps hand, with a name at the top. For the first time there was nothing of it left in him to attend to. Not nothing: he knew better than that. The thing itself had not changed by being written down. Nothing done stops having been done. But the watching of it was no longer his to do by himself. From Thursday it would be on a cart.

He kept the forty seconds anyway. He kept them for the name at the top.

The silence ended. He said the Amen into it.

The carrier came up on the Thursday with frost still on the lane. There were no crates. She turned the cart in the yard, and put her boot on the hub.

"Anything to go?"

"One," said Fintan.

She took it. She read the outside of it, as carriers do. Her eyebrows went up a little, at the distance and nothing else.

"That's a long way," she said. "It'll go out from the landing with the rota's post. You'd be the spring getting anything back. If there's anything."

"I know how long it is."

"Right." She put it inside her coat, not in the bag, which he had not asked her to do. She took the brake off.

He did not watch the cart down this time. He went to the stand. He started with the second hive, since it had the hardest winter in front of it.

"A letter's gone down the hill," he said. "It's mine. I don't know what comes back, or whether anything does. You're to know it."

He told the third, and the first. Then he put two fingers under the back edge of the second hive and lifted. It was a little heavier than it had been. It was not heavy.

He went up to the lodge and put the cloth away.
