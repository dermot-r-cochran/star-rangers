:::pov brother-fintan

The first warm day came on a Tuesday. It was ten degrees on the lodge wall at half past ten and still rising, and Fintan went to the second hive first.

He had known all winter that he would. It was the hive that had gone into the frost light. He had found that out in the late autumn with two fingers under the back edge of the box, which is how a keeper weighs stores without opening anything, and it had come up as easily as a kettle. He had boiled sugar hard and laid a slab of it across the tops of the combs, with the cover off for a count of ten. After that there had been nothing to do for it but lift the back edge once a fortnight and enter what his fingers told him. It had got lighter every time. A hive in winter should. He had not been able to tell from the weight whether it was getting lighter at the rate of a colony eating or the rate of a colony dying, and the sound had been no help. It had sounded well in the autumn, starving.

The same fortnights had another count in them, and he had kept that one too. In the late autumn he had sent a letter down the hill. It came to less than a page, ruled like a filing, and it was about a thing he had stood beside every night for ten years and more and had said aloud to nothing but his hives. It had a name at the top. The carrier had read the outside of it and said that it was a long way, and that he would be the spring getting anything back, if there was anything. She had come up every second Thursday since, when the lane allowed, with sugar and lamp oil. He had not asked her once whether there was anything for him. She was not a woman who would carry a thing up a hill and forget to hand it over.

He did not think he had been waiting. He had weighed that in the second month, and found he could not sign for it. A man who does not ask is not thereby a man who is not listening for the cart.

He used very little smoke. He lifted the cover.

The slab was gone. There was a rim of it left along one edge, as thin as a fingernail and licked smooth, and the board he had poured it on was clean. Under where it had lain, across the tops of four combs, were the bees.

They would have gone under his two hands. He noticed that at once, since it was a size he knew. He had found that much alive in the third hive on the first warm day of the year before, and had carried it across the stand in a box, because it would not have seen the month out by itself. He took out the comb at the middle of this one, slowly, and turned it to the light.

There was a patch of sealed brood on it the size of his palm. Around the patch there were open cells, with something white curled in the bottom of each. Near the edge of them, walking, narrow, in no hurry, was the queen he had declined to sign for in the summer and again in the autumn.

He put the comb back as he had found it and closed the hive.

He took the commonplace book down before he did anything else, since a reading that is not entered has not been taken. He ruled the page as he ruled every page, the left column for what was confirmed and the right for everything else. The left took two lines. *Second hive: through the winter. Small. Queen seen; sealed brood, a palm.* In the right he wrote: *Whether it builds: not known until young bees fly. Six weeks.*

It had come through on sugar. He put it to himself in those words, because there was a warmer way to put it and he did not trust the warmer way. A slab of boiled sugar, laid on in a count of ten, had been above the cluster when the cluster came to the top of its own stores. That was confirmed, and it was all of it. He had not kept the hive alive by standing at it, and he had stood at it a good deal.

He went along the row. The first hive was as it always was. The third had brood on three combs and was flying. Then he told them. He kept the old custom, which is that the bees are told what happens in the household they belong to, and he told each hive what he had found in the other two. He ended each telling as he ended all of them: *you're to know it.*

The cold came back at four.

:::pov hive-bee

She was old now.

The hold had gone up the comb through the dark, a cell's height and another, eating as it climbed. It had come to the end of what was put by. Above the end there had been the hard plain stores that nobody had gone out for, and the hold had hung under them for a long time with its tongues to them. They had got thin. She had felt them get thin with her feelers.

There were fewer sisters. She did not count. But the hold had its edges close on every side, and the ones who had let go of it lay on the floor below, and nobody had yet had the warmth to carry them out.

The mother had begun again. That had come before the light changed, in the middle of the hold where it was warmest: one cell, and the next. There were small ones now, curled and white, and some already closed over. She had fed them with what the hard stores made in her. It was what there was to do.

Then the roof came off, and it was not cold.

Light fell in, and a little smoke, thin, and the air that came with them was soft. The comb beside hers went up into the brightness and turned there and came down again into its place. The roof came back. The big slow tread stayed near for a while. The low sound came through the wood, the short shape, a few beats of it, and went away along the row.

The door was bright, and it had a pull in it. It had had none since the cold came. She had not known that she remembered what a pull was.

She went out. Her wings were worn pale at the edges, and they held. The air in front of the door was full of sisters going round slowly, learning it again. The grass under them was flat and without colour. Past the wall, on the low ground, there was a tree gone yellow all over.

It was not the hard stores. It had a smell that went all the way in. She walked in the yellow until her legs were heavy with it. She came home along the line of the wall, and at the door a sister touched her, feelers to feelers, and let her by.

Inside she gave it up to the first one who reached. It went into a cell beside the small ones. She turned round on the comb.

The hard stores had not been there, and then they were. The yellow she had gone out for.

She went out for it again.

:::pov brother-fintan

The carrier came up on the Thursday with a sack of sugar and a drum of lamp oil. There were no crates to go down, since the wax-house has nothing ready so early in the year. She turned the cart in the yard. He carried the sugar in, and when he came out she had not put her boot on the hub. She was standing beside the wheel with her coat unbuttoned at the neck.

"This came up to the landing on Monday, with the rota's post," she said. "It's not a letter. I'd sooner say that before you see it."

She took it from inside her coat. That was where his letter had gone in the autumn, and he had not asked her to do that either time.

It was a slip, a hand long and half as wide, on the stiff grey stock the rota's post uses for its own business. He had seen them go by for years, tied in bundles for the circuit. He had never had one, because he had never sent anything far enough.

"They send one back for anything they've carried past the last landing and put into a hand," the carrier said. "It's how they know they're finished with it."

There were four lines on it. The first was the direction he had written on the outside of the letter, copied small by a clerk. The second was a date in the deep winter. The third was the mark of the post at the far end, a stamp he did not know. On the fourth line, in ink, there was a signature.

It was the name he had written last, at the top of his sheet. It was not in a clerk's hand. He knew whose hand it was.

He read the slip twice, top to bottom, as he would have read any slip. Then he turned it over. The back was blank. He had known that it would be, and had turned it over all the same, and he noted that.

"That's all there was," the carrier said. "I asked at the landing. I don't, as a rule."

"I know what it is."

"I'd not want you thinking I had the rest in the bag."

"No." He held it by its edges. "I've signed a great many of these. Not the post's. The Corps has its own. You come on to a deck, and the officer going off hands you the board, and you sign that you have it. You sign before you've read a line of the log. It doesn't say you've read it, or what you'll think when you do. It says the thing is in your hand and not only in his."

She considered that. She had carried for the hill for most of his time on it, and she had the habit of all carriers, which is to know the weight of a thing without asking what is in it.

"Is it enough?"

"I don't know that it's meant to be. It's a slip." He looked at the date again. "It's a good deal more than I had on Wednesday."

"Right," she said.

She buttoned her coat and put her boot on the hub. The pony leaned, and she took the brake off. The cart was at the first bend before he heard what had been missing. She had not asked whether there was anything else to go. She asked it every time, with the brake already in her hand, and it had never been a question. Today it would have been one. He thought that was why she had left it out, and that it was kindly meant.

He took the slip inside and entered it. In the left column he wrote: *Letter of the late autumn: delivered, deep winter. Signed for, in the addressee's own hand.* In the right: *Opened: not known. Read: not known.*

There was a third line that wanted writing, and it was not a reading. It was what he thought. He had kept that book since he came to the hill, and had not once put what he thought in either column. He saw no cause to begin on the strength of a slip. He left the line empty and closed the book.

At dusk he went down to the stand. He began with the second hive, since in the autumn it had been the first to be told.

"Something's come back up the hill," he said, with his hand flat on the cover. "It's a slip from the post. The letter got where it was going, and it was signed for. It isn't an answer. I don't know that there'll be one, and I'm not owed one. You're to know it."

He told the third, and the first.

The rota had sent nobody yet that year, so the night office was said alone.

He lit the one lamp. The slip lay on the table with nothing on it to hold it down. The psalter fell open where it always fell, at the watchers and the morning. On most nights of the last year he had turned it to something else. Tonight he left it, and read the verse through. *My soul longs for the Lord more than watchmen long for the morning.*

He had always taken that for a verse about longing. He had kept morning watches on three stations and a boundary cutter, and he knew how much a body can want the light, and he had thought that was what the psalmist was measuring against. He saw tonight that he had been reading past the watchman. A man on the middle watch does not long for the morning because the morning will explain anything. Whatever was out in the dark at two is generally still out there at six. He longs for it because at six somebody comes up the ladder and takes the board, and signs.

He was careful with that. A text of that size can be laid over a slip of grey card until the card says more than is written on it, and he had four lines. He did not make the slip the morning. He let the verse tell him what a watch is, which he ought to have known better than most. A watch has an end. The end is not the watchman's to bring, and the matter need not be settled first. He had stood for ten years and more beside a thing as though the standing would be over when the thing had explained itself. No watch he had kept in uniform had ever ended that way.

He prayed for the watchers, as on every night. He added the ones who have been handed a thing they did not ask for, and have signed for it, and have it now in a room he would never see. He asked that they be kept. In the autumn he had asked that whoever opened the letter be kept in the opening, and he asked it again in the same words, since he did not know that it had been opened. He believed that was heard, as he believed it every night. The slip was on the table beside the psalter. He did not join the two.

Before the Amen, the forty seconds. He had kept them every night since he came to the hill, in full silence, for the small steady wrong things that he stood beside and asked nothing of.

The clock at Threshold. It was the record's, still.

The hive. It was three boxes in order, and one of them through a winter on sugar, with a palm of brood in it and nothing signed.

The one in him. He turned to it, as on every night, and it was where the slip said it was. It was a long way off, in another hand, and it had been there since the deep winter. It had been there for weeks before he knew. All that time he had gone on standing over it for forty seconds a night, at a post that had been taken.

Nothing done stops having been done. He had told himself so in the autumn, and it was as true with a signature under it. What had ended was smaller than that, and he was exact about its size. He had made a true reading and put it out, and a hand had marked it received. No instrument gets more than that. It is not told what was made of its reading, and it does not wait to be.

He kept the forty seconds. He kept them for the one who had signed.

The silence ended. He said the Amen into it.

He banked the lamp, and went to sleep like a man who has come off watch.

The carrier came up on the Thursday fortnight with wick, and a note from the circuit about the summer's candles. She turned the cart in the yard, and put her boot on the hub.

"Anything to go?"

It had gone back to not being a question.

"Not this time," said Fintan.

It was the usual answer. He went over it once, where he stood, and it was true.

She took the brake off.
