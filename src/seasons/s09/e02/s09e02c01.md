:::pov noah-adeyemi

The near terraces were three stops out, and Noah had been not asking for nine days.

He was better at it than he had been in the early spring. He no longer brought a can. He came out at the hour with nothing in his hands and crouched at the plant, and the hatch opened, and the Deputy came out and stopped one step further off than there was any reason to stop. They said a few true things to each other across the step. Then the Deputy walked on along the ring and he went in.

It had been like that since the day he asked his mother's question for her. He could have gone on like that until the Corps said otherwise, and that was the trouble with it. He had looked up how long anybody kept three bars.

So on the ninth day he stood up, too fast, and said it.

"Have you seen the terraces?"

"There is a window in the corridor," the Deputy said. "Forty metres spinward of the hatch."

"Not through a window."

"Then no."

"I could show you. The near ones. It's three stops." He heard how small that sounded, three stops, to somebody who had come to Eden from wherever the Corps kept its Academy. "After. When you're off."

The Deputy thought about it. He had learned what that looked like. It was not a person looking for a kind way to say no. It was a person going through a thing clause by clause, and he had found that if he waited, he got all the clauses.

"I have service dress," the Deputy said. "Two sets."

He waited. Nothing came after it.

"All right," he said.

"That is the difficulty."

"That you've got two?"

"That I have nothing else. The kit list is service dress, two sets, a reader, a torch, boots for grating and the posting order." The Deputy said it the way other people said the days of the week. "If I go to the terraces, the tab goes to the terraces. On this side of the ring it is a rank. A rank walking between the beds at ring's evening is the Corps arriving, and nobody there has called it. The Corps does not come uncalled. I was told to write that down."

It would have been easy to laugh, and he knew what a laugh cost at this hatch. He did not think it was a joke. The Deputy had been asked a question and was answering all of it.

"I'm calling it," he said.

"You have no standing to."

"I live here."

"So does everyone on the terraces. They were not asked."

He did laugh then. It was all right. He had got to know the difference between the two laughs since the spring, and so had the Deputy, who stood where the Deputy stood and did not move.

"So it's not no," he said.

"It is not no. It is that I do not know what people wear."

"Which people?"

"People."

He went in, and it stayed with him through supper and after. *What do people wear.* Asked like a line on a form, by somebody who would have filled the form in properly if anybody had ever handed it over.

The shirt was at the back of the press, where it had been for two years. It had been cut for the reach he was expected to have, and he had stopped growing a hand short of it, and it had been folded away as a thing that was always going to be somebody's. It was plain undyed cloth. It had a collar with nothing on it.

The next day he came out at the hour with something in his hands.

He had thought about that too. A full can had been a lie, and empty hands had been the truth, and he did not know what a folded shirt was. He crouched at the plant with it on his knee. He found out when the hatch opened, because the Deputy's eyes went to his hands first, as they always did, and this time they stayed there.

"It's too long for me in the arm," he said. "It isn't anybody's. It's been in a cupboard."

He held it out by one edge, across the step. The Deputy took it by the other. It was the first thing that had ever gone from one of them to the other that was not a sentence.

"I will need a quarter of an hour. The room the Corps keeps is further along the ring."

"I'll be here."

"I know," the Deputy said, and it came out almost as a laugh, and went off along the ring carrying the shirt flat on both hands, like a document.

::::: scene 2

:::pov iklix-varn

The service dress went on the hook, and the tab went with it. Three bars, seated at the collar by a quartermaster and not to be moved by anyone else. Varn did not move it. It stayed where it was, and Varn moved.

The shirt was long in the arm, which was right. A hand went up to the collar to check a thing that was not there, and found cloth.

The trousers were the Corps', and the boots were for grating, the list having no others. Varn considered whether a person in half of a uniform was the Corps half arriving. A rank is worn at the collar. The legs, Varn concluded, were only legs. It was not a finding.

Then back along the ring, the way a Deputy came at the hour.

The corridor did not look. It never had. But a man with a crate said "Evening" as he went by, without lifting his head, and Varn was four doors further on before working out that in two months nobody in that corridor had said it to the tab.

Noah was at the plant. He stood up, and Varn watched him look at the collar, as he always looked, and find nothing there to read.

He had always had the word from there. Varn had not understood until now that it was where he got it.

"I don't know what to call you," he said. "Without it."

Varn had not considered this. The posting order said *Deputy Officer Varn*. The log said it. Smith said *Varn*, as a fact. The other name had not been said aloud on Eden. It had last been said by somebody reading it off a list.

"Iklix."

He said it. He got it right the first time, which not everybody did, and Varn supposed that he had had it since he looked up the tab, and had been carrying it about since the early spring without once using it.

"Noah," Varn said, because it seemed owed.

At the platform the gate took an ordinary fare. It did not ask whose. Varn stood at it a moment longer than a fare needs, and somebody behind breathed out, politely, and Varn went through.

Three stops.

The Academy teaches the inside of a ring as a figure: the radius, the rate, what a level does when the floor is a curve. It had not taught that a terrace on such a floor is a staircase with no top. The beds went up ahead in steps, each a hand higher than the last, and they went up behind as well, so that wherever Varn stood was the lowest place in sight. Canes in rows. Wide leaves holding the last of the light. Water came down between the beds in a cut channel, loud for its size.

And the air. Varn had been two months in a corridor, a compartment lit from nowhere and a room with a hook. This air had been through something green on its way. Wet soil. A leaf somebody had crushed, walking. Something sweet that was nearly over.

There were people on the terraces. A woman tying canes. Two children carrying a full can between them and losing a little at every step. None of them looked, and it was not the corridor's way of not looking. Here there was nothing to look at. A person in a shirt that was long in the arm.

"Is it what people wear?" Varn said.

Noah looked the shirt over, seriously, collar to cuff. "Nobody's looked at you since the platform."

"No."

"Then that's what people wear."

The paths between the beds were one person wide. He went first, since he knew the way, and Varn followed, and they were a good way up before Varn noticed that the step had gone.

It had not moved. There was nowhere to put it. Beside him there was a bed of beans on one hand and a bed of something low and grey-leaved on the other, and so Varn was behind him, at a pace, which is where anybody walks who is being shown the way. A path one person wide reports nothing about the two people on it. Varn found that restful. A little further up it was not entirely restful, and Varn did not log either.

He talked. In the corridor he said one true thing and stopped. Here he pointed, and named what grew, and said whose it was, by the people and not by the doors: the school's, his mother's cousin's, a man who grew far more squash than he could eat and left it on the wall for whoever came. He did not say where his own shift was, on the Compact side, or what it was for. He never had. A Deputy notices what has not been said. For the first time Varn found that a thing noticed could simply be left with the person it belonged to.

He asked nothing about the hatch. He had asked twice, in the early spring, and been answered.

Where the near terraces ended there was a low wall, and the channel came out from under it, fast and clear. He crouched and put both hands in, the way he crouched at the plant, and looked up.

"It comes round cold," he said. "Go on."

Varn crouched. The water was cold enough to feel in the wrists. It pulled a little at the fingers, going by. Since the dock Varn had put a hand to a handle, a pen, a rail and a shirt. This was the first thing on Eden that had touched back.

"That's as far as I come, most evenings," Noah said. He was looking along the rise, where the beds went on climbing into the dimming light and windows were beginning to come on.

"And past it?"

"More of it. It's a ring."

Varn looked at that for a while, hands in the water. It could be supported.

They went back at ring's evening. The train asked more of the knees going home than it had coming out. Varn noted it, and did not know yet that the ring had a name for that.

At the door with the plant he stopped, and Varn stopped.

"Iklix," he said, trying it at his own door.

"Noah."

He went in. Varn walked on to the room the Corps kept. There was one hook. Varn hung the shirt over the service dress, and looked at that, and took both down and hung them the other way about, so that the tab was on the outside. It would be wanted first.

:::pov zoe-smith

"Varn," she said, as a fact, and the Deputy came to the desk and stood where a person stands.

She had heard most of it through the latch, over two days. A question about terraces. A kit list, recited. And then a sentence of her own coming back down the corridor from the wrong side of the hatch: *the Corps does not come uncalled.* She had said that on the twenty-fourth day, over a letter from the Starwarden, about engineers and a dock ring, and had told the Deputy to write it down. The Deputy had written it down. The Deputy had then applied it to a bed of beans.

It was not what the sentence was for. She had gone looking, on and off through one shift, for the place where the Deputy had it wrong, and she had not found the place.

She wrote none of it down. It had happened in a corridor, and after that somewhere she had no need to know about.

What was in the compartment was on the deck. Where the Deputy's boots had been, at the start of the shift, there was a little dark soil, shaken out of soles that were ridged for grating. Smith knew the grating on this ring and it did not carry soil. She knew one place on the near arc that did.

At the end of the shift she turned the log toward the Deputy and said, "Enter it."

Varn wrote the instruments. The hatch, opened once and closed once. The officers, named. No event. Then the pen stopped, and the Deputy looked at the deck, and for the length of one agreement between the instruments she watched somebody decide whether a thing belonged to the watch.

It had happened in the compartment. Somebody who was not here could not check it tomorrow unless it was written today.

Varn wrote: *Deck: soil, a few grams, from the Deputy's boots. Removed.* Then the Deputy removed it, with the edge of the posting order, which she had not said to do.

She read the entry as though it had been longer, and initialled it. In a column of shifts going back years it was the only line with a terrace in it, and it did not say so.

At the hour the Deputy went out. Through the latch she heard a person stand up, not too fast.

"Varn," he said.

"Noah."

There was a step in it. She could hear that there was. It did not sound like the one from the early spring. It sounded like where the Corps stands, and like two people who both knew that was all it was.

The lights held. She closed the log.
