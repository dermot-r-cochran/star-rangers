:::pov zoe-smith

The plant was watered on the sixth day of every week, in the morning, and had been for longer than Smith had stood the watch.

She knew it the way she knew most of the ring, by small things and at a distance. Dark soil in the pot as she came along to the hatch on a sixth day. Pale soil by the fourth. In all her tenure the woman who lived behind that door had not missed one, and had not once done it at any hour but the morning, and the two of them had never exchanged a sentence. They nodded. The woman nodded to the service dress, politely, as the Compact side nods to it, and Smith nodded back, and that had been enough for years.

On this sixth day the soil was dark when Smith came in. So it had been done.

At the hour, with the log initialled and the Deputy's hand not yet on the hatch, she heard a can set down on the corridor floor. A full one. A full can and an empty one do not make the same sound on a deck, and she had heard this sound before, in the early spring, two days running.

She had watched a son arrange to be at that plant when the hatch opened. She had read the signal without difficulty, as the oldest one on any ring. She was now, it appeared, going to see where he had the method from.

This was not the oldest signal. It was the second oldest, and it did not crouch. Through the gap the latch left she could see a pair of hands held loosely together and a can standing beside a pair of flat shoes, and nobody was pretending to look at any leaves.

It was not hers to say. It had not been hers in the spring either. She stayed at the desk.

"Varn," she said, as a fact, since the Deputy had stopped with one hand on the hatch and appeared to need one.

The Deputy used the handle.

:::pov iklix-varn

The soil was dark. It was the sixth day, and it had been watered that morning. Varn had noted it going in.

There was a can on the floor beside the pot, and it was full, and the woman standing over it had not come out to water anything.

Varn read the corridor in the order the log had taught. What was there: a can, a person. What was not: Noah, who at this hour was always here. What had changed since the last entry: the person was looking straight at the tab, which nobody on this side of the ring did, and had her son's face. It was the same face with the uncertainty taken out of it. His always looked as though it had been about to say something else. Hers had decided some time ago what it was going to say.

"Deputy," she said.

It was the Compact-side word. It was said politely, to a uniform.

"Deputy Officer Varn."

"I know. He looked it up. Then I looked at what he'd looked up." She glanced down at the can and back. "It doesn't need watering. I know that."

It was too late to stop it. Varn had the sentence already, in another voice, from the eighth day, and something got as far as the mouth before it could be held.

She saw that. She did not seem to miss much. "He's said that one to you."

"He said it first."

"He had it from somewhere."

She looked the Deputy over, then, and took her time, and it was not the way the Academy's boards looked. It went to the collar and did not stay there. It went to the boots, and stayed there rather longer. Then to the wrists, where the service dress ended, as though she were measuring something she already had the figure for.

"Does it fit in the arm?"

Varn went through the possible meanings of that and arrived at the only one. "The shirt."

"I cut it when he was fifteen, for the reach I thought was coming, and it never came. It's sat in the press two years." She did not ask whether Varn had known she made it, and Varn had not. "Does it fit?"

"It fits in the arm."

"Good." She seemed to file that. "You took it up the terraces in those boots."

"The list has no others."

"No. I don't suppose it has." She folded her hands again. "You stand a long way off."

There it was. Varn had stood where Varn stood, one step further from her than there was any reason to stand, and she had named it inside two minutes. Her son had not named it in a whole spring. He had heard it once, in the early spring, and paid for it.

She was owed all of it.

"It is where the Corps stands," Varn said. "The tab is a rank on this side of the ring. This is the distance it keeps from a resident's door. It is not a distance from anybody in particular."

"And without the tab?"

"Nearer."

"How much nearer?"

Varn said what could be supported. "The paths on the near terraces are one person wide. It has not been tried on anything wider."

She laughed. It was a short laugh, and there was nothing in it that could have been written down, and Varn knew it. It had been hers before it was his. Varn did not step back. Varn noted that there had been no need to decide.

Then she stopped, and when she spoke again it was lower, though there was nobody in the corridor.

"I've lived behind that door since before he was born. They come along the ring with a bag. A while after, they go back along it with the same bag, and there's a different walk at the hour, and nobody tells us, because why would they." She did not turn her head toward the hatch. She had stood a pace from it this whole time and had not once looked at it. Varn took that for what it had always seemed to be. "How long do they leave you?"

"The Corps has not said."

"No."

"Nobody stays a Deputy on a first posting." It was not a comfort, and it was not meant as one. It was what there was.

"He knows that. He'll have known it before he ever picked up that can." She bent, and took the can by its handle, full as it was. "I'm not asking you for anything. I've no standing to; I'm told that's how it's put. I wanted it said by somebody, out loud, in this corridor. I know. He knows. And you know."

"It has been said," Varn said.

She nodded. It was not the nod she gave to service dress, and Varn could not have said, for a log, where the difference lay.

"Sixth day, mornings. That's me."

"I know. The soil is dark."

That stopped her, with the door half open and the can dragging at her arm. "You read the soil?"

"It is on the way to the hatch."

"Twenty years and more I've watered that," she said, "and I never once thought it was being read." She looked at the pot as though it had been keeping something from her. Then she looked at Varn, and said it properly, with all of it in, as someone says a thing she has looked up and means to get right.

"Deputy Officer Varn."

She went in. The door closed. Varn stood in the corridor for the length of one agreement between the instruments, and then walked on along the ring.

There was nothing to enter. The corridor was not the watch's. A resident had asked about an arm.

:::pov noah-adeyemi

His shift had run long. He worked out afterwards that she had known it would.

The can was standing just inside the door, and it was full.

His mother did not leave a can full. Nothing in that house stood about half used. He looked at it, and went back out and looked at the pot, where the soil was as dark as it was on any sixth day, and came in again and found her at the table with a length of cloth and her mouth full of pins.

"You took a can."

She took the pins out one at a time. "I did."

"To a wet plant."

"I'm told it runs in the family."

He sat down. There did not seem to be anything else to do.

"What did you say?"

"I said *Deputy*. And at the end I said the whole of it, rank and all. That one answers to the whole of it." She smoothed the cloth with the flat of her hand. "I was told the arm fits."

He had not told her where the shirt had gone. He had not needed to; she had made it, and she knew the inside of that press better than he did.

"And it stands a long way off," she said. "Your hatch."

"That isn't—" He had the whole defence ready, and had had it since the early spring: that it was not unfriendliness, that he had thought so once and been wrong.

"I asked. It's where the Corps stands. I was told so to my face, by somebody who wasn't sorry about it and wasn't proud of it either. It isn't a distance from anybody in particular." She was looking at the cloth and not at him, which was a kindness. "Out of the uniform it's nearer, I'm told. A path's width. It hasn't been tried on anything wider."

He went red to the collar. She let him.

It was a strange thing to be handed across his own table. He had minded the step, in the spring, and then he had stopped minding it, and since the terraces he had not known what he thought about it. Here it was, said flat, in words that could only have come from one person on the ring. *It is where the Corps stands.* So the step was the tab's. It had a rule to it, and anybody could have looked the rule up.

The other distance had no rule to it at all. That one was Iklix's own.

He found, sitting there, that he could like a step like that. He thought he possibly already did.

"They don't leave them long," his mother said.

"I know."

"I know you know. I said so." She put a pin in. "I said it out loud, the once, so it's been said. You'll not hear it from me again."

She did not tell him to be careful. He waited for it through three more pins, and it did not come, and he understood that this had been her whole errand: a full can, a wet plant, and a thing said once in a corridor to the only other person it concerned.

"It's a good arm," she said, after a while. "I'd cut another, if I had the length."

The can stood inside the door all week. On the next sixth day she carried it out in the morning, as she always had, and used it. Nothing was wasted in that house, a point once made included.
