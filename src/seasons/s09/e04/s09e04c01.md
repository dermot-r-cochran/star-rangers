:::pov iklix-varn

The order was on the desk, and the officer was not.

It was one leaf, printed, because an order on a reader is an order until the reader fails. *Field Officer Smith absent, one shift. Watch to be stood by Field Officer Ruiz, relieving, Passage oversight.* It did not say where she was. A Deputy does not ask why an officer is away. A Deputy notices that the order did not say.

At the foot of it there were two lines in a hand Varn knew, the one the boards trusted. *Instruments as logged. Deputy Officer Varn knows the watch.*

Varn read that standing up, twice, which a Deputy is allowed.

It was not praise. She did not write praise. It was a thing a relieving officer would need, set down in the fewest words that could be checked: there are six instruments, and there is a Deputy, and here is the condition of each. Varn noted that the Deputy had the longer line.

The compartment was the compartment. The six lights were the colour they were. Nobody said *Varn* as a fact, and Varn found out by its absence how much of the room that had been.

A Deputy cannot stand a watch alone. The watch is the nearest qualified officer to the event if the event comes, and three bars are not that; so for the length of time between the hatch closing behind Varn and the hatch opening again, the posting consisted of six instruments and somebody who knew them. It was eleven minutes. Varn stood them at the desk, where a person stands, and wrote nothing, there being nobody to turn the log.

The hatch opened without a knock, which was right, and the opening was full of Field Officer.

"Varn! Ruiz." Four bars, a broad face, a kit bag that had been to more places than Varn's. He put out a hand.

There was nothing in the doctrine about hands. Varn had been told so by the way one was not offered on the first day. There was also, it now appeared, nothing in the doctrine against them. Varn took it. It was warm, and it let go at the right time.

Then he turned and shut the hatch. To the seal.

The corridor went out like a light.

Varn had not known it was in the compartment. It had come in under everything, through a hatch left on the latch: a door, a child on something with wheels, the trolley that had started this summer to complain about one of its own, somebody's voice saying a name three doors down. It had been there since the first day. Now there was the hum of the rack and a man setting his bag on the deck.

"She leaves that open?" Ruiz said, seeing the Deputy look. "Does she. Well, the book doesn't say either way. I like a door shut. You know where you are." He pulled the chair out and sat in it, and nodded at the other. "Sit down, Deputy. It's a shift, not a parade."

Varn sat.

It was a while before Varn noticed where. The second chair had been against the wall, and Varn had brought it to the desk, and had set it down, without deciding to, one step further from the desk than there was any reason to set it. It had felt like the right place. It was a report, then, and an honest one, and the Academy would have had it corrected. Varn left the chair where it was.

He was good company. That was the difficulty of the shift, and Varn was most of the way through it before finding the words. He asked where a Deputy slept on this ring and whether the room had a window. He knew the six instruments and said what each one was for, which Varn knew, and then what would happen in what order if they ever stopped agreeing, which Varn also knew, and he said it well. He told a story about his own first posting that had a cargo lift in it and ended.

"Best first posting in the Corps, this," he said. "Do you know why? Because nothing happens. Day after day of it, and you learn to write that down properly. That's what the watch is for. It makes officers who can write down nothing."

Varn looked at the six lights.

It was possibly true. It was a thing Smith had spent a spring and half a summer not saying. She had said, on the first day, that the watch does not watch anything, and had let that sit until the thirtieth before saying it was also advice, and Varn had carried it about in the meantime and found a use for it in a corridor. Said out loud, across a desk, in one go, it was about the size of a kit list.

He read back through the log, since a relieving officer should, and he read it as a man reads something he is enjoying.

"Good hand. *Struck, one line.* Ha." He turned a leaf. "*Field Officer Smith related the account of Aoife of Cill Aoife to the Deputy, from memory. Fourteen minutes.* She tells you stories?"

"One. On the seventh day."

"What's it about?"

Varn said what could be supported. "A girl at a well, on Earth, a long time ago. Something came to the well. She said what she saw. It is in the archive."

"And?"

"It does not end."

He laughed, not unkindly. "That sounds like Smith." And he turned the leaf.

Varn sat very still on a chair one step too far from the desk.

He did not have it. He had four bars, and a first posting behind him with a Field Officer in it, and nobody had ever put this across a desk to him. He did not seem to be short of anything. Varn had supposed, without once looking at the supposition, that it came with the rank: that somewhere above three bars everybody was carrying a well. It did not come with the rank. It came down one line of desks, and a compartment on a station that no longer stood was on that line, and so was this one.

It would have taken fourteen minutes. The hatch was shut and the shift was long. Varn found the story sitting ready, entire, in the order she had told it, and found also that it would not be handed over. It was not a secret. It was not Varn's.

"*Deck: soil*," Ruiz read. "*From the Deputy's boots.* You logged your own boots."

"It happened in the compartment."

"So it did." He was delighted. "Four bars inside two years, you. I can always tell."

That could not be checked. Varn noted that this was what was wrong with it, and also why it was pleasant.

At the end of the shift he did not turn the log. He pushed it across. "You write it. She'll want it in a hand she knows."

Varn wrote the instruments. Then the hatch, and the pen stopped.

On every other shift since the early spring the hatch had stood on the latch, and Varn had never entered that, because it had never been otherwise. Today it had been otherwise. Somebody who was not here could not check it tomorrow unless it was written today.

*Hatch opened once, by Field Officer Ruiz, and closed to the seal for the length of the shift.* The officers, named: Field Officer Ruiz, relieving; Deputy Officer Varn. No event.

Ruiz read it as though it were exactly the length it was, and initialled it, large.

"She won't tell you this," he said, getting up, "so I will. That's a good log. You're doing well here."

"Thank you, Field Officer."

It was kindly meant. Varn turned it over on the way to the hatch and could not find anywhere to put it down. There were two accounts of the Deputy in the compartment now. One said the Deputy was doing well. The other said the Deputy knew the watch. Only one of them would bear weight, and it was the one that had not been meant as a kindness.

:::pov noah-adeyemi

The hatch was shut.

He knew before he was near it. He could not have said how. He had never in his life looked at the thing, not really, except on one afternoon in the early spring, but it turned out that he had always known how it sat at this hour, the way he knew which of the corridor's lights was the one that flickered. At the hour it sat a finger's width off its frame. Today it was flat to the wall and there was no line of light round it.

He crouched at the plant with nothing in his hands.

*They go back along it with the same bag,* his mother had said, *and there's a different walk at the hour, and nobody tells us.* She had said it once, to somebody else, and it had come home to him by way of a kitchen table.

The handle turned.

It was four bars, and it was not hers. A big man with a kit bag, who did the thing nobody in the corridor did, and looked straight at him, and smiled.

"Evening! That's a healthy plant. What is it?"

"It's off the terraces," Noah said. His mouth had gone dry.

"Is it? Good for it." And he went off down the middle of the corridor toward the spoke cars, with a walk that took up all of it.

A different walk at the hour.

Noah stood up. He did it too fast, and there was nobody to see. He looked at the hatch, flat to its wall, with a number on it and nothing else. He counted. He got to thirty, which was a long time to stand in a corridor finding out something about yourself that you had thought you already knew.

He had thought he had it ready. He had looked it up in the spring. He had been told it across his own table. He had it ready the way you have a fare ready, and it turned out not to be the fare.

The handle turned again.

Three bars. Grey-brown. A step, the usual one, and the eyes going to his hands and then, quickly, to his face, and staying there.

"Varn," he said. It did not come out level.

"Noah."

"The hatch was shut."

It was all he could manage. It was enough, for somebody who read soil.

"Field Officer Smith is away. One shift. The Corps sent a relieving officer from the Passage oversight, and he prefers a hatch shut." And then, when that had not done it, and plainly, in the voice that said what could be supported: "It is on a printed order. She stands tomorrow's. If I had been moved there would have been an order for that too, and I would have read it before he did."

He breathed. He had not been, particularly, until then.

"I thought—"

"Yes."

Neither of them finished it. The Deputy stood where the Corps stands, and did not come nearer, and he was glad of that, a little, since it gave him the width of a step to put his face in order.

"He asked me what the plant was," he said.

"He shook my hand."

He laughed. It was not much of one. "What did you do?"

"There is nothing in the doctrine against it."

Down the corridor the trolley went by with its one bad wheel, and a door opened and shut. He put his hands in his pockets, having nothing to hold.

"Three stops?" he said.

"I will need a quarter of an hour."

"I'll be here."

"I know," the Deputy said.

:::pov zoe-smith

She had been away one shift, on a matter that was the Corps' and that her file would carry in a line.

The order was where she had left it. Under her two lines there were now three of Ruiz's, in a hand that took up the room it wanted. *Stood. Nothing. Good Deputy. Sits a mile off the desk, though; you might mention it.*

She read the log. The instruments. The officers, with a name she had not initialled beside before. And between them a clause that no entry in that column had carried: *closed to the seal for the length of the shift.*

She looked at the hatch, which was on the latch. The trolley was going by.

In all the shifts since the early spring the Deputy had never once entered that it stood so, and had been right. A log records what happened. It had taken another officer's habit to turn hers into something that happens. She had never given anybody the reason for the latch, and she saw no need to start by putting it in a log. She left it out, where it had always been.

"Varn," she said, as a fact.

The Deputy came to the desk and stood where a person stands.

So it had gone out for a shift, the step, and come back in. She had told fourteen Deputies nothing about it and one Deputy one thing, on the grounds that it was not a mistake but a report. She saw no reason to change the grounds. A report has a date on it, and it is about whoever was standing in front of it at the time.

She filed the order, with Ruiz's lines on it, under what they were evidence of.

She did not mention it.
