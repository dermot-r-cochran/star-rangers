:::pov zoe-smith

Two of the fourteen had left the Corps, on good terms, and both still wrote.

One wrote when there was news. The other wrote once a year, at the turn of it, whether there was news or not, and what he wrote about was weather. He had gone to live somewhere that had it. Rain on a named day. A wind that had taken the roof off a shed and set it down whole in the next field. He wrote it the way she had taught him to write a shift: what happened, in a form somebody could check. She had no means of checking a wind. She believed all of it.

She answered each letter with what she could support. That made short letters. *The watch is on Eden now. The instruments are six. The event has not come.*

This year a second letter came in the late summer, out of its season. It said he would be on Eden's ring for six hours on a day he named, between a spoke car and a ship. It asked whether a person with no bars might come to the hatch.

She wrote back the hatch number.

He had been her ninth. Of all of them he was the one she had been least able to assess, because an assessment records what the Deputy did, and what Okonjo did, for the length of a first posting, was stand the watch correctly and ask nothing. Not on the fourth day and not on the twentieth. He was not sullen and he was not slow. He had seemed to think that asking was the wrong answer. She had told him the account of the girl at the well on his last shift, since it was not a reward, and he had said, "Thank you, Field Officer," and gone to catch a spoke car.

He had made four bars. Then, some years on, he had written to say he was leaving, and why, in two lines, and she had never had the two lines expanded.

She had told the account fifteen times and had been waiting, each time, to be told by the Deputy what it was for. She had said so aloud once, in the spring, to the fifteenth. Fourteen of them were out in the Corps and beyond it with the thing in their kit, and not one had ever sent anything back.

She did not expect anything of a visit. She noted that she knew, without looking at the letter, which day it was.

"There will be a visitor on the shift," she told the Deputy. "He will be entered."

:::pov iklix-varn

He did not knock, and he did not use the handle.

The hatch was on the latch. It moved in a hand's width, under two fingers, and stopped there, and a voice outside it said, "Field Officer." It was not a question.

"Okonjo," Smith said, as a fact.

Varn had not known there was a third way to come through that hatch. A knock was for someone who did not know what it was. The handle was for the watch. This was somebody who knew exactly what it was and held that it was no longer his.

He was older than Varn by a posting's worth of years, perhaps two postings'. No collar to speak of. Hands that had been out in something. He came in and looked at the rack before he looked at either of them, all six lights, left to right, and Varn saw him finish counting and stand easier. Then he crossed to the desk and stopped.

He stopped where a person stands. A moment later he looked down at his own feet, and something went over his face that was nearly a smile, and he did not move them.

"Fifteenth," he said, to the tab.

"Deputy Officer Varn."

"Okonjo. I was the—"

"Ninth," Smith said.

"Ninth." He looked round the compartment, at a rack that was not the one he had stood under and a desk that might as well have been. "It's the same room."

"It is a different room," Smith said. "It is the same watch."

"Yes. Sorry." He seemed pleased to have been corrected. He turned back to Varn. "Has she told you the well?"

"On the seventh day."

"You asked for it."

"I asked why the log could not say that nothing had happened."

He laughed, short. "I never asked her anything. A whole posting. I thought a Deputy who had to ask hadn't been listening. She gave it to me on my last shift, standing about where you are, and I thanked her and left." He glanced at the log, closed on the desk. "Did you enter it?"

"Fourteen minutes."

"I didn't. I've thought about that since. I'd have liked it to be somewhere I could go and look."

"A log that leaves out a story is not wrong," Smith said. "It is only shorter."

"I know. I've had twelve years to work out that you'd say that."

Varn stood the shift. That was the Deputy's part, and it was a strange one: six lights holding, and across the desk two people who had once stood exactly as Varn and Smith stood now, talking in sentences with most of the words left out. A wind. A shed. Whether the roof had gone back on. Smith asked what could be checked and he told her, and Varn understood that this was a conversation that had been going on by letter for years, at the rate of one exchange a year, and was being allowed to run for an afternoon.

Then he said, "I told it to my daughter."

Smith's hands were loosely together. They did not move.

"In the spring. She's seven. She couldn't sleep and she wanted something long." He was not apologising. He was making a report. "I told it the way you did. I didn't dress it up and I didn't put an end on it. The well, the sixteen years, the night, the cloth. That it was the saint and looked nothing like her. Shown and not healed."

Varn looked at the Field Officer, and looked away again, since a Deputy does not watch an officer's face for a ruling. But Varn wanted the ruling. The account was passed officer to officer, across a desk. Varn had sat a whole shift in the summer with it ready and had not given it to a man with four bars. This one had carried it out of the Corps altogether and told it at a kitchen table, to a child.

"It is in an archive that anyone may ask for," Smith said.

It was not a ruling. It was what could be supported. Varn noted that he had waited twelve years, and worn four bars for some of them, and that nothing she had said showed which of those had made it his to give. Perhaps neither had. Perhaps it had been his since the last shift, and he had simply taken that long to find somebody he wanted to have it.

"She asked me something," Okonjo said. "I didn't have it. I've been carrying it since the spring, and there's one desk I know of."

"Ask it."

"She wanted to know if Aoife sat up again. The next year. The same night." He said it carefully, in a child's order. "The vigil was her habit, the account says. Then one year the thing comes. So the year after, when that night came round again. Did she go and sit by the well?"

Nobody said anything for the length of one agreement between the instruments.

Varn went through the account as it had been told across this desk. A thorn well. A girl of fourteen. Sixteen years. The vigil, alone. The air folding. The warm water, three days. *Shown, not healed.* And then nothing. It stopped at the scribe. Varn had asked what it was, and what the Corps said it was, and whether she was chosen, and what she had been shown. It had never occurred to Varn that there had been a following spring.

"The account does not say," Smith said.

"No. That's what I told her."

"It says the vigil was her habit before. It says she tended the well until she died, and gives the year, and that is twenty-five years on. It says the well never ran warm again." She was speaking a little more slowly than she did. "It does not say whether she kept the eve. I have had it since before you were born. I have told it fifteen times. I have never asked that."

"Nor me," he said. "It took somebody who was meant to be asleep."

"What did she say? When you told her it does not say."

He smiled properly, for the first time. "She said, 'I would have.' Then she went to sleep."

Smith said nothing at all.

He stayed a little longer. He did not sit, and was not asked to. At the hatch he turned, with two fingers on the edge of it as he had come.

"I'm not going to ask you what it's for," he said.

"No."

"I worked out some time ago that you'd have said."

Varn kept very still. On the thirtieth day, at that desk, she had said: *I do not know. I was never told.* She had said it because she had been asked, and in the right order. Now a man who had never asked her anything stood in the hatchway and told her he had noticed the size of the silence, and what it probably meant, and that he would leave it where it was.

She did not say he was right. He had not asked.

"Write at the turn of the year," she said.

"I always do."

The hatch came back to the latch behind him. He had not once touched the handle.

At the end of the shift she turned the log toward the Deputy and said, "Enter it."

Varn wrote the instruments. *Hatch opened twice: by the Deputy, and for a visitor.* The officers, named. *Visited by Ferran Okonjo, formerly Field Officer, at the Field Officer's word. Fifty-five minutes.* No event.

The pen stopped over the page, and went no further. A question had been asked in the compartment, and it had been a child's, and it was on its way back to her by ship.

Smith read the entry as though it had been longer, and initialled it.

::::: scene 2

:::pov zoe-smith

The Deputy went at the hour. She did not.

She had been waiting to be told what it was for. Fifteen tellings. She had thought a year that it was about the well, and a year that it was about what came, and then she had stopped thinking about what it was about and stood watches. In all of that she had looked at one night. The girl had lived twenty-five years after it. She had never looked at one of them.

What had come through the hatch was not an answer. It was a question, third hand, from somebody of seven who had never seen a desk.

*Did she go and sit by the well?*

She was aware that it had landed somewhere, and she was an officer who noticed small things before she could have said what they were. She did not try to say what this one was. It was not an entry. It was not an assessment either.

The archive would send the account to anyone who asked for it. She asked. She had not, since her second year, when she had found that her own Field Officer told it with the well warm for four days and that the page said three.

She read it standing, all of it, to the scribe's last line and the annotation after.

It did not say.

She had not thought it would. It was a different thing to have checked. She wrote to him that night, on one leaf: *I have read it again. It gives the habit, the night, the three days and the year of her death. It does not say whether she kept the eve.* It was the shortest letter she had ever sent him, and it took the longest.

She did not add what a child of seven had said. That was already his.

The six lights were the colour they were. She had stood under lights like them, on this ring and others, for longer than the man who had just left had been alive, and she had told fifteen Deputies on their first day that the event had not come in her tenure.

She closed the log, and came back the next day, and stood the shift.
