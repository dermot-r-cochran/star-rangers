:::pov iklix-varn

In the autumn, in the middle of a shift, one of the six lights changed colour.

Varn was at the desk with the rack behind and to one side, and did not see it. Varn knew it. It was the way a person knows a sound has stopped. Something that had been the same since the early spring was not the same, and the back of the neck had the news before the eyes did.

Varn turned round.

The fourth from the left. Five lights were the colour they were. One was not.

Smith had not turned. She had looked at the clock on the desk, once, and put the pen down, which she did when a shift was over, and it was not over. Then she stood with her hands loosely together, facing the rack, and did nothing else whatever.

*The event has not come in my tenure.* She had said it on the first day, flatly, as a fact about the posting. Varn had not until now heard that it was a sentence with a date missing from it.

The Academy has a great deal to say about the event. Varn found that all of it was present, in order, and that none of it was any use for the next minute and a half, because every line of it began at the next agreement. Six instruments file every ninety seconds. Until they filed again, there was nothing to act on. There was a compartment lit evenly from nowhere in particular, and a light, and an interval.

So Varn stood it.

It was very long. The hum of the rack was at the pitch it was always at. Varn's wrists were cold. Somewhere in the first third of it Varn's weight went onto the back foot, the old way, toward where a Deputy used to stand, and Varn felt it go, and brought it forward again. The honest step was not honest any more. Varn knew where a person stood in this room.

The hatch was on the latch. The corridor came in under everything, as it did. A door. The trolley, with its wheel. Two voices, a long way down, and one of them laughed. None of them knew. They were going about an afternoon on the far side of a plain flush panel the colour of the wall, with a door on either side of it, and at one of those doors a plant that had been watered on the sixth day.

*The nearest qualified officer to the event if the event comes.* Varn had carried that sentence across the ring four times in the spring and believed it understood. It had been understood as a figure. A distance, from an officer to an instrument.

Nearest had another side to it. Varn had not looked at that side before. It was the side with the doors on it.

Past the middle of the interval Varn thought of the mount. It was not possible not to. Approach mount three, the bracket at its root replaced in the summer, the reference agreeing, a line in a log that said *whether it still rings, not known.* All six instruments hung from that mount.

One light had changed.

That could be supported. Varn took hold of it, and it was not a comfort. It did not say what this was. It said one place where this was not, and left everywhere else.

Varn did not look at the Field Officer for what to do. There had been one look, at the start. She was standing. That was the instruction.

The six filed.

The fourth light was the colour it was.

Nothing else in the compartment changed. The watch had known before anyone could have read anything, which was what the first day had promised, and the knowing had amounted to two people on their feet.

Smith picked up the pen.

"Stand your shift," she said.

"Was that it?" Varn heard how young that sounded, and let it stand, since it was the question.

"No."

"How do you know?"

"I do not know what it was." She said it as she said the posting. "I know what I am required to do if a light does not come back. I was not required to do it. That is all I know about the last interval, and it is all you know. You may read the agreement log. Then there is a form."

The agreement log was the one thing on the watch a Deputy could read without being told to. Varn read it standing. For one interval it said that five had agreed and the fourth had not, and gave no class, and did not use the word *event*. For the next it said six.

The form was the watch's, and it had four lines and a fifth. The time, to the second, which was the instruments' and not the Deputy's. The duration: one interval. The instrument. The agreement of the rest.

The fifth line was *remarks*.

Varn had remarks. There was a mount, and a summer, and a note in a habitat engineer's hand. There was the fact that it was the fourth and not another. All of it was a Deputy's opinion about a structure a Deputy could not hear. It would have gone up to the Passage oversight as the watch's opinion, and the watch had none.

Varn left the line empty. It cost something. Varn had rather expected it to.

Smith read the four lines and initialled them, and the notice went where notices went. What the oversight did with it was the oversight's.

At the end of the shift she turned the log toward the Deputy and said, "Enter it."

It took longer than usual and every clause of it was true. *Six instruments agreed at each interval but one. At that interval the fourth was out of agreement with five, and its light changed colour; at the next, six agreed and the light was as before. Notice sent to the Passage oversight.* The hatch, opened once and closed once. The officers, named.

The pen stopped over the last two words.

Varn had written them at the foot of every entry since the first day, when they had been dictated. Today they looked like an opinion. Then Varn looked again at the agreement log. The word was the instruments'. They had a place to file it and they had not filed it.

*No event.*

Smith read the entry, and for once it was as long as she read it. She initialled it.

:::pov zoe-smith

She had seen a light change four times before, on three racks. Each time it had come back at the next interval.

That had told her nothing about the fifth. She had known it would tell her nothing, standing there, and had gone looking for it anyway in about the tenth second. Four lights that came back are not a rule about lights. They are four entries. She stood the fifth as she had stood the first, on a station that no longer stood, with a Field Officer beside her who had not said a word either.

The hatch was on the latch. She had a reason for that and had given it to nobody. For one interval she listened to what the watch was nearest to: a door, a wheel, somebody laughing. She did not close it.

She had not watched the Deputy. She had been aware of the Deputy, as she was aware of the rack. One look, in the first second. Then nothing. Somewhere in the middle the weight had gone back toward the old place, a step from the desk, and had come forward again. Nobody had told it to. Then a stillness that was not a frozen one.

The next form the Corps sent would ask what the Deputy had done on the watch. She would write that on a shift in the autumn the Deputy stood one interval. The boards would know what that meant or they would not. She had never told them what anything meant either.

*The event has not come in my tenure.* It was still a fact. She would say it to the sixteenth, on the first day, in the same voice.

For ninety seconds it had been a fact she could not have supported. She noted that, with the rest.

:::pov noah-adeyemi

The hatch sat a finger's width off its frame, which was right, and he crouched at the plant with nothing in his hands.

The handle turned. Three bars, grey-brown, a step.

"Varn," he said.

"Noah."

The eyes went to his hands, as they always did. Then they did a thing they did not always do. They went to the plant, and to his door, and along the corridor one way, and then along it the other, and came back to him. It was how his mother counted pins back into the tin at the end of an evening. All there.

Something had happened in there.

He knew it the way he had known in the summer that the hatch was shut. Nothing was different that he could have pointed to. The service dress was the service dress. But the Deputy had a way of standing that used no more of a body than the job needed, and today one hand was not quite still.

"Long one?" he said.

"The same length as all of them."

He waited. He had learned to wait, at this hatch. If he waited, he got all the clauses.

"One part of it was longer," the Deputy said.

That was all of them.

He had asked once what was behind the hatch, and been told *a watch*. He had asked what it watched, and been told that it didn't, and he had laughed. He had been holding a watering can at the time. It had seemed to him a very funny thing to be told with a straight face. A room where nothing was watched, with a rank on it.

He would not have laughed today. He did not know what had been in the room. He knew who had been standing next to it.

He did not ask. It surprised him a little how easy it was. He had thought that not asking would be like holding the can full, all that weight and nowhere to pour it. It was not. He could carry this. It was Iklix's, and it was kept behind a door he lived beside, and he found that he would rather live beside it than be told.

"The squash man's left a whole wallful," he said. "Somebody's going to have to do something about it."

The Deputy looked at him for a moment. Something in the face let go, without a sound.

"I will need a quarter of an hour."

"I'll be here."

"I know." And then, instead of walking on, the Deputy stayed where the Corps stands. "Noah. Nothing is wrong. That can be supported. It is in a log. I cannot say more than is in it."

"I didn't ask."

"No. You never have."

It was not said as a complaint. He was not sure what it was said as. He went a little red in case.

The Deputy went off along the ring toward the room the Corps kept. He stayed down by the plant. The soil was two days from the sixth and beginning to pale, and the leaves were doing well on it.

On his other side was the hatch. He did not look at it. He had lived by it all his life, and knew where it was.

*What's behind it?*

*A watch.*

It had been the whole answer. He had been given the whole answer on a fifth day, in the early spring, and had not been able to hear it for laughing.

He stood up, not too fast, and waited the quarter of an hour.
