:::pov iklix-varn

Late in the autumn somebody asked the Deputy the way.

Varn was coming along the ring at the hour, as on every day since the early spring, and the corridor was doing what the corridor did. Ahead of the hatch a person stood with a bag at their feet and a reader in one hand, reading the doors, and Varn knew the stance before knowing anything else about them, because Varn had stood in it. They had been let off a spoke car within the hour. They had a number and nothing else.

They did not look at the tab. They looked at a face that was walking as though it knew where it was going, which was fair, and held the reader out, and said a door number.

"Four doors past the hatch," Varn said. "On the left."

"Past the what?"

Varn stopped. A Deputy on the way to a shift does not stop in a corridor, and Varn stopped anyway, because the person was right. There was nothing there to be past. There was a door with a name on it, and a door with a plant outside it, and between them a plain flush panel with a number, the colour of the wall.

"That," Varn said, and pointed.

The stranger looked at it. It was the first time since the spring that Varn had seen anybody look at the hatch, and it took them about as long as it takes to look at a wall. Then they counted four doors on, on the left, and thanked the Deputy, and picked up the bag, and went.

Varn stood where Varn was.

On the first day, with a kit bag and a posting order folded once, Varn had watched two residents walk past that hatch without a glance and had understood that they had never looked at it. It had seemed a sound reading at the time. It had been made by somebody who had been on the ring for the length of a corridor, and it had gone into no log, and so in most of a year nothing had ever sent it back.

Nobody finds a door by a thing they have never looked at.

Varn had not been taught to say *past the hatch*. Nobody on the ring had said, *this is how we say it*. Varn had heard it in the corridor for three seasons without hearing it: a man with a crate, a child calling to another child, a woman at the door with the name on it telling somebody on a reader where to leave something. *Past the hatch. Before the hatch. The door after the hatch.* It had gone in the way the hum of the rack had gone in. Every one of them knew exactly where it was. They had stopped needing to look, and Varn, who had stopped counting the six lights somewhere in the third week and would still have known before turning round if one of them had changed colour, ought to have recognised that sooner.

There was nothing to strike, the reading never having been entered. Varn struck it anyway, with one line, so that it could still be read.

Then Varn used the handle.

"Varn," Smith said, as a fact.

The six lights were the colour they were. Varn went to the desk and stood where a person stands, and the shift began, and was the shift. At the end of it Smith turned the log toward the Deputy and said, "Enter it," and Varn wrote the instruments, and the hatch, opened once and closed once, and the officers, named. No event. The pen did not stop. A stranger had asked the way in a corridor and been told it, and the corridor was not the watch's.

Smith read it as though it had been longer, and initialled it.

It was the hatch line that stayed with Varn, going out. *Opened once, by the Deputy, and closed once.* It was in the log in an Academy hand for every shift since the early spring, and it was a fair account of what the Deputy had done on Eden. A corridor, the length of it between the room the Corps kept for its Deputy and this handle. Two hundred metres toward the Compact side, on orders. The underside of the dock ring, on orders. An arc of the ring ridden out and ridden back, on evenings off the watch, as far as the near terraces and never further. Varn had that afternoon given a stranger directions on a habitat with the confidence of somebody who had seen it.

*Stand where you like,* Smith had said on the first day, and Varn had taken it as a remark about a compartment. She had since said that another of that day's sentences was also advice. She had not said so of this one. Varn noted that she had not said it was not.

The room the Corps kept was further along the ring. Varn went to it, and took off the service dress and hung it, and the tab with it, three bars, seated by a quartermaster and not to be moved by anyone else. There was a shirt that had never been on any kit list. Varn put it on, and a hand went to the collar from habit and found cloth.

Then Varn walked back along the ring the way a Deputy came at the hour, and reached the hatch, and did not use the handle.

The soil in the pot was dark. It was the sixth day of the week, then, and his mother had been at it that morning, and Varn found that this had been known before looking. Since the spring it had been here, at the plant, at the hour: the Deputy coming out through the hatch and somebody crouched at the leaves with nothing in his hands. It had never once been the door. The door was his, and the corridor was nobody's, and the corridor had suited them both.

Varn knocked, which was right.

:::pov zoe-smith

The Deputy had gone at the hour. Smith had not. The end of a quarter brought its own forms, and she wrote them the way she wrote everything, after the thing had been stood.

The hatch was on the latch. Through it the corridor went about its evening: a door, a child, a trolley with one wheel that had needed seeing to since the summer. Then steps she almost knew. She had listened to that walk come along the ring at the hour for the better part of a year, in boots for grating, and these were not boots, and it was not the hour, and she did not place them until they stopped. They stopped a door short of the hatch.

Then a knock. Two knuckles, twice, unhurried, with the manners still in it.

She had heard that knock exactly once, on the other side of her own hatch, on a first day in the early spring. She had thought then that the Academy still taught manners and still did not teach hatches, and she had been right about the Academy.

A knock is for a door somebody lives behind. Nobody lives behind a hatch. There had been nothing wrong with the knock. It had been one door out, and it had taken the Deputy from the early spring to the late autumn to carry it along, which was not slow. She had known officers who never carried theirs anywhere.

She left the hatch on the latch for a reason, and had given the reason to nobody: a compartment that nobody looks at should not also be a compartment that nobody can hear. She had never settled whether that ran the other way. In fifteen Deputies it had not needed settling.

She got up, and crossed the compartment, and closed the hatch. It closed the way a hatch closes, without a sound.

Then she went back to the forms. She wrote nothing down. It had not happened in the compartment.

:::pov noah-adeyemi

People did not knock at their door. People who came to it knew them, and called through it.

So he opened it already knowing, more or less, and still looked at the collar first. He always did. It was not rudeness. The collar told him which name he had, and at the hour, at the plant, in the grey-brown, it was *Varn* and there was a step. He had learned to like the step. It was only where the Corps stood.

There was no tab. There was a shirt, and above it a face he had mostly seen over service dress, and it looked the way it always looked without the tab, which was younger, and more like somebody in particular.

"Iklix," he said.

"Noah."

Then he watched Iklix look past him, not into the room, which would not have been like Iklix, but at the door itself, the inside of it, standing open against the wall. His mother's lettering was there at eye height, where it had been since before he could read it. ADEYEMI.

"It is on the inside."

"I said. In the spring." He heard how fast he had said it in the spring. "I told you the first time you asked my name."

"You did. I had not been able to check."

He laughed. It was not a long laugh, and nobody took a step back, and he noticed that nobody had, the way he still noticed it every time.

"I have a question for the door," Iklix said.

"Go on."

"I gave somebody directions today. Four doors past the hatch, on the left." Iklix said it as carefully as if it were going into something. "It was correct. I have been on this ring since the early spring. I have seen this corridor, and two hundred metres toward the Compact side, and under the dock, and the line as far as the near terraces with you. I have told a stranger where things are on a ring I have seen that much of. I have never been all the way round it."

He waited for the question. Then he understood that this had been it. It was how they asked for things behind that hatch: they said what could be supported and stood there.

"Nobody's been all the way round," he said.

"Somebody must have."

"The drivers, if it had drivers. You go where you're going and you come back. That's what it's for." He thought about it honestly, because Iklix would know if he had not. "I did it once. I was nine, it was a dare. I fell asleep somewhere on the far side and woke up at our stop and told everybody I'd seen all of it."

"Had you?"

"I'd seen the inside of my eyelids all the way round."

Iklix did not laugh, quite. It came near. "Then it has not been done."

"No," he said. "I suppose it hasn't."

It had taken him two days and a watering can, in the spring, to come out of this door with nothing in his hands. Iklix had come up the corridor without the tab and knocked. He did not say that. He took his overshirt off the hook, and stepped out past the plant, and pulled the door shut behind him, and the name went back inside.

They passed the hatch. It was shut, which it mostly was not at this hour; he had lived beside it for long enough to know its habits without ever having looked for them. Neither of them turned a head.

"She gives it as the address," he said. "My mother. The door after the hatch. Before it, if you're coming the other way. She can tell which way round the ring a person lives from which they say."

"I heard myself say it today. I do not know when I started."

"I never told you what she said. In the spring, when she had me ask if the hatch was staying, and you said it's not the Corps that came, it's the one that stays." He had carried that home across four metres of corridor like something full to the brim. "I told her. Exactly like you said it."

"What did she say?"

"She said, 'Well, of course it is. How else would anybody find us?'"

Iklix was quiet for about six doors.

"I thought I was telling her something."

"You were telling me," he said.

They went out by the terrace door, into the ring's evening, and at the platform he turned toward the side the light trains went from.
