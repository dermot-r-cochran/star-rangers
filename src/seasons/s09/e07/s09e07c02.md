:::pov iklix-varn

The gate at the platform took an ordinary fare and had no interest in whose it was.

Varn had noticed that on the first evening off the watch, months ago, and noticed it again every time. On the fold-approach side the tab was a rank. On the Compact side it was a courtesy. On a platform, in a shirt, Varn was a person with a fare, and a woman with a bag of greens said, "Shift along, love," and Varn shifted along.

The train came in from the left and Noah stepped onto it without looking at the board. Nobody on Eden looked at the board. There were two ways round a ring and everybody had been born knowing which was which.

It pulled out against the spin, and the weight went.

Not all of it. A little: enough to feel under the ribs, and in the wrists, and in the way the floor asked slightly less of the knees. The Academy teaches it as a correction. A body carried round a ring a little slower than the ring is turning weighs a little less than it did standing still, and a body carried faster weighs more, and an instrument on a moving car has to be told so. Varn could have written the term. Nothing in the Academy had said that it would arrive in the body like the moment before a laugh.

"Out the light way, home the heavy way," Noah said. He had one hand on the rail overhead. "That's the rule. You're tired by then anyway, and it's how you know you went somewhere."

"And all the way round?"

He thought about it. "Light the whole way. You never pay it back." He looked mildly troubled. "It feels like cheating."

Varn took the rail too. Their hands were on the same length of it, a hand's width apart, and the train's motion closed the width and opened it again and neither of them moved a hand. Varn could have logged the distance. Varn did not.

The near terraces went by, the stop where they had always got off, and the doors opened on it and closed, and that was the last of the ring that Varn had seen.

After that it was all new, and it would not hold still to be looked at. The land rose ahead of the train and rose behind it, as it did everywhere, so that wherever the train stood it stood at the bottom. Planted roofs. A long run of workshop fronts with their shutters down for the evening. Water in a cut channel, keeping pace with the line for a while and then turning under it. A playing field tilted up the curve with nobody on it and its lines very white. Stops whose names Varn had read on the line's map and never heard said, and at each of them people got off who lived there, and had never been asked the way to the hatch, and would not have known what was meant.

The lights along the band were going down toward the ring's evening. Ahead, where the rise went into haze, windows were coming on.

"Next one," Noah said. "That's as far from our door as you can get and still be on Eden. When we were small we said you could feel it."

"Can you?"

"No." The doors opened. "But we said it."

The full weight came back on the platform like a kit bag taken up again. There was a low wall beyond it, and a terrace below the wall, and round leaves spilling down the stone in the last of the light, with flowers in them the colour of the lamps.

Varn looked up.

There was the roof, and the long band of windows, gone dark blue. Past that, not to be seen, the hub. Past the hub, the same distance again, the other side of the ring, floor toward them, with a corridor on it, and in the corridor a door with a name and a door with a plant.

"Home's straight up," Noah said. He pointed, roughly.

"A little spinward of that." Varn moved his arm by the width of a hand, and it was the first time that evening either of them had touched the other, and Varn finished the sentence anyway. "There. That is the hatch."

He kept the arm where it had been put. "You can't know that."

"The watch is on the far side of one wall from your mother's lettering. I have stood between them and it since the spring. I know where that is from anywhere on this ring." It had come out more certain than Varn usually let anything come out. It could be supported. Varn let it stand.

He picked a leaf off the wall and ate it, the way a person does a thing he has done since he was small, and then picked another and held it out. It passed from his fingers to Varn's, and neither hand hurried.

It tasted green, and then, late, of pepper.

"Nasturtium," he said. "Ours is one of these. I grew it from seed off this side the year I started school. It's never flowered. There isn't the light for it in the corridor." He sat down on the wall. "It does all right on once a week. It'd rather be here."

Varn sat. The stone was cool. Up the rise, a long way round, where a sky would have had stars, there were lit windows, and they were kitchens.

"What do you think about, in there?" he said. "All shift. I asked you once what it watched and you said it doesn't, and I never asked again. That seemed like the whole answer."

"It was the whole answer."

"But you think about something."

Varn said what could be supported. "There is a story. It is not a secret. It is in an archive that anyone can ask for. She told it to me on the seventh day, across the desk. A girl at a well, on Earth, a long time ago. Something came to the well one night, and she said what she saw."

He waited. "And?"

"It does not end."

"Tell me it."

"Not yet."

He did not push. He had never pushed. He looked at the water going by under the wall, and Varn found that the reasons were owed.

"It is told across a desk, by the officer who has you first. It was told to her that way, and she has told it to every Deputy she has had. I asked her what it is for, in the spring, and she answered me, and the answer is hers and I cannot give it to you. I have had the story for three seasons. I do not know yet what I would be handing over. When I have a Deputy I will tell it, and then I think I will be allowed to tell it to anybody."

"When's that?"

"Field Officer first. Years."

"Here?"

That was the question under the question, and it had been under most of the year.

"Nobody stays a Deputy on a first posting," Varn said. "The watch stays. The officer stays, if she asks to. The Deputy is moved on when the Corps says, and the Corps has not said." It was not a comfort, and was not meant as one. "When it says, I will tell you the same day. That I can support."

He nodded. He had known it; Varn could see that he had known it since he looked up what three bars were, because the same page would have told him how long anybody kept them.

"I can wait," he said.

Then he heard it, and went a little red in the going light, and did not take it back. "For the story," he said. And then, because he had never been able to pretend he had not looked: "Not only for the story."

There was a hand's width of cool stone between his hand and Varn's, as there had been on the rail.

The desk step had moved without being decided. Varn had been told so by an officer who never told a Deputy what a thing meant, and had believed her. This was not that. Varn looked at the width of stone and decided, and would remember deciding, and put a hand flat down beside his so that the sides of them met.

His was warm. The stone was not.

Nothing on the terrace changed, and nobody said anything, and that was apparently allowed.

:::pov noah-adeyemi

He did not move his hand. He was not sure he could have.

He had asked what was behind the hatch, in the spring, with a can he did not need, and been told *a watch*, and laughed, and paid for it. Now the answer was sitting on a wall on the wrong side of the world with pepper on its tongue, and had just told him plainly that the Corps would take it away sometime and that he would be told the same day.

Nobody had ever promised him anything that exactly. People said *soon* and *we'll see*. This had a day in it, and he would be able to check it.

He knew it the way he knew a spoke car had stopped. The weight was all there, and it was where it should be.

:::pov iklix-varn

They took the light way on, because it was the only way that finished the ring, and came in at their own platform from the side that neither of them had ever arrived from.

It was ring's night, nearly. The dwellings along their stretch stood a little above the line, in a low run, each with its window lit. Varn had never seen them from outside. From the corridor they were doors.

Varn counted them from the wrong end without meaning to, and then stopped counting, because there was a gap.

One dark width, in the run of lit windows. Not broken; there was nothing there to be lit. A length of wall the colour of the wall. On one side of it a window with a lamp, and on the other a window with a shelf of small pots along the sill.

A compartment lit evenly from nowhere in particular. Varn had stood in it since the early spring and had not once thought to ask where a window would have gone.

"That's how I find ours," Noah said. He was not pointing. He was standing at the car's door with his overshirt over one shoulder, and looking, in the way he never looked in the corridor. "From the train. Since I was little. You look for the dark one, and we're the next along."

"Everybody on this car can see it."

"Everybody on every car. It's the only one." He glanced at Varn, and then back. "Nobody minds it. It's just where we are."

The train slowed, and the weight changed a beat before it stopped.

:::pov zoe-smith

She finished the forms at ring's night and closed the log, which had been closed already, and went out through the hatch and shut it behind her.

The corridor was dim and nearly empty. A resident carrying a sleeping child nodded to the service dress, and she nodded back, and stood a moment with her hands loosely together, as she did in corridors.

Two people were coming along the ring from the platform end.

She knew most of the ring by small things and at a distance. One of them she knew by a walk she had heard at the hour since the spring, in other shoes tonight. The other she had once watched fail, twice, to say something to a plant. They were not talking. There was no step between them.

She did not note it. No assessment she had ever written had a place for it, and the next form the Corps sent would ask what the Deputy had done on the watch, and she would write what the Deputy had done on the watch.

Her own door was that way, past them. She turned and went the other way along the ring, which was the long way, and by some distance.

She had told fifteen Deputies, on their first day, that the event had not come in her tenure. It had not. She walked home at the ring's own weight, and wrote nothing down.

At the door with the plant he stopped, and Varn stopped, and there was the plant between them, which had never flowered and was doing well.

"Iklix," he said.

"Noah."

He went in. The name on the inside of the door showed for as long as the door was open, and then it was a door.

Varn walked on along the ring, toward the room the Corps kept. The corridor curved gently away in both directions, and they were one direction; Varn had the fare to prove it. A late resident came the other way and passed the hatch without a glance.

Varn was three doors beyond it before noticing that there had been nothing to decide.

Nobody looked at the hatch. Varn had not either, and knew where it was.
