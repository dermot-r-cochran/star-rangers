:::pov iklix-varn

Everything on the kit list was in the bag, and one thing that was not on it.

Varn had checked it twice in the room the Corps kept, which was one more time than the list needed. Service dress, the second set. A reader. A torch that the reader made unnecessary and the list kept anyway. Two posting orders, printed, each folded once: the one that had brought a Deputy onto this ring in the early spring, and the one that was taking the Deputy off it. And a shirt of plain undyed cloth, long in the arm, with nothing at the collar. No quartermaster had issued it. It had been cut behind the door with the plant, for a reach that never came, and it had been to the terraces a great many times. Varn put it in last.

The hook was empty. Varn looked at that, and shut the door, and it was a room the Corps kept.

Then along the ring with the bag at the hip, the way a Deputy came to the shift.

The soil in the pot was dark. It was the sixth day, and she had been at it that morning, as on every sixth day for a dozen years and more. The hatch had its number and nothing else. Two residents went past it while Varn stood there, and neither of them looked at it. Every one of them knew exactly where it was.

Varn used the handle.

"Varn," Smith said, as a fact.

The six lights were the colour they were. Varn set the bag down by the wall, and went to the desk, and stood where a person stands.

The shift was the shift. That was the strange part of it, and Varn was most of the way through before finding the words. Nothing in the compartment knew that it was the last one. The rack hummed at the pitch it hummed at. The instruments filed their agreement every ninety seconds into a log that nobody on the watch read, and would file it tomorrow. The hatch was on the latch, and the corridor came in under everything, as it did: a door, a child on something with wheels, the trolley.

Varn had come onto this ring expecting the event, and had been told on the first day that it had not come. It had not come. A light had changed colour for one interval in the autumn and had come back, and the instruments had filed no word for it, and Varn had written down what happened. That was the posting. Varn could not have told a board what had been done in it. Varn could have told a board what had been entered.

Late in the shift Varn said the thing that had been waiting since the order. It was not a question, so the Academy's rule about questions did not cover it.

"I have not found out what it is for. The story."

Smith put the pen down.

"I had it across this desk on the seventh day. I asked you on the thirtieth, and you said it is handed over as a question, and that you have been waiting for one of us to tell you." Varn kept the voice level. "I thought I might have something to give back before I went. I have not."

"No," Smith said.

Varn waited for the rest of the sentence.

"Fourteen went out through a hatch of mine with it. None of them had anything by that day either. One came back with a question, in the summer, and it was not his own." She picked the pen up. "The order in your bag has a date to report by. That was handed over without one."

It was not a comfort, and was not meant as one. It could be supported. Varn found that this was better.

"The officer there will not have it," Varn said. "Field Officer Ruiz did not."

"You have met one Field Officer who did not. That is one entry." She wrote something, and it was not about this. "I do not know what the officer there has. Neither do you."

"No, Field Officer."

At the end of the shift she turned the log toward the Deputy and said, "Enter it."

Varn wrote the instruments. The hatch, opened once and closed once. The officers, named. Then: *Deputy Officer Varn relieved of the watch at the end of this shift, by order*, and the reference. *No event.*

It was in an Academy hand, like every entry since the early spring. Above it the column ran back up the leaf and over, in a dozen hands and then in one. Tomorrow's would be in hers. Varn looked for the place where a Deputy would have written something more. There was no such place, and that was correct.

Smith read it as though it had been longer, and initialled it.

Then she turned back to the desk, as she had on the first day when there was no more to show.

"Stand where you like," she said.

Varn had the bag half up.

On the first day it had been the last thing she said about the posting, and Varn had taken it for a remark about a compartment. She had told the Deputy since that another of that day's sentences was also advice. She did not say so of this one now. She did not say anything else.

Varn noted that it had been said, this time, to somebody with a bag on one shoulder and an order to a compartment that was not hers.

"Field Officer," Varn said.

"Varn."

Varn went out through the hatch, and drew it to behind, as far as it stood on her shifts and no further. Nobody had ever given the Deputy a reason for that. It was how the hatch was left.

:::pov noah-adeyemi

He did not crouch. There was nothing to check on a plant that had been watered that morning, and he had got past needing to be found doing something.

The hatch opened at the hour, and came back to a finger's width off its frame. Three bars, grey-brown, and the bag, which he had never seen. It was smaller than he had thought it would be. All through the spring he had pictured somebody arriving with more than that.

The eyes went to his hands, and then to his face, and stayed.

"That's all of it?" he said.

"It is the list. And a shirt."

"That was never on a list."

"No. It went in last."

They had had the evenings. Three stops out, mostly, and once the whole light way round again in the dark, to see whether it still felt like cheating, and it did. Whatever there had been to say without the tab had been said without it, on a wall where a man left leeks. None of that was the corridor's. This was the other thing. This was the Corps going back along the ring with the same bag, and it was done in the grey-brown, at the step, where anybody could see it.

"I have a question for the door," the Deputy said.

"Go on."

"If something is sent to you from off Eden. A letter. What is put on it?"

He had not expected that one, so he answered it straight. "Adeyemi. Then the door after the hatch, fold-approach side. That's what we give."

"The hatch has a number."

"Nobody outside knows the number. Everybody knows the hatch." He heard what he had said. "It gets here. It always has."

"Then I can support that I will write. I cannot support what will be in it. There is a great deal on a watch that I can say no more of than is in the log."

"Tell me the weather."

"It is a station. I do not think it has any."

"Then tell me that."

The Deputy looked at him for a moment. He thought it was being entered somewhere.

"There is the other thing. The story. I said I would tell it when I have a Deputy. That is years, and I will not be on this ring for them." It came out clause by clause, and he waited for all of them. "It is in an archive that anyone may ask for. You could ask. I would not be handing it over. You would only have it."

He had thought about that. He had looked up how a person asks.

"I'd need her name," he said. "The girl at the well. You never said it."

He watched the Deputy go back through it: a wall, a terrace, the far side of the ring.

"No. I did not."

"So don't." His voice was not level, and he let it not be. "I said I can wait. I meant for you to tell it. Not for somebody to send it."

"That may not be a thing I can make happen."

"I know. I looked up how long four bars takes." He took his hands out of his pockets, since they were no use there. "I didn't say I'd be let. I said I can."

Nobody said anything. Down the corridor the trolley went by, with its wheel.

He looked at the collar. He always had. It told him which name he had. The tab was there, three bars, seated where a quartermaster had put it, and at the hatch, at the hour, in the grey-brown, the name was *Varn* and there was a step.

"Iklix," he said.

It was the wrong name for the collar, and he knew it was. He said it properly, with all of it in.

The Deputy did not correct it. The Deputy stood where the Corps stands, one step further off than there was any reason to stand, and did not come nearer and did not move back.

"Noah."

It was the voice from the wall, and not the one from the hatch. For the length of a name there were two people in the corridor and one step, and the step was only where one of them was standing.

Then the Deputy walked on along the ring, toward the spoke cars, with the bag at the hip.

He did not watch the whole way. The corridor curved up, and it took people out of sight from the head down, and he had never liked seeing that done to anybody. He went in. His mother was at the table with her hands flat on it and nothing in them.

"That's the walk gone," she said.

"Yes."

"And we were told."

"The same day."

She nodded. It was not the nod she gave to service dress. She did not say anything else, having said in the spring that she would not.

::::: scene 3

:::pov noah-adeyemi

For some days there was one walk at the hatch, and it was hers.

Then he came along the ring from his shift, at the hour, and there was somebody standing in the corridor with a bag.

Grey-brown. A small kit bag at the hip. A leaf in one hand, folded once, and a reader in the other, and they were reading the doors. They had a number and nothing else.

Three bars. He did not need to look that up.

They were younger than he had expected, and then he worked out that they were not. They were about what Iklix had been in the early spring. It was he who was most of a year on.

The Deputy found the number. It was on a plain flush panel the colour of the wall, between a door with a name on it and a door with a plant outside, and the Deputy stood in front of it for a moment like somebody who had expected more of a door. Two people from further along went past, talking, and neither of them looked at it. He watched the Deputy see that. He thought he watched the Deputy take them for people who had never noticed it was there.

He could have said. *We know where it is. We find our own windows by it.* It was not his to say. Somebody he knew had found it out without being told, and it had taken from the spring to the late autumn.

So he said the only thing that was his.

"Evening."

The Deputy turned. The eyes went to his face first, and then, late, to his hands, which had nothing in them.

"Good evening." It was said carefully, by somebody not sure whether a resident was to be answered.

He went on to his own door. Behind him he heard it: two knuckles, twice, unhurried, with the manners still in it.

He stopped with his hand on the door.

He had heard that knock once before, in the late autumn, from the inside of this door, and had opened it already knowing. He had thought it was Iklix's own. So it was the Academy's. They must teach it, and send them all out knocking like that at whatever number they were given. Carrying it one door along, to a door somebody lived behind, had been Iklix's.

Nobody answered. A hatch did not get answered. After a little the handle turned, and the Deputy went in, and the hatch came back to a finger's width off its frame.

*A different walk at the hour, and nobody tells us.* Nobody had told them about this one. He found that he had not needed telling. He knew what three bars were, and about how long anybody kept them. He knew what was behind the hatch.

A watch.

He went in. The soil was two days from the sixth, and beginning to pale.

:::pov zoe-smith

The sixteenth Deputy the Corps had handed her knocked on the hatch.

So the Academy still taught manners, and still did not teach hatches. She wrote neither down.

A moment before the knock she had heard a resident say "Evening" in the corridor, and be answered. She knew the voice. In all her tenure on this ring she had not heard anybody in that corridor say it to a tab. She put it with the small things, under no heading.

The handle turned. Three bars, a kit bag, and an order that had been folded once and then smoothed flat again. This one had sat down with it. She said the name, as a fact, and then her own, as another, and did not offer a hand.

"The event has not come in my tenure."

She said it flatly, as a fact about the posting, in the voice she had used fifteen times. It was a fact. For one interval in the autumn it had been a fact she could not have supported, and she had stood the interval, and at the end of it the sentence had still been true. She said it with that in it, and in the same voice.

She watched it be heard. Some of them heard a warning. Some heard a promise that nothing would be asked of them. This one looked at the six lights, quickly, as though one of them might have been listening.

She showed the Deputy the instruments. Six, each on a different principle. Every ninety seconds. A log that nobody on the watch read.

"The watch does not watch anything," she said. "It stands here. It is the nearest qualified officer to the event if the event comes. That is the entire posting." She turned back to the desk. "Stand where you like."

The Deputy stood one step further from the desk than there was any reason to stand.

They all did. It was not a mistake. It was a report, and an honest one, with today's date on it. A step nearer there was a place on the deck where a person stands. Somebody had stood in it from the thirtieth day of a posting to the last, without having decided to, and had gone out through the hatch on a sixth day with a bag. There was nothing on the deck to show it. She had looked, the next morning, and had noted that she looked.

She would tell this one nothing about the step. She would tell the story of the girl at the well when it was asked for, or on the last shift if it was not, and she would not say what it was for, since she did not know. Fifteen were out in the Corps and beyond it with the thing in their kit. One had sent back a question. She had stopped expecting more than that. She had not stopped waiting.

The hatch was on the latch. The corridor came in under everything: a door, a wheel, a child.

At the end of the shift she would turn the log toward the Deputy and say, "Enter it." She did not know what this one would write. The posting had never required her to know anything in advance.

The six lights were the colour they were, and the sixteenth stood the shift.
