:::pov tekka

The handlers had padded everything, which was kind of them.

Tekka had worked out what the padding was for by the second carrier. The station was a human one, on the route between Sol's yards and the long transit, and its handlers moved passengers across a gap between two ships through a soft sleeve with a line down the middle of it. A human crossed by pulling along the line. A passenger the size of a Chthonari was carried, in a rigid frame with a grip on it, and the frame was lined on every face with a thick grey foam so that nothing in it could be knocked. Four frames had been brought to the lock. The crew lay on the station's deck beside them. It was plate, untuned, and it rang a little, as everything the humans made did.

It was deck, though. Four on one deck could feel each other well enough.

The module had gone already. The burrow the crew had sealed and lived in for six weeks under Eden's dock ring was freight now, and it crossed on the station's arm with nobody in it, because the station's rule was that nothing living rides in freight under transfer. Tekka found no fault with the rule. A rule is a course somebody has run before. It only meant that for the length of the sleeve the crew would be in the foam.

Kattik had tried. Kattik had the slate out, the one kept for the air, and had written on it in the humans' letters: *This crew prefers bare structure. No padding, please.* The handler had read it, and had gone away, and had come back with a second liner for each frame.

Nobody struck anything for a moment.

"They read *padding*," Tsikk said through the plate, "and they brought some."

"They read *prefers*," Kattik said. "It was the wrong word. I do not know the right one." The slate went back into its case. There is no word for it in the humans' letters, and the crew had been told so before leaving the Belt: the air could be taught that the Undersong was there, and that was the ceiling. Kattik had gone to the ceiling and come back with more foam.

So Tekka gave them the course.

It was the one thing to hand. The transit's sheet for passengers gave the sleeve's length and the rate a handler was permitted to move a carried passenger, and Tekka had turned those into a count at the working beat, the way one turns any tunnel into a count. Forty to the mouth of the sleeve. Ninety in it. Ten more to the far collar. "At a hundred and forty there is metal," Tekka said. "Before that there is nothing to listen to, so do not listen. Count. It is a short course and it has no turns."

"And if there is no metal at a hundred and forty?" Tikket asked.

"Then you have counted fast. Apprentices do."

Kattik set the order. Tekka first, to run the course and prove it. Then Kattik, with the log. Then Tsikk. Tikket last. "The one who hears the most keeps the deck the longest," Kattik said, and Tikket did not argue. Tekka had learned on Eden to notice when Tikket did not argue.

The handler closed the foam over Tekka. The deck stopped.

That was all it was. Tekka had expected more of it. No plate, no crew, no station, and one's own six limbs folded against a soft face that returned nothing. Tekka counted. A memorised thing does not need a deck under it. That was the use of memorising, and nobody in the crew had ever said so. Forty: a change in the push of the foam, which would be the mouth. Ninety more.

At a hundred and thirty-one the frame came down on metal, and the foam opened, and there was a hull.

Nine early. The handler had been quick. Tekka noted it, and struck a name into the new deck, a single strike held into its ring.

Nothing struck back.

The hull was loud with machinery, somebody else's and none of it tuned. That was only strangeness. Under the strangeness there was nobody. Tekka had been on unfamiliar decks before, and alone at the end of a tunnel with the crew a long way up it and faint. Tekka had never in a working life lain on a structure and found no person in it.

It would be a hundred and forty until Kattik came. Tekka began the count again and stopped at six.

A hundred and forty from when? The count had a length and it had no beginning. Tekka did not know when the foam had closed on Kattik, or whether it had yet. Somewhere back in the sleeve a course was being run, and the one who had set it could not tell where on it anyone was.

Tekka lay on the stranger's hull and waited, and for once had nothing memorised that covered it.

::::: scene 2

:::pov tikket

Three times the deck lost one of them.

The Undersong marks a person two ways. Felt, through whatever is underfoot, or not felt. There is nothing between. Tekka had been on the plate, held and ringing, and then the foam closed and Tekka was not felt, and no strike exists that means *is in the sleeve*. Then Kattik. Then Tsikk, who struck the plate once on the way into the frame, sharp, for no reason but to leave something on it.

Then it was Tikket's deck and nobody else's, and the handler's two heavy feet, and the frame.

The foam closed.

Tikket had stopped believing a deck on the second day on Eden. That had been a hard thing and a simple one. There had still been a deck. One could go and lie on the third mount at the end of a shift and be told the truth by it.

Here there was no deck to believe or not. Tikket struck a limb against the lower carapace, two quick strikes and a close, and felt it land, because a body is a structure too. It was a name with one listener. Tikket did not do it again.

Count, Tekka had said. Tikket counted. Forty, and the push of the foam changed, and that was the mouth of the sleeve; Tekka's courses were always right about where things were.

At sixty-something the push went out of the foam altogether.

They had stopped. Tikket did not know why and had nothing to ask it with. The count went on, because it was all there was to do. A hundred. A hundred and thirty-one, when by rights Tekka had touched metal. A hundred and forty, when there was to be metal for anyone.

There was foam.

The course had ended and Tikket was still inside it. Past a hundred and forty Tekka had given no numbers. Tikket went on a little way, and then let the count go, because a count with nothing at the end of it is only a way of striking nothing.

What came up in its place, unasked, was the mount.

Tikket did not memorise. That was the standing difference between Tikket and half the crew, and Kattik had as good as said so. But six weeks of shift-ends on one member leave it in the limbs, and there it was, the third of six, ringing a little under a load nobody had written down. It was as exact as it had been on the last shift before the lift.

Was it right?

There was no way to tell. On Eden the remembered mount had been checked against the mount at the end of every shift, and corrected. Here was the mount as Tikket had it, and nothing to lay it against. It might have drifted already. It might drift a little every day from now on and never once feel different. It was the first thing Tikket had ever known with nothing under it, and Tikket held it very carefully and did not at all like holding it.

Then, through the frame, a beat.

It was faint. It came in where the grip joined the frame, the one hard path through all that kindness. A slow double strike and a rest, and again. It was not a machine, because a machine does not waver, and this wavered a very little, the way a member does that is alive to its load. Tikket knew the humans by their feet, heavy and two-beat on a deck. This was not feet. It was inside one of them, and it was coming down an arm.

Tikket could not read it. It said nothing about whether the one holding the frame was easy or afraid, or thinking of something else entirely. Nothing of that kind had ever crossed from a human, and nothing did now. It was only a structure doing what it was left to do, steadily, in a sleeve where everything else had stopped.

Tikket laid it against Tekka's count, out of habit, and it was slower. That made two measures that disagreed, and no deck to settle them. Tikket kept both.

The push came back into the foam. Some while later, which neither measure agreed on, the frame came down.

Metal.

The hull roared. It was a stranger's and it was the best thing Tikket had ever lain on. Three names went into it almost at once, a single strike held, a heavy and a light and a heavy, a scrape into a strike. Tikket put the fourth under them, and the deck had four on it.

::::: scene 3

:::pov tekka

"Your count was right," Tikket said. "I stopped in the middle of it."

"Then it was wrong."

"No. The sleeve is ninety. I was in it for more than ninety." Tikket struck this in the flat register, for things that are the case. "Both."

Tekka turned that over and could not make it sit. A course is as long as it is. One runs it fast or slow, but the tunnel does not stop partway and wait. Tekka had come in nine early and thought the handler quick, and had not asked what it meant that a handler could be quick. It meant the course had a person in it. Tekka had counted the handler as part of the sleeve.

"I memorised the wrong thing," Tekka said. "I memorised the tunnel. They were carrying us."

Kattik had the slate out again. Under the line about bare structure there was more writing, in another hand, loose and fast. The handler had written it at the lock on the far side and had put the slate back into the case before the frame was opened.

Kattik struck it out for the crew in the register kept for other people's words.

*Sorry. Padding is the rule for the sleeve and I can't waive it. We had to hold in the middle for the freight arm, longer for the last of you. I kept a hand on the frame bar the whole way across, all four. Somebody told me once that you can feel that.*

Nobody struck anything for some time.

"I felt nothing," Tekka said. "I was counting."

"So was I," Kattik said.

"I was asking the sleeve," Tsikk said. "It had no monitor. I asked anyway."

They waited for the youngest.

"There was a beat," Tikket said. "In the grip. I thought it was only what a human does."

"What did it say?"

"Nothing. I could not read it." Tikket struck this slowly. "It was there, and it went on being there while we were stopped. That is all I have."

Tekka thought about the second liner. The handler had read *no padding* and brought more, and the crew had lain on the plate and decided what that showed. And then that same one had taken four frames across a gap, one at a time, with a hand held where a hand was no use to any human, on the word of somebody, once.

It was not hearing. Tekka would not have it called hearing. It was a person who had been told that there was a thing they could not perceive, and who had done the one small thing the telling allowed.

Kattik did not write in the log. The log was for the job, and the job was behind them. Kattik wrote on the slate, under the handler's hand, though the sleeve was sealed and the handler was on the other side of it and the slate would go home to the Belt in its case and never be read by anyone in the air.

*Felt.*

It was half of all the grammar there is for a person. Tekka had never heard it struck about anyone who lived in the air, and lay there on a loud strange hull, among the other three, hearing how it sat.
