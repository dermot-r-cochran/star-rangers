The bar was still sounding when Kattik came into the contract hall, and so was every other open job on the wall.

That was the wall's purpose. A crew that takes work off the Belt leaves a bar behind it in the hall, cut from the hall's own iron and left free at one end. While the job is open, the bar is free, and it sounds with everything else in the cluster, faintly, under the feet of anyone who comes in. When the job is done the hall's keeper wedges it. A wedged bar is a job with nothing left in it. Nobody keeps a list. One lies on the floor of the hall and knows how many crews are out and roughly which.

The crew's bar was the fourth from the port end. It had been free since before the transit out, and it was free now, a thin steady note that Kattik had felt from two tunnels away.

Kattik had brought the log.

A log is a thing kept for the air. Nothing in the Belt needs one, since a member that is doing more than it was left to do says so to the whole cluster before the shift turns. But a job off the Belt is done on other people's structure, among people who cannot feel it, and they keep their structures in writing. So a crew out there keeps a writing of its own, and brings it home, and the keeper reads the last line.

The keeper was old, and cut slowly, and had wedged more bars than Kattik had stood on decks. The keeper took the log's last entry the way a thing from outside is taken, struck out line by line in the flat register.

*Mount three line: read. By whom, not known to this crew. Mount still rings.*

"Then why is the crew here?" the keeper said.

It was not a rebuke. It was the question the rock would ask. Nobody in the Belt leaves a member ringing over what it was left to carry and goes home. It is not a rule. It is what leaving means.

"The mount is not ours," Kattik said. "We cut the run beside it. The run is sound. The mount is the habitat's."

"Then the habitat is on it."

"The habitat cannot hear it. Nobody there can. They live on that ring all their lives and none of them can hear a member of it." Kattik had struck this through twice on the transit, and it came out no better the third time. "They keep the structure by writing. Somebody measures, and writes what was measured, and somebody else reads the writing and signs that it was read. The mount was written as sound."

"And it rings."

"It carries more than the writing says. We wrote that. It was struck out of their record. Then, by somebody, it was put back."

"By whom?"

"Not known to this crew."

The keeper lay still on the hall floor for a while, with the open bars going on overhead. "Whose is the mount?" the keeper said at last, slowly, as one tests a cut.

"The habitat's. The instruments that hang from it are another body's. The schedule it is inspected on is a third's."

"Three keepers. And nobody on it."

"Nobody who can hear it."

"Why would anyone write that a member is sound?" It was not asked to win. The keeper wanted to know. "One stands on it."

Kattik had no strike for the answer. The log-keeper knew what a certification was. Kattik had held one and read it, and had written a line beside it. But *a true thing that belongs to nobody* would not go into the rock. The Undersong has the thing, and it has those who are in contact with it, and it has nothing for a thing that everybody is in contact with and nobody holds. On Eden the crew had not been able to put what it heard into the air. Kattik had not expected to find that it went no better the other way.

Tekka had come along the wall to be near the argument, as Tekka always did.

"The job is certified," Tekka said. "The client issued the certification. In the air that is the settlement, and the bar can be—"

Tekka stopped.

"No," Tekka said, in a different register. "That is what I said on Eden."

"It is," Kattik said.

"I withdraw it." And then, as was Tekka's way with anything once it had been seen plainly, Tekka gave it a name so that it could be found again. "It is the Eden answer. A thing is certified, so it is settled. I had it for six weeks. I did not know I had brought it home."

The keeper let that go by, since it was the crew's own.

"I will wedge the bar when the log has a last line," the keeper said.

"It has a last line."

"It has a line that is still going." The keeper returned the log, which in the Belt is done by lifting the limbs off it. "That is no mark on the crew. The bar says the job is not finished, and the job is not finished. You know what an open bar carries."

Kattik knew. A crew cannot be in two tunnels. A crew with a bar sounding in the hall takes no new commission, on the Belt or off it; it is given relieving work, the small cuts that take an old load off an old member, until the bar is wedged. Nobody thinks less of it. Everyone who comes into the hall can feel which crew it is.

The humans had written *n/a* and had a file to put it in. The Belt had a bar that anyone could feel. Kattik could not have said which was the harder to work under. The bar, at least, was accurate.

:::pov tsikk

The collar at the port was the only thing in the cluster that had not been left.

Everything else was cut. One took rock away until what stayed was a gallery, a member, a hall, and what stayed had been rock all along. The collar had been made. Somebody in the Federation had formed it out of plate, somewhere else, and brought it, and fixed it to the rock at the port with a ring of fastenings, so that ships built by people who breathe could seal against a burrow. It was good work of its kind. It was not tuned to anything. It rang a little, all the time, at no ratio to the galleries behind it, and people crossed it without lingering.

Tsikk had been coming out to it since the crew came home.

It was hard to explain, and nobody had asked. At home a question struck into the wall comes back before one has finished striking it. The burrow's monitor is cut into the work. It hears what the work hears, and it answers as the rock's own, and after six weeks away Tsikk could not always tell the asking from the knowing. On Eden the answer had come from somewhere else, through a panel, in the humans' letters, a shift's turn after the question. Tsikk had not expected to miss that.

And there was the other matter, which Tsikk had been turning over since the lift and had put to nobody.

The habitat's mind had answered *normal* for six weeks. It had said so to four people who were lying on a member that was neither normal nor wrong. Tsikk had struck the question from the deck as one addresses a burrow's monitor, taking it that a structure with a mind in it was a structure that listened to itself. Then somebody set a second ear on the ring, and the answer changed in a shift.

A monitor that hears its own structure does not need a second ear.

So it had not been cut in. Tsikk had worked that far on the transit and the rest of the way out here on the collar. Eden's mind was fixed to Eden the way this plate was fixed to the port, made somewhere and fastened on. It knew its structure by what the instruments told it, as the humans did. It was the ring, and it lived in the air with the rest of them. Tsikk had asked it what it read and had taken that for asking what it carried, because at home those are one question.

That was not a complaint against it. It had answered every shift. When its record changed it had said so, at once, to a contractor with no standing, without being asked any differently. It had given what it had. Tsikk had asked for less than was wanted and had been given exactly that much.

Tsikk came onto the collar at the turn of the shift and found it already occupied.

Tikket lay at the far side of the plate, by the fastenings, with two limbs laid along the seam, exactly as on the third mount. Tsikk's first thought was not a generous one, and was Kattik's from the spring: *an apprentice who hears a fault in every strange structure*. The collar rang. One of the fastenings was duller than its neighbours, and Tsikk had it now too, lying on the same plate.

Tsikk waited, because an apprentice is owed that.

"It is fine," Tikket said.

"It rings."

"Everything they make rings. This one is strange all the way through." Tikket moved one limb to the dull fastening. "That one sits in softer rock than the rest. Whoever set it knew, and drew it less, and it carries what it was left to carry. Listen to the two beside it. They have not taken anything up for it."

Tsikk listened. It was so.

"On Eden you could not say why," Tsikk said.

"On Eden I could say why for one mount in six. The other five were strange, and I put them with the course because I could not tell strange from wrong. Kattik told me so." There was no heat in it. "I come out here to find whether I can tell yet."

"You could go and stand in any gallery."

"In there I am told." Tikket struck the rock side of the collar, once, lightly, and the cluster gave it back at once in forty voices. "Before I have listened. I cannot find which part of it is me. Out here nothing answers for me. If I am wrong on this plate, I shall be wrong by myself."

"And are you?"

"No," Tikket said. "It is only strange."

Tsikk lay on the made thing a while longer, beside the youngest of the crew. The apprentice had gone looking for the one place at home where it was still possible to be mistaken, and Tsikk found nothing to add.

:::pov kattik

The relieving cut was in an old gallery on the sunward side. A store had stood there for some generations and had been cleared, and the member under it was still braced for a weight that was gone. It was small work. Take off what the member no longer needed, and leave it to sound with its neighbours again.

Deciding what to leave is the whole craft. An apprentice cuts to another's decision for years, and Tikket had cut to Kattik's since the crew was formed.

Tsikk had struck it through to Kattik from the port that morning, plainly, without comment: what the apprentice had said about the fastening, and that it was so.

Kattik had told Tikket once, on a mount on Eden, which kind of wrong it is that gets better. Kattik had said then that it would not be said again, and it was not.

"You decide what stays," Kattik said.

Nobody struck anything. Tekka began to and stopped, which was twice in a season.

Tikket went onto the member and asked it what the burrow asks. *What is this still doing?* The rock answered at once, as it does at home, in the whole cluster's voice, with what any cutter in forty would have left.

Tikket did not start.

Kattik felt the apprentice lie there, past the first answer and past the second, for longer than a relieving cut has ever been worth. Tikket was waiting for the one answer in all that sound that was Tikket's own. Kattik had not known until then that such a thing had to be waited for here. Nobody who had never left would know it.

Then Tikket marked the cut. It was a little inside where Kattik would have marked it. It was not wrong.

They cut to it. Four on one member, in a gallery that answered. Two tunnels behind them a thin bar went on sounding in the hall for anyone to feel, saying that this crew had a job still open, a long way down a gravity well, on a structure none of them would stand on again. Nobody remarked on it.
