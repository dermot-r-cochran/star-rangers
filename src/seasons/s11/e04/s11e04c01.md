::: pov aravena

Egede picked the stone up on the plain, in the wind, thirty paces from the station door, and Aravena saw it happen.

It was before the shift, in the grey that Fliade has for a morning. The frost had not gone and was not going to. Far off on the plain an Anstolik herd was bellowing at another herd about something. Aravena had come out to check the straps on the unit, because the cold made them stiff, and Egede was already out there, walking slowly with eyes on the ground. That was not like Egede. Egede looked at instruments. The ground was Nakagawa's.

Egede stopped. Crouched. Looked at one stone for a long time without touching it.

Then Egede stood up and walked back to the station door, counting. Aravena could see the lips going. Then back out to the stone, counting again. Then Egede took out a handset and held it over the stone, flat, the way you take a picture of a thing where it lies.

Only then did Egede pick it up.

It was about the size of two hands. Dark, and flat on one side. Egede turned it over, and brushed the frost off the flat side with a thumb, and it went into the long pocket on the leg of the coverall, and the flap was closed over it.

Egede saw Aravena then.

"Morning," said Egede.

"Morning."

That was all. Egede went in. Aravena finished the straps.

They went down the long passage, all three, with the light, talking. It was shift sixty-seven. At the junction the imager stayed in its case, because nothing came out of its case until the knock was done. Aravena picked a stone off the junction floor. Not the first one. A good one, rough, the kind that would not roll.

Egede watched the picking. Egede had not watched it before.

Then the third passage, and the squeeze, and the silt, and the one on the line. The stone set down beside the last one. The step back. The thing said. The rock moved, through the boots.

It was the fourth time. Aravena's knees had still not got used to it.

When Aravena came back to the junction Egede was standing by the imager's case with one hand flat on the leg pocket, the way you keep a hand on a thing to be sure it is still there.

::: pov aravena

The pocket was full the next shift, and the one after.

Egede did not take the stone out. Egede did the wall, and ran the imager, and said things, as always. But in between, Egede had questions. They came one at a time, over the top of the work, as if they were about nothing.

"How near do you get? To him. To the one on the line."

"A hand from the feet. That's where the stone goes."

"And the silt. Is it wet?"

"Damp. It holds a print."

"When you put the stone down. Does it look at you, or at the stone?"

Aravena had to think. You do not watch yourself being looked at. You are too busy.

"At me, when I come out. Then at the stone, when it's down. Then it answers."

"It looks at the stone."

"For a moment. Yes."

Egede nodded, and wrote nothing, and went back to the wall.

Aravena knew what that was. Egede built a thing the way Egede read an instrument, with the ways it could go wrong first. Every question was a piece being fitted. How near. How long. What it looks at. Egede was not being curious. Egede was getting ready to ask for something, and wanted to have already answered everything Nakagawa would say.

On the way up the long passage that shift Aravena went by the place at the bottom, where the third passage came in, without stopping. It was still strange to do that. The feet just went on.

Ahead, Egede walked a little to one side, so that the leg with the pocket did not knock on the wall.

::::: scene 3

::: pov nakagawa

Egede put it on the table that evening, at the station, after they had eaten, with the wind going.

Nakagawa looked at it without picking it up. A stone of the plain. Dark, fine-grained, the size of two hands. One face of it was smooth. Not broken smooth. Worn. It had a shine on it like a thing that has been handled for a hundred years, and nobody had handled it. The wind had done that. It had lain on the plain with one side to the weather for longer than the Corps had existed, and the grit in the wind had polished it.

"Thirty paces north of the door," said Egede. "I have a picture of it where it lay, and which way up. It is the planet's own. Say the rule."

"You say it."

"Nothing stays on this world that the planet would keep if the station were abandoned. Nothing left that cannot be taken back." Egede touched the stone. "The planet keeps this already. It kept it before we came. So if it is left at the line, nothing has been left. That is your own argument for the junction stones, and it holds for this one, word for word."

"Go on."

"There is no wind below the roof. Nothing down there looks like this. Nothing *feels* like this. They would know, at one touch, that it came from somewhere else." Egede looked up. "From here. From where we live. One shift, Aravena carries this in place of a junction stone. The same knock. But this time the stone says where we come from."

It was a good argument. Nakagawa let it be a good argument for a moment. Egede had taken the rule and gone through it, not round it, and had come out the other side with the rule still whole.

"What does a knock say?" said Nakagawa.

"Someone is here."

"What would this one say?"

"Someone is here. From up there."

"To us. I asked what it would say to them."

Egede did not answer.

"We know one thing to say at that line," said Nakagawa. "One. We learned it five shifts ago, by reading two years of our own log. We do not know what a strange stone is, to people who have been reading stones since before we had a planet to come from. It might say *from above*. It might say *this one is not from here, be careful*. It might say something there is no word for at this table. I am not going to find out by trying it on the one who answers."

"He gave us something," said Egede. "We keep stepping round that. He carried our unit the whole way down, through that crack, and stood it on its feet for us. And we have given him nothing. We knock. We say *we are here*. We never say *we saw what you did*."

There it was. Nakagawa had known it was under the argument, the way you know there is water under a floor.

"What did we get?" said Nakagawa. "Say what it was."

"The unit."

"Whose unit?"

"Ours."

"With what added?"

Egede began to answer, and did not.

Nakagawa turned to the third of the party, who had said nothing all evening, and who had knelt to it.

"Nothing," said Aravena. "Nothing on it was scratched. I checked it over that night. There wasn't a mark on it that wasn't ours."

"Our own instrument," said Nakagawa. "Put where we would find it. With nothing of theirs on it and nothing taken off. You filed that it was returned. I filed that a line was drawn. Aravena says a thing at a door. I have not chosen and I am not choosing tonight. But there is one fact under all three, and it is that one." A pause. "And when we answered at last, we did it with a stone off their own floor, with nothing of ours on it. I do not know that that is why we were answered. I know it is what we did the morning we were."

The wind went.

"So we never give them anything," said Egede.

"I don't know what they give. I have not been shown it. I know what we were shown, and I will go on doing that until I am shown something else."

Egede looked at the stone for a while.

"It goes in the file," said Nakagawa. "The proposal, and the picture, and the refusal, together. A refusal kept without the thing it refused teaches nobody anything."

"Then I want a line under it. Mine."

"Say it."

Egede said it. It was not the kind of line Egede usually asked for. There was no word in it that was going to end up in a document.

Nakagawa wrote it down exactly. *I wanted to say thank you and there was not a way.*

::: pov aravena

In the morning Egede took the stone back.

Aravena had come out to do the straps. Egede walked from the station door, counting, and stopped, and took out the handset and looked at the picture, and then at the ground. The frost had grown over the place. Egede scraped it back with the side of a boot until there was a patch of bare grit the shape of the stone, a little paler than the rest.

Then Egede knelt, and set the stone down in it.

It was not right the first time. Egede turned it. Looked at the picture. Turned it again, a very little, until the worn face was to the wind, the way it had been for longer than anyone had been counting.

Aravena had seen that done before. Aravena did it every shift, at the junction, kneeling, turning the unit on its base until it sat the way the party set them. And somebody else had done it once, on the silt, to the same unit, and got it right.

Egede stood up and brushed both knees.

"You paced it out before you picked it up," said Aravena. "That first morning. I thought that was just you."

"It was just me."

"You knew you'd be putting it back."

Egede thought about that, honestly, the way Egede thought about a reading that might be wrong.

"I knew what the answer would be. I thought it ought to be asked anyway. Somebody should have to say no to it out loud. Otherwise we've only not done it, and that isn't the same." Egede looked down at the stone. "It's the planet's. It can go on being."

They went down the long passage, all three. Shift seventy. At the junction the imager stayed in its case.

Aravena bent for a stone.

"Not that one," said Egede, from the far wall. "The one behind it. The rough one. That one'll roll."

Aravena looked. Egede was right. Aravena took the rough one, and held it to the front, and went down the third passage with the light low.

Through the squeeze. Onto the silt. Someone was standing on the line.

The stone, beside the last one. The step back. The thing said, going up at the end.

The rock at the edge of the silt, moved. Through the boots.

When Aravena came back, Egede was not standing by the case. Egede was walking the junction floor with a handset, slowly, eyes down, stopping at every loose stone the size of two hands, and turning some of them over with a boot to see whether they would roll.

"What are you doing?"

"Counting the good ones," said Egede. "Somebody should know how many there are."

Aravena knelt to the unit. "Log. Shift seventy. Upper junction. Party of three. Party announced itself at the line. Answered."

The unit hummed, once, low, to say it had heard. There was nothing in the log about a stone from the roof. That was in the file, with the no beside it, and one line underneath.
