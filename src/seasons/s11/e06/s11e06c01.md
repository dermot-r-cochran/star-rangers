::: pov nakagawa

*Shift seventy-five. Upper junction. Party of three. Party announced itself at the line. Answered.*

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade, and by now the way was short. Nakagawa read it that evening at the station, in Aravena's voice, and then read back up the file, because a thing you read every day should be read all together now and then, to see what it has turned into.

Twelve of them. One a shift since the first, each the same two sentences.

Except shift seventy-two.

*Stone dropped at the squeeze. Picked up. Set. Answered, late.*

Nakagawa had asked one thing about that, on the day. "How late?" And Aravena had gone a colour, and said, "Inside the interval. Near the end of it." Nakagawa had asked nothing else. It was all in the ten words. Aravena had written down the dropping, which nobody had seen and nobody would ever have known. That was the part of the entry that mattered to a survey lead.

Egede came in out of the wind with a handset and sat down across the table.

"I've finished the junction."

"Go on."

"Loose stones on the floor. Two hands across, near enough. Rough enough not to roll. I've turned every one." Egede put the handset on the table and turned it round. There was a number on it, and a second number under that, and a third. "That's how many there were. That's how many there are. That's how many shifts we've got, at one a shift, before somebody has to start on the second passage."

Nakagawa read the third number. It was not small. It was not as large as a survey lead would have liked.

"Thank you. That goes in the file."

"There's a second part." Egede took the handset back. "The difference between the first number and the second is twelve. I'd like to know where the twelve are."

"On the line."

"I know they're on the line. In what arrangement?"

Nakagawa did not know. That was a strange thing to find out about your own survey at your own table. Nakagawa turned to the third of the party, who was mending a strap.

"Side by side," said Aravena. "Each one next to the last. Along the line."

"A row," said Egede.

"I suppose. Yes."

Egede sat back.

"Six shifts ago," said Egede, "I put one stone on this table. One. The planet's own. And I was told, and I wrote it down, that a thing left at the line is a thing left." Egede was not angry. Egede was doing the thing Egede did, which was to hold the instrument up to its own reading. "We have left twelve. In a row. We are building a wall, very slowly, on somebody else's doorstep, and nobody has said so."

::: pov nakagawa

It was a fair hit, and Nakagawa took it standing still.

"Say the rest," said Nakagawa. "You have a rest."

"The junction stones are the planet's. Granted. The planet keeps them. But the planet kept them all over the junction floor, where the roof dropped them. It did not keep them in a straight line on the far side of a crack. A stone is the planet's. A row isn't. Somebody *made* that." Egede leaned on the table. "If the station went tomorrow, and somebody came in a hundred years and walked down that passage and through the squeeze, what would they find? A row of carried stones. They would know in one look that people had been there. That is exactly the thing the rule is for."

"Yes," said Nakagawa. "It is."

"So?"

"So you have a proposal. You always have."

Egede had. "One down, one up. Aravena sets today's stone and lifts yesterday's and carries it back to the junction. There is never more than one on the line. The knock's the same. And I get my third number back, because the junction never runs out."

It was tidy. Nakagawa looked for the hole in it and could not find one from the table. That was the trouble with the table. It was on the roof.

"Aravena."

Aravena put the strap down.

"You are the only one of us who has seen them. What has happened to the stones? Since you set them."

"Nothing."

"Say more than that."

Aravena thought.

"They're where I put them. All of them. The first one's at one end, the rough one, from the first morning. Then the second. I put each one next to the last, about a hand off. If one had moved, there'd be a gap, or two together. I'd have had nowhere to put the next." A breath. "There isn't a gap. I'd know. I look at them every time, on the way down to my knees."

"None has been moved."

"Not by a finger."

"That is a fact," said Nakagawa. "It goes in, tonight, once. *Stones set, twelve. Stones found displaced, none.*" And waited, because Aravena had not finished. You could see it.

"They move things," said Aravena. "That's all. They moved the unit. Somebody smaller than me picked it up and carried it down the passage and through that crack and stood it up straight. And the one on the line moves a rock every shift that I couldn't shift with both arms. They know how to pick up a thing and put it somewhere else. It's what they *do*." Aravena looked at the table. "There are twelve stones a hand from that one's feet. If they were in the way, they wouldn't be there."

"That's a reading," said Egede.

"I know it is."

"It is a reading, and it is yours, and it goes in under your name," said Nakagawa. "The not moving is the fact. Keep them apart. They are easy to file together."

::::: scene 3

::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa went out for a while, into the wind, which was where the thinking was done on Fliade. It was dark and there was nothing to see. That helped.

Egede was right about the row. Nakagawa turned that over twice and it stayed right. Twelve stones in a line was a made thing. The party had made it. A survey lead had refused one stone from the plain for less.

And Egede's way out of it was clean. One down, one up.

Nakagawa tried giving the order. Not aloud. Only to see what it looked like. *Lift the last stone.* Aravena on the silt, with the one on the line standing a hand away, bending to take back a stone that had been set down there yesterday, and answered.

That was where it stopped.

Every one of them had been answered. That was in the log twelve times. The party set a stone, and the ground came back through the boots. Nakagawa did not know what that made the stone, to the one who answered it. Nothing, perhaps. A stone. Or it might be that a stone, once answered, was finished with, and anybody could have it. Or it might be the one thing on that silt a visitor must never touch.

The survey did not know. And there was no small way to find out.

Nakagawa went back in.

"The stones stay," said Nakagawa. "We lift none."

"On what ground?" said Egede. Not sharply. Egede wanted it for the file.

"Say the rule again. The end of it."

"Nothing left that cannot be taken back at will."

"At whose will?"

Egede began to answer.

"I wrote half that rule," said Nakagawa. "I meant ours. There was no other. We could carry every one of those stones back up tomorrow. Twelve trips. It is not that we can't. It is that when I picture Aravena doing it, there is somebody standing there, and I do not know what they would take it for. That is not *at will*. Not ours alone." A pause. "The rule was written for a world with nobody at the door. It is a good rule. It has run out, at that one place, and I would sooner write down that it has than pretend it reaches."

"So we go on building the wall."

"We go on knocking. And you look, every shift, as you have been looking. If one of them is ever moved, by anybody, you say so at the junction before you say anything else, and it goes in the log that day, and then we will know something." Nakagawa looked at Egede. "Your objection goes in whole. It is correct. I am not answering it. I am declining to act on it, which is a different thing, and the file should be able to tell them apart."

Egede thought about that, and nodded, slowly, the way Egede did when a point had been lost on better grounds than expected.

"And your number," said Nakagawa. "The third one. Keep it up."

"That was always going to be my job," said Egede.

Aravena had picked the strap up again and was not mending it.

"It's a count," said Aravena. "Isn't it. The row."

"Go on."

"You kept one. When nothing was happening. Sixteen. It was in your column, where nobody could see it but you." Aravena turned the strap over. "This one's just lying there. Anybody who comes to the line can see how many."

Nakagawa had not thought of it that way, and was glad to have been handed it, and was careful with it.

"Anybody can see it," said Nakagawa. "Whether anybody counts it, we don't know. It is there to be counted. That much I will have."

::: pov aravena

Shift seventy-six.

Aravena picked the stone at the junction, with Egede watching from the far wall to see which, and went down the third passage with the light held low.

In the squeeze Aravena held on with all ten fingers. That was since seventy-two. The stone had gone out of both hands in the narrowest part, and gone on ahead, and Aravena had heard it land on the far side and bounce. Twice. It was the worst sound Aravena had ever heard on Fliade. Then out onto the silt with nothing to hold, and the one on the line looking. Then the picking up, and the kneeling, and the thing said, in not much of a voice.

And then nothing, for a long time.

Aravena had stood there and counted the interval and thought: *I have broken it. It was the one thing we had and I dropped it.* The order was to wait the interval and come back, whatever happened. Aravena had been getting ready to come back.

Then the rock, through the boots.

Aravena did not know what had happened in that wait. Nobody on the roof ever would. Something had been thought about, a hand away, and Aravena had been what it was about. That was all there was to know of it.

Out onto the silt now. The light found the feet. Someone was standing on the line.

And beside the feet, the row.

Aravena had looked at it every shift on the way down to kneeling. But only to find the end. Now Aravena looked at all of it. Twelve stones on the pale silt, a hand apart. The rough one from the first morning. The flat one. The one Egede had said would not roll. And the ninth, from seventy-two. Aravena had been sure, that day, that it would always be the one that fell.

It was not possible to tell. It sat in the row like the others. It had been set, and answered.

None of them had moved. Not by a finger.

Aravena knelt, and set the thirteenth beside the twelfth. Stood. Stepped back. Said the thing, going up at the end.

The one on the line bent, and put a hand to the rock, and moved it.

Aravena stood still for it, for about as long as it takes to say a name.

Then back through the crack, slowly. At the junction Aravena knelt to the unit.

"Log. Shift seventy-six. Upper junction. Party of three. Party announced itself at the line. Answered."

The unit hummed, once, low, to say it had heard.

Nakagawa was looking across the junction, and did not ask. Aravena shook their head. None moved.

"Thirteen," said Egede, from the far wall, and took one off a number on a handset, and opened the imager's case.
