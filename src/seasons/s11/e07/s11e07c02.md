::: pov aravena

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there, into a unit that was there too, and read afterwards by someone who was not. This time it is mostly about the reading afterwards.

Aravena picked the stone at the junction. Not the first one. A good one, rough, the size of two hands, the kind that would not roll.

"You're running the junction out of good ones," said Egede, from the far wall, where the imager was still in its case, because nothing came out of its case until the knock was done. That was the order. Egede kept it, and had opinions about it, and kept it.

"There are more."

"I've worked out how many."

"Of course you have," said Aravena, and went down the third passage with the light held low.

The squeeze was no better with practice. Everything is worse with a stone. But Aravena knew where to turn it now, and where to breathe out, and came through onto the silt with most of the same skin.

Someone was standing on the line.

In the open, large, grey against the stone, still. Aravena had stopped expecting to stop expecting it. Every shift, coming out of the crack, there was a moment before the light found the feet when it seemed possible that this would be the time nobody was there, and it never had been. The same one, Aravena thought. You could not be sure. The survey could not tell one adult from another at that range, and had never been at that range before. But it stood the same way, in the same place, and the silt under it had taken the shape.

Aravena knelt, and set the stone down beside the last one, a hand from the feet.

There was a row of them now. Aravena had not meant to make a row. You put a thing at a door where a thing at a door goes, and the next day there is yesterday's, so you put it beside. Nothing was left behind that the planet would keep. The planet already kept them. They had only been moved a little, one a shift, down a passage and through a crack, and laid along the place where the party's feet had stopped.

Aravena stood, and stepped back, and said the thing, and it went up at the end.

The one on the line bent, and put a hand on the rock at the edge of the silt, and moved it.

Through the boots. Every time. Aravena stood still for it, for about as long as it takes to say a name. It had not worn off. Aravena hoped it would not.

Then back through the crack, slowly, not scraping more than there was need to.

At the junction Aravena knelt to the unit and said, "Log," and the shift, and the junction, and party of three. "Party announced itself at the line. Answered." The unit hummed, once, low, to say it had heard.

Egede opened the imager's case.

::::: scene 2

::: pov aravena

Last up the passage, at the end, the way it always was. The unit on Aravena's back. The others ahead, their light going up with them and the talking getting fainter.

At the bottom of the long passage, where the third passage came in, Aravena did not stop.

Aravena noticed it every time: the place, going by. Sixteen shifts of standing there with the light low, asking the dark whether that was all right, and waiting longer than anybody waits. And then one morning with a stone, and after that the feet had simply gone on up, without being told to, and had gone on up every shift since.

Egede had noticed that too. Egede noticed most things eventually.

"You've stopped talking to the dark," Egede had said, some shifts back, not unkindly.

"I haven't stopped. It's got somewhere to go."

That had been the whole of the conversation, and Egede had let it be, which was a kindness.

It was true, as far as it went. The question went down in the morning now, with the stone, to someone who was standing there. But Aravena had asked it, those sixteen times, of the one who carried the unit. Of Four. Who was smaller, and had held it while it woke, and had put it where they would find it.

The one on the line was not Four. It was big.

So the question had somewhere to go, and it was not quite where it had been sent. Aravena did not know whether Four knew about the stones. Whether anybody down there told anybody anything. There was nobody to ask, and no way to ask it, and the survey does not file a thing because one of the party wonders it on the way up a passage.

Aravena went up, toward the cold.

::::: scene 3

::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa read the unit's channels every evening at the station, after the others had eaten, because a thing read the same day is a thing you can still do something about.

And then, that evening, read the other thing. The thing that was not the same day at all.

It had been started weeks ago, the evening of the boulders, when Nakagawa had laid the shifts out in a row and seen that every arrival had one. That had been enough to act on. It had not been finished. A survey lead who acts on a pattern and never goes back to count it has taken somebody's word for it, even if the somebody was Nakagawa.

So: the whole file. From the first month. Every arrival of the party in the upper levels, in order, down the left. And beside each one, where there was one, the entry from before it. *Rock shift, below margin.*

There was one beside every arrival.

Nakagawa went down the column slowly, the way Nakagawa read ground. The first month, when the party had not known what it was hearing and took it for the ground settling. The long middle, when it had a class of its own and nobody opened the class. Every one. The party coming down the long passage, swinging its light, talking. And below, before the light could have been seen from the margin, somebody shifting a stone to say: *someone is here.*

And the party walking on to the junction, and setting the unit down, and getting out the imager.

Sixty-three times.

Nakagawa had a line for that already. *The party has never announced itself.* It was in the file. It was true. It was not, Nakagawa saw now, the whole of what the column said. It said what the party had not done. It did not say what had been done to the party, sixty-three times, a thing done properly and on time, by people the party had its back to.

There is a phrase for that. The party had used it ever since one evening, about that evening, as a story against itself.

Nakagawa wrote it in the survey lead's column, in the plainest words there were. *The party was announced to on every arrival. The party did not look up.*

Then sat with it, in the wind.

Then, because the file was open and the habit of a column is to be read to the bottom, Nakagawa read the right-hand side as well. The other direction. Not the party arriving. Somebody arriving at the party.

There was not much. The adults had never come up past the margin; that was the first thing the survey had learned about them. There was the unit, carried, and the unit had been asleep for most of that and the party had been on the roof. And there was one entry, on one shift, on the thermal channel. A profile. Warm, upright, at the edge of the working light, where the third passage came in.

Eleven minutes.

Nakagawa looked at the space beside it, where the rock shift would be.

Nothing. It had always said nothing. Nakagawa had read that on the evening itself: *no boulder shifted, no sound.* And had lain awake on it, thinking, somebody stood behind us and we did not know.

It read differently in a column.

On the left, sixty-three arrivals with no announcement, by a party that did not know there was such a thing. On the right, one. Nakagawa went and got the other two.

::::: scene 4

::: pov nakagawa

"Look at that," said Nakagawa, and pointed, first at one side and then at the other.

Egede got there quickly. Egede always did.

"He didn't knock."

"Individual Four's approach has no rock shift ahead of it. That is what it says."

"He didn't knock," said Egede, "and every adult on this planet knocks every single time, and we have just spent two years finding that out. So he's the one who doesn't. He's small, he comes up where they don't, he doesn't do the thing they all do." Egede spread both hands, the way Egede did when a word was coming that would not be allowed. "He's *young*. Children don't knock. They barge in. Anybody who has ever met one —"

"We have no way to age a Pandoid," said Nakagawa.

"I know we haven't."

"We have never had one."

"*Smaller*," said Egede. "Fine. The smaller one doesn't knock."

"Did not. Once. That is one approach." Nakagawa let that stand a moment. "It is a good guess. It is the guess I would make. That is the trouble with it. It arrives with the measurement's clothes already on."

Egede made the face that meant the point was taken and would be raised again within the month.

Aravena had not said anything. Aravena was looking at the two sides of the column, the long one and the short one, and Nakagawa watched a thing arrive, the way you can watch a thing arrive in a face. Nakagawa had seen that face go a colour twice, in that room. It did not, this time.

"He came in the way we do," said Aravena.

Nobody said anything.

"That's all. I don't know why. Maybe Egede's right. Maybe he just wanted to see." A breath. "But that's what it looked like from here, isn't it. Somebody walks into where you are and doesn't say so and stands there. It was awful. We've told it ever since. And it was *one time*, and it was the only time any of them ever did to us what we did to them every shift."

"And what did we do about it?" said Nakagawa.

Aravena thought.

"Nothing. We didn't know. We had our backs to him."

"And what did they do about us? Sixty-three times."

"Knocked," said Aravena. "And stood back. And left us the place."

The wind went. Egede looked at the long side of the column for some time, and then, quietly, struck out something in a notebook that was Egede's own and not the survey's, and did not say what.

"Is that a reading?" said Aravena. "What I said."

"It is a reading. It is yours, and it is a fair one, and I am not going to file it." Nakagawa turned the file so that both of them could see the survey lead's column. "This is what goes in. *One approach to the party by one of the people below, unannounced. The party's approaches to the upper levels before the first knock, every one, unannounced.* Side by side. Neither one is a finding. They are two things that happened. If they belong together, somebody will be able to see it without my telling them. If they do not, I have not told anybody that they do."

"That's less than we know," said Egede.

"It is exactly what we know. The rest is what we think. We are entitled to think it. We are entitled to think it at breakfast."

That got Aravena, a little, at the corner of the mouth, and Nakagawa let it.

::::: scene 5

::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa filed it when the others had gone, in the wind, and did not close the file straight away.

Two years on the ground. Nakagawa had thought, for most of the second, that the survey's first real event on this world was eleven minutes long and had happened behind its back. That was how the party told it. *Nobody looked up.* It was a good story, and it had kept them careful, and a survey that is told a story against itself once a week does not get comfortable.

It had not been the first. It had not been anywhere near the first. The first was in the first month, a shift of rock below the margin, taken for the ground settling.

And the eleven minutes was still there, and still a fact, and no less strange. Somebody smaller than the others had come up past the margin without a sound and stood at the edge of the light and gone. The survey did not know why. It was not going to pretend to. But ever since, the party had read that entry as the one time something on Fliade had been done to it. And it was the one time, in the whole file, that somebody below had done what the party did.

Nobody was hurt. Nothing was taken. Nakagawa had thought that, too, the first night.

There were things the file did not have, and Nakagawa made the list, because a list of what you do not have is the most useful page in any file. Whether the one on the line and the one who carried the unit had ever spoken of the party. Whether the small one knew that a stone went down every morning. What the answer meant, beyond *you may stand there*. What Aravena said at the line, which Nakagawa had not asked and would not. None of it was the survey's to know. Some of it might never be.

What the file had was a row of stones on a line, each one answered. And a column, long on one side and short on the other, which the survey had kept for two years without knowing what it was keeping.

Egede would say that was luck. Nakagawa thought it was the only method there was. You write down exactly what happened and not one thing more, every shift, including the shifts when it is nothing. Then one day you know how to read it, and it has been true the whole time.

Eleven minutes is a long time to be looked at. Nakagawa had not slept for about that long, the night of it.

Two years is a long time to be knocked for.

Nakagawa put the light out, and slept, which was the only test the survey had ever trusted. In the morning it looked the same. Aravena went down with a stone.
