::: pov aravena

The supply run's sheet had two columns, DOWN and UP, and Egede filled it in the night before, because Egede filled in anything that had columns.

DOWN was the run's business. Cells. Rations. Empty sample cases, twelve. A lens, to replace the one Egede had changed in the seven weeks and no longer wished to discuss.

UP was the station's. Sample cases, full, nine. Spent cells. And at the bottom, in Egede's small square hand:

*Passengers: 1?*

Aravena read it over Egede's shoulder. In three years Egede had never put a question mark on a sheet. Egede put *under investigation*, which was a different thing: it meant Egede had an answer and was waiting for the instrument to agree.

"She hasn't said," said Egede, without turning round.

"No."

"Her pack's by the door."

"I know."

It had been there since the morning before. One pack, closed and strapped, the way it had come down. Section Lead Ferrant had arrived on a run like this one, with that pack, to read a file. She had read it. She had filed her reading, in her own file, at its grade. Then there had been the shift with two on the line, and she had written one more line in the survey's log. *Direct observation. Reading unchanged.*

And since then, every shift, the handheld had gone through the squeeze on Aravena's chest and come back with the same thing in it. One upright shape. One thermal return. One on the line.

There was nothing left for her to read. A person whose page has been read goes back to where the pages are kept. Aravena knew that, and had got used to someone sitting on the floor in front of the third shelf every evening all the same.

"Nobody's asked her," said Aravena.

"It isn't mine to ask," said Egede. "I only do the sheet."

The run came down a little after that, in the usual wind.

::: pov odile-ferrant

She sat on the floor in front of the third shelf with the handset on her knee, in uniform, with the wind working at the station wall, and tried to write the last entry.

It ought to have been easy. She had written it a dozen times, at a dozen instruments. *Instrument resealed. Reporting band as installed. Log unread from this date.* Then your name, and you stood up.

She got as far as *unread.*

She had said it to the survey lead herself, the first evening. *When I go it goes back to nominal, and nominal will mean what it meant.* She had been rather pleased with it. It was a description, she had said, not a comfort.

It was not even a description. She saw that now, with the word half written.

Take it in order, the way she would make a junior take it.

She could leave the band as it was. Wide. Then the gravimeter would go on telling this station *nominal* every day, and it would be telling three people who had watched her read what was inside the band. They could not read it themselves. They would only know, each time they ticked it, how much room the word had in it. That was not what nominal had meant. Nominal had meant nobody wondered.

Or she could draw the band in close. Then the instrument would say *not nominal* every day, because of the offset, to a planetary survey with nobody who could say what the offset was. An alarm with no reader. On her side of the Corps they had a name for that, and it was noise.

Or the log could go on being read.

There was no fourth thing. She looked for one.

Then she asked herself the other question, plainly, because it was the one that would disqualify her. *Are you staying to see it again?*

An analyst who is waiting sees things. Everyone on her side knew it. You draw a band and you file what falls outside it, and the day you catch yourself hoping the needle will move, you hand the instrument to somebody else. If the answer was yes, she would be on the run in an hour, and right to be.

She was honest about it. She had not thought about the line in days. She had thought about the log. It was read every evening now, by someone who could, and the thing she could not find a form for was making it *unread* again. The best-documented case in her trade was an instrument that had sat for eleven years with nobody reading inside its band. She had been trained on that case. She did not see how to sign her name under the start of another.

So she did not write the entry. She wrote to her own side instead, under seal: that a station under the boundary-proximity requirements carried an instrument and no reader; that she asked to remain as its reader until one was posted, or until the side ruled that a station of this kind does not get one; grounds as stated; nothing to grade.

The relay took it. There would be no answer before the run went. So for this run the choice was hers.

The berth was not. That had to be asked for.

::::: scene 3

::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa was carrying sample cases out to the run when Ferrant came round the side of the station, into the wind, without the pack.

Nakagawa noticed the pack first. Not having it changes how a person walks.

"I need to ask you for something," said Ferrant. "I want to ask it properly. It's your station."

"Ask."

"The spare berth. Past this run."

Nakagawa set the case down, because some things should not be answered while carrying something.

"For how long?"

"I don't know. I've written to my side. Until they send a reader for the third shelf, or tell me a station like this doesn't get one."

"And if they tell you that?"

"Then I reseal it and I go, and the log goes unread under somebody's name and a date. That's a different thing from its going unread because I walked off." She had to raise her voice a little over the wind, and plainly disliked doing it. "I tried to write the handing-back this morning. I can't. I told you nominal would mean what it meant. I'd like to withdraw that. It was wrong when I said it."

Nakagawa had known it was wrong when she said it. Nakagawa had not said so, because it was not the survey's instrument. That was not a lapse, quite. Nakagawa looked at it for a moment anyway, in case it wanted to go in the file with the others.

"There's a question I have to ask," said Nakagawa.

"Whether I'm staying for the line."

"Yes."

"I asked it first. No. If it were yes I'd be on that run, and you should put me on it." Ferrant looked at the cases, not at Nakagawa. "You can't have somebody at that shelf who's hoping. That's the other thing I came to say. I won't come down to the junction again unless you ask me. I went to see where your light stops, and then to watch my own instrument work. I've seen both. A knock is one person and a stone. A junction is the three of you. I'd be a fourth, standing at a door, for no reason I could file. The handheld goes without me. I can read its frames up here."

Nakagawa thought about sleeping on it. It was the only test the survey had ever trusted.

But the run would not wait for a night. And some things look the same in the morning because they have looked the same for weeks.

"The berth's been yours since you broke that seal," said Nakagawa. "I hadn't written it down. I will."

"Thank you."

"I signed that word twice," said Nakagawa. "The manifest comes round again at the turn of the year. I was not looking forward to signing it a third time, knowing what it has room for."

"What will you sign?"

"That depends what Egede ticks. Egede will want to know what class *read* is."

Ferrant very nearly smiled. Then she picked up the case Nakagawa had put down, and carried it out to the run. It was a thing that needed carrying, and she had two hands and was on duty.

::::: scene 4

::: pov aravena

The run does not stay. It has the wind to think of. It gives the station as long as it takes to turn one load round, and then somebody has to sign the sheet.

Egede stood in the lee of the station with the sheet and a pen, and the last line of the UP column, and Ferrant coming back from the run with her hands empty.

Egede waited a moment before speaking. Egede did that now, since the seven weeks. It was like watching somebody check a seal.

"I've got a query on my sheet," said Egede. "Passengers. Is it one, or is it none?"

"None," said Ferrant.

Egede wrote *none.* Then the pen went back to the question mark, to block it out.

"Leave that," said Ferrant.

"It's wrong."

"It wasn't wrong last night. Last night it was the only honest thing on the sheet. Strike it so it can be read, and write the answer beside it. Then anybody who picks that up can see there was a morning when you didn't know." She had said something like it once before, Aravena had heard, about a different file. "Those are the best lines there are. Most people throw them away."

Egede struck it, one thin line. *Passengers: 1? none.* Egede looked at it with deep dislike, and signed.

The run went up into the wind. Aravena watched it go, as always, because it was the only thing on the planet that left.

When Aravena went in, the pack was back in the spare berth, unstrapped. One thing had been taken out of it and put on the shelf above the bunk. It was a second uniform tunic, folded. That was all. It seemed to be how Ferrant unpacked.

::::: scene 5

::: pov aravena

That evening went the way the evenings went.

Nakagawa read the unit's channels at the table. Thermal: the party, and the Anstolik, passing. Imager: nothing at the third passage mouth. Sound: nothing. Then the log, standing up, in the survey lead's hand, with what the handheld had seen in it. One upright shape. One thermal return.

Ferrant sat on the floor at the third shelf with the handset, and read.

"Anything?" said Nakagawa.

"The offset is where it has been since before your first knock. There's nothing else tonight." She said it to the table and not to the handset. "It's in my file, at what it's worth."

Nakagawa nodded, and wrote nothing, because it was not the survey's.

Egede had the next year's manifest form out already. It was not due for weeks. Egede liked to have forms out.

"Third shelf," said Egede. "When this comes round. Do I still tick it nominal?"

Aravena stopped mending the strap.

"What did you check, the last two years, before you ticked it?" said Ferrant.

"That it was powered. That it was logging. That the seal was whole."

"Is it powered?"

"Yes."

"Is it logging?"

"Yes."

"The seal's broken, and you can write who broke it. I'll sign beside that. Then tick it. That's what nominal means on your sheet. It always was. It means the things you can check."

"That isn't what it means in there." Egede pointed the pen at the handset.

"No. In here it means inside a band. That one's mine." Ferrant turned the handset over on her knee. "I told the survey lead the word would go back to meaning what it meant. I had it the wrong way round. Your meaning never went anywhere. It was true every day you ticked it. It was only that there was a second meaning under the seal, and nobody on the roof to keep it. Now there is. You keep yours. I'll keep the other."

Egede thought about a word with two meanings and a keeper for each. It was not the kind of thing Egede enjoyed. Egede wrote something small in the margin of the form anyway.

Later, in the dark, Aravena lay in the berth and listened. The wind. Nakagawa asleep. Egede asleep, with a form. And on the third shelf, very slightly, the hum.

It had hummed every night since the station went up. Aravena had liked the unit's hum from the first shift, the one that says *I heard*, and had never thought about this one at all. It was not that kind of hum. It was not said to anybody.

But it was read now. Every evening, by somebody on the floor in front of it, who was not waiting for it to say anything, and who had stayed.

Aravena knew a little about that. There was someone who stood on a line, every shift, in the open, and was there. For a long time Aravena had thought it was waiting for the party. Lately Aravena thought it was only where it stood.

Aravena did not file that. It was not a reading, and it was not about the third shelf.

In the morning there would be a stone to pick, a good one, the size of two hands.
