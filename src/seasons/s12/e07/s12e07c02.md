::: pov aravena

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there, into a unit that was there too, and read afterwards by someone who was not. Most of what follows is the ordinary part, which the log has always been worst at.

Aravena knocked.

The stone, picked at the junction, the size of two hands. The squeeze, sideways, the stone turned lengthwise and the handheld pressing its hard square into the breastbone. Out onto the silt with the light held low and the breath not back yet.

The one on the line. In the open, still, grey, large.

The light found the feet. Then Aravena's hand took the light to the side, an arm's length along the line, the way it had at every knock since the shift there were two.

Silt. Lit flat, the way silt is when nothing stands on it.

The light came back.

Aravena had never decided to look. The hand did it, the way the knee had once decided where the stone went before Aravena was on the ground. It took about as long as a breath. It was not in any log, because Aravena had not put it there, and the handheld, which pointed where the light pointed, kept a frame of silt every shift and had no opinion about why.

Kneel. Set the stone down, a hand from those feet. Stand. One step back toward the crack. Say the thing, facing the one on the line, going up at the end.

Wait.

The one on the line bent, and put a hand to the rock at the edge of the silt, and moved it. Through the silt, through the boots. Aravena had felt that more than a hundred times now and it had not once felt like the time before.

Back through the crack. Not running.

"Log," said Aravena, kneeling to the unit at the junction. "Shift two-zero-four. Upper junction. Party of three. Knock answered."

The unit hummed, once, low.

Egede was at the far wall with the imager, and looked round, and did not say anything, which was still new enough to notice. For seven weeks of this year Egede had written *artefact* over a thing that was there. Since then Egede had taken to waiting a moment before speaking, as if checking a seal.

"One?" said Nakagawa, reading the ground, not looking up.

"One," said Aravena.

"Good," said Nakagawa, and wrote something, standing.

::::: scene 2

::: pov nakagawa

"I need to ask about your column," said Egede, at the station table, after they had eaten.

It was nearly the turn of the year. Egede had the summaries to begin: every instrument's year, by class and by count, a page an instrument, in Egede's hand, for the survey lead to sign. Egede liked the summaries. A year of anything, laid out in rows, was the nearest the specialist came to peace.

"Ask."

"It has a number in it. It's had one for weeks, going up by one a shift. I have to write the year and there's a count in the file that isn't under any instrument and hasn't got a class."

"No."

"What is it a count of?"

Nakagawa had known the question was coming, and had a short answer and a long one, and gave the long one, because Egede had earned it.

"The evening after there were two on the line," said Nakagawa, "the handheld showed one. One shape, one thermal return. And I wrote, under the entry, *second shape not present.* You were standing where you are sitting."

"I remember."

"Tell me what you said."

Egede looked at the table. It is one thing to be right and another to be asked to say it again.

"I said *not present* is a reading. It says there's a thing, and the thing is somewhere else tonight. We don't know there's a thing. We know there was a frame with two in it and now there's a frame with one." A pause. "I said you'd made it a resident."

"You did. And I struck it, and wrote what the handheld saw, and put the lapse in the file under my name, next to yours." Nakagawa turned the log so that Egede could read the form, though Egede knew it by heart. *Party announced itself at the line. The one who answers was present and answered. Handheld: one upright shape, one thermal return. Nothing said is recorded. The line was not crossed.* "You spent seven weeks writing a thing down as nothing. I spent one evening writing nothing down as a thing. It is the same mistake with its coat on inside out, and you caught mine a good deal faster than I caught yours."

Egede went a little red, and said nothing, and Nakagawa let that stand as the thanks it was.

"So the column," said Egede, after a while.

"Is the number of shifts the log says that. One on the line."

"That's every shift since the first stone. Bar one."

"Yes."

"Then it's a count of ordinary."

"Yes."

Egede took that in with the look of someone handed an instrument with no manufacturer's plate. "I can't put *ordinary* in a summary. There isn't a class."

"It isn't going in a summary. It's mine." Nakagawa had kept a count in that column once before, in the second year: the shifts on which nothing happened, which had been a count of waiting, and had stopped at sixteen when something did. "This isn't that one. It doesn't stop and start again. If there is a shift when the log says something else, the log will say something else, and this will go on being the number of the others."

"Why keep it?"

That was the better question. Egede always got to it eventually.

"Because for a year the log said *knock answered,*" said Nakagawa, "and nobody on this roof thought to write how many were there to answer it. It took two to make anyone write *one.* I would like it written down that we know now, on the shifts when there's nothing to make us."

Egede thought about it for as long as it takes to say a name.

"That's not an instrument's year," said Egede.

"No."

"Good," said Egede, with real relief, and went back to the rows.

::: pov odile-ferrant

Ferrant sat on the floor at the third shelf with the gravimeter's handset on her knee, and when she had finished with it she took up the handheld and went through the day's frames, because it was her instrument and somebody ought to.

There were not many. Silt, feet, grey fur going up into the edge of the lamp. A stretch of silt, an arm's length to the side, empty. Feet again. The stone going down. The step back. The crack.

She had the same frames for every shift since the handheld first went with the stone. They went into her file as frames, one optical, one thermal, at the grade a frame is entitled to. She did not count them. On her side of the Corps you draw a band and you file what comes outside it, and you do not keep a tally of the days it stays in; that way lies an analyst who has started waiting, and a waiting analyst sees things. The survey lead counted. That was the survey lead's trade, and Ferrant had stopped thinking it was the lesser one some weeks back.

"Anything?" said Nakagawa, from the table. It was asked every evening, and it was asked properly, as a question that would take any answer.

"Inside the band," said Ferrant.

Then she heard it, and laughed, once, which she did not often do on duty. It was what the instrument had said to this station for two years.

"I'll say that again in my own voice. The offset is where it has been since before your first knock. There's nothing else tonight. That's the whole of it, and it's in my file at what it's worth."

"Thank you," said Nakagawa, and wrote nothing, because it was not the survey's.

Aravena was on the other berth, mending a strap. Ferrant turned the handheld so the frame of empty silt faced the room.

"You look there," she said. "Every shift. It's in the lamp track."

Aravena did not stop mending. "I know. I don't file it."

"I'm not asking you to file it. I'm asking, because I'm the one person here with a page for the thing, and I want you to have what the page says." She set the handheld down. "It says that isn't a place. Whatever stood there was not standing on your silt. There is nothing in anything I've read that makes that arm's length different tonight from the arm's length on the other side. You could put the stone on it. You could put your boot on it."

"I know," said Aravena.

"You never have. I went back through the frames."

"No."

"Are you going to?"

Aravena pulled the thread through and thought about it, honestly, the way Aravena thought about any real question.

"No."

"Why not?"

"I haven't got a reason." Aravena looked up. "I look because it would feel rude not to. I don't know who to. That's why it isn't in the log. It isn't for the survey. And I'm not putting a stone there for the same reason, which isn't one."

Ferrant considered the nine years of reading the page that stood behind her, all of which said that a thing done for no reason at a boundary site is a thing to write up.

"No," she said. "Nor would I."

"That's not on your page."

"It isn't on anybody's. I checked that too." She picked the handset up again, though she had finished with it. "I said at the junction that I had no word for where it stood. I still haven't. I notice none of us has needed one to know where not to put our feet."

Nobody wrote that down. She was getting better at telling which things those were.

::::: scene 4

::: pov nakagawa

Nakagawa read the unit's channels, as every evening. Thermal: the party, and the Anstolik, passing. Imager: nothing at the third passage mouth. Sound: nothing, in three classes.

Then the log, standing up.

*Party announced itself at the line: one member, one stone of the upper levels, set down on the line and left; handheld carried, pointed low, sound channel off. The one who answers was present on the line, in the open, and answered: a shift of rock at the edge of the silt. Handheld: one upright shape, one thermal return. Nothing said is recorded. The line was not crossed by any party.*

And in the survey lead's own column, yesterday's number, and one.

It was a plain entry. It had taken three years to be able to write it. The first year the party had walked in without knocking and logged the answer as geology. The second, it had learned to knock. This one, it had called a thing a fault, and then learned to write what an instrument saw, and then stood a person on a line beside something nobody could ask and watched the stone go down by the right feet anyway. And at the end of all that, the entry said: one member, one stone, one answer. One on the line.

Nakagawa read the first of those entries again, from the second year, to set the two side by side. *An adult was present at the line, in the open, and did not withdraw.* It had seemed, then, like the whole of the news. It did not say *one.* Nobody had known it was a thing that needed saying. You do not count what you have never seen be otherwise.

What the people below made of the silt beside the one who answers, Nakagawa did not know. Whether they looked at it. Whether they had a word. They had not been told anything and had told nothing, and the log said so, as it had every shift, and would.

Nakagawa had thought of sleeping on it before signing. But there was nothing in it to sleep on. It was ordinary, and counted, and known to be.

Nakagawa signed it, and put out the light, and slept anyway.
