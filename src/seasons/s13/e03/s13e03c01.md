A line is taken standing. The one who carries it stands, and the reciter stands, and nobody else in the hall sits down, which in Knight Pellam's hall meant the Knight, his consort, a cousin, and a dog that had not been told.

Osbern Kell had taken lines in better halls than this one and in worse weather. The hall creaked. It was a hall above Kalenjar that had once had a provost's desk in it, three reigns back, and now had the mark on the floor where the desk had stood, and the house had kept the mark swept. He approved of that. A house that sweeps round what it has lost can generally hold a list.

"Say it," he said.

Pellam's youngest said it. She had been off the boat two days, and looked as if she had not yet got Casimor out of her cloak.

"*A door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time, on the right side past the ninth, and one stands in it.*"

She said it well. Whoever had her on Vellamar had taught her to give a new line slowly, so that the shape held. He waited for the rest.

There was no rest.

"And the name," he said.

"There isn't one."

"The one standing in it."

"Has no name. Nobody carried one. I was there, I'm the one who would have carried it, and there wasn't one to carry." She kept her chin level. "That is the line."

Osbern had been saying the chronicle for fifty-one years, and he held it the way every reciter holds it, by the ends. *Who came first. Who stood at the door. Who went down and was long.* A line of the Castle's part closes on its name and on what the named one does, and the closing is the handle. You pick the line up by it. This one had been handed to him with the handle off.

"Again," he said.

She said it again, and he said it back, and he heard his own voice go on past *stands in it*, reaching for the close, and find nothing there, and make a small foolish sound in his throat. The dog looked at him.

"You stopped late," said Pellam's youngest.

It was not a thing a child of fifteen says to a reciter of the court. It was, however, true, and he had spent fifty-one years teaching children that a line is said as it was given or it is not said.

"I did," he said. "Again."

It took the afternoon. By the end of it he could say the line and stop where she stopped, and stand in the stop without leaning out of it, and it felt like standing on the last step of a stair in the dark and being told, correctly, that there was not another. Knight Pellam had stood the whole time. He was a man with nothing to preside over, hearing his house's name said in the chronicle's voice.

"It is taken," Osbern said. "It is the court's. A new line goes up the steps on Aduine before it goes under the Grove. I will cross at the next calm."

"Up the steps for what?" said the girl.

"To be looked for," he said, and asked for his cloak.

::: pov wren-pellam

Kalenjar was polite about it.

Wren had not expected that. She had expected to be asked. She had come home across the water with the first new line the Castle's part of the chronicle had been given in her lifetime, and she had thought, on the boat, that the harbour would want to hear it from her, and had got ready to be modest.

Nobody asked her for the line. They had it already, from the boat's crew, who had it from the runner, and they said it to each other in the salt-houses in their own voices with the words in a different order. What they said to her was *Pellam's*, with a nod, and *back from Casimor, then*, and once, from a woman gutting on the quay who did not look up, "An open door. In the Castle. Well."

Wren knew what *well* meant in Kalenjar. It was what the harbour said about the size of a fish that had got away.

She stood on the quay with the wind off the anchorage and understood what she had brought home. Nobody had seen a door of the Castle standing open, in all the watch's telling. The court's children went to Krilzat and learned the names and came home and said them. They did not come home with rooms. And this one was a Knight's youngest, fifteen, from a house with a swept mark on its floor, and the only other person who had been in that hall with a lamp was a boy who kept a door on a rock where nothing landed, and he was not going to come to Ostrel to say so. The watch did not cross.

They did not think she was lying. That would have been easier. They thought she was fifteen.

At home it was the other way about, and no better. Her father said the line at table. He said it to the cousin, who had heard it taken, and he said *in Pellam's time* the way he said the one thing their house still had, and each time he came to the end of it he stopped where she had stopped and looked pleased, as if the stop were a flourish.

She lay at night in the hall that creaked in a light wind and listened to it creak. She thought about the old stone, which made no sound at all, and about a room past the notches with the dark closed up in front of it. The keeper's son would have been down there this evening with the lamp. Standing, he would say. Right side, past the ninth. It was the whole of what the watch had, and it was more than Kalenjar would take from her.

The old reciter had gone to Aduine on the second calm, to have her line looked for. She had asked her father what that meant.

"There's a book," her father said. "The Sisterhood's. When the court has a new line the book is asked whether it has the thing too."

"And if it hasn't?"

Knight Pellam thought about it honestly, which was a thing she liked in him.

"Then we have a line," he said, "and the book has not. That has happened. Nobody presses it."

She had been on Krilzat. She had stood in a doorway and watched a boy lower a lamp. It had not once occurred to her that anybody would need to look her up.

::::: scene 3

::: pov osbern-kell

The morning boat from Aduine was a grain boat, and Osbern sat on the render sacks with his cloak drying on him and said the line to himself with the stop in it, to keep the stop from healing over.

He had been up those steps twice before in his life. Once for a crowning and once for a death, and each time he had said a line at the top in the reciting voice and a woman in grey had gone to a shelf. It was a long climb for a short answer. He had never grudged it.

This time the woman who went to the shelf had been a girl with ink on her hands, and she had come back with a page.

He turned that over, on the sacks. The page had been held out. The Abbess had read it where it was, in the novice's hands, so that he could see nobody had touched it, and he had been near enough to see the writing on it, small and upright, row on row, like rain on a still harbour. He had his letters, the court's. Those were not the court's. He could have looked at that page until the tide went out and taken nothing from it.

*The Accounting also has a name.*

Fifty-one years. He had never carried a line with the close off it, and he had spent an afternoon in a creaking hall learning to stand in the place where the close should be, and had rather disliked it. Then the Abbess of Aduine told him, in her dry voice, that the close existed. It was in the room with him, on a sheet of the Abbey's writing an arm's length from his face.

He had been one question from it.

He had not asked. He had known on the steps that he would not, in the way he knew which foot went first on a stair. The court had sent him to ask whether the book had the room. He had asked that, and been answered, and a reciter who comes down with more than he was sent for has stopped being a reciter and become a man with news. He did not put it to himself more finely than that. He was seventy-three and wet, and the boat was coming round into the anchorage.

"Well?" said the steersman, who had carried him over and had been wanting to ask since the Aduine shore. "Has it got her door?"

Osbern considered whether the answer was the court's to hear first. It was, in form. In practice the court was across the water on Vellamar and the steersman was here, and the line had been in every salt-house on the harbour for a week with the words in the wrong order.

"The book has the room," he said. "Right side. It says the room was always there."

The steersman said something reverent about fish.

By the time the mooring was made fast it had gone up the quay ahead of him.

::::: scene 4

::: pov wren-pellam

She knew before he reached the hall. It was in the harbour's face.

She had gone down for bread, and the woman on the quay who had said *well* put her knife down, which Wren had never seen her do, and said, "The book's got your door, Pellam's. Always had, it says." A man mending net looked at her as he would have looked at a steward. Two children followed her as far as the salt-houses and were called back, and the calling was not sharp.

So they believed her.

It went through her like the first warm room after the crossing. They believed her. An old man had climbed some steps and said her line, and now Kalenjar knew that she had stood where she said she had stood and seen what she said she had seen. She walked up from the harbour with the bread under her arm, taller than she had gone down, and she did not ask herself what a book could know about what she had seen. That was not the kind of question the morning was for.

The reciter came up to the hall at midday and would not sit. Her father stood. The cousin stood. The dog, which had learned, stood.

He said it in his own voice and not the other one. It was an answer and not a line.

"The court asked whether the Accounting has the room. The Abbess of Aduine had it looked for, and it was found, in their writing, in their count of the Castle. The right side. A room, and one standing in it. The Abbess says it was always there. The chronicle says it was given. She does not press the difference, and does not expect the court to."

"Always there," said Knight Pellam, slowly. He had the look of a man deciding whether his house's line had just been made larger or smaller.

"And one thing more." The reciter looked at Wren then, and not at her father. "The Accounting has a name for the one standing in it."

The hall creaked.

"What is it?" Wren said.

"I do not have it."

"She wouldn't give it."

"She said that the Abbey does not offer it, because it is the Accounting's and not the court's, and the court did not ask for it. Those were the words."

"But you were *there*." It came out of her at the pitch of someone much younger. "You were in the room. It was on the page. You could have—"

"I could not read the page."

"You could have asked!"

"The court asked whether the book has the room," said the old reciter. He said it without heat, the way he had said *again*. "It has the room."

She waited for the rest. With him there was never any rest. He put his cloak on, and bowed to her father, a reciter's bow and a short one. Then he said to her, because fifty-one years had made him exact, "You stop in the right place. Do not let them teach you to stop late."

He went down to the harbour.

::::: scene 5

::: pov wren-pellam

She thought, for most of that afternoon, that he was a stiff old man who had climbed a great many steps and brought back half of what was at the top.

Her father had settled it for himself by supper. Larger. The book had the room and the court had the giving of it, and both of those were in Pellam's time, and he said the line at table to the cousin and stopped at the end of it and looked pleased.

Wren did not say it at table. She said it later, in the dark, in the hall that creaked, under her breath in the voice that is not a speaking voice, slowly, so the shape would hold.

*A door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time, on the right side past the ninth, and one stands in it.*

She stopped where a name would go.

It was the same stop. She had made it on Krilzat at the gate, for the keeper's son, and in this hall for the reciter, over and over, until he had it. She had never minded it. It had been the true end of what there was. Nobody had the name, so the line had none, and a line that says only what it was given is a line said right. She had carried a great deal less than that across the water and been proud of it.

Not a word had changed. She said it again to be sure.

*And one stands in it.*

And then the stop. On the other side of a strait, on a shelf, in writing she could not read, was the thing that went in it. A woman in grey knew it. A girl with ink on her hands had found it. It had been there, the Abbess said, always. It had been there on the first evening, when she said *Mereth* and the keeper's son lost his voice, and it had been there in the doorway with the lamp going up and down. It was there now. And the old man had stood an arm's length from it and come home dry of it, and all Kalenjar was pleased.

She lay and listened to the hall.

That morning she had carried a line with nothing at the end of it. Tonight she was carrying one with something missing. They were the same words in the same order, said at the same pace with the same stop, and she did not think she would ever again be able to say them as the same line.
