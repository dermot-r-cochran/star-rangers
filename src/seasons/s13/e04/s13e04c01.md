::: pov oriel-fenwick

On the upland terraces a wall is counted in spring, after the storms, and it is counted by walking it.

Oriel had walked her father's since she was six. You go along the top with a tally-stick, and where a stone has shifted you cut the stick, and at the end you carry the stick down to the strip-reeve, who has not walked the wall and takes your word for it because the stick has mud on it. Nobody in Difenland had ever counted a wall from the house. You could, of course. You could sit at the table and say *forty paces, three stones gone*, and it would look the same in the ledger. That was why the stick had to have mud on it.

She thought about the stick most evenings now, with the Castle's count in front of her.

It was a loose page, in the hand, and she was to copy the right side of it before the light went, every day, and get it wrong. She had it nearly by heart. *The ninth door past, right side. A room. One standing in it.* And the name, and two words after the name. She did not say it, even inside. She wrote it where it went.

But the page was a count. It had a left side and a right. Each door was a line, door after door, down a hall on an island Oriel had never seen. It was laid out the way her tally-sticks were laid out, in order, as you come to them.

Somebody had walked it.

That was all. It came to her on the fourth evening, between one line and the next, and she sat with the pen up. A sister had gone down that hall with something to write on. She had stood at the ninth door past on the right, and looked into a room, and seen one standing in it. Then she had come back across two straits to this room and written it in the hand. Oriel wanted to know who she was. She wanted it as she would have wanted to know who built a wall she was mending: not to check the work, only to be able to nod to her.

She asked the Abbess after the evening office, at the top of the steps.

"The count of the Castle. Who made it?"

"The Accounting has the count."

"Yes, Abbess. But somebody walked it. It's laid out as you'd walk it. I'd like to look for her."

The Abbess regarded her for the length of a wave.

"Where would you look?"

"On that shelf. The loose pages. If a sister crossed to Krilzat, it'll say she went. The render boats are written down. The storm-blessings are written down. My father's walls are written down."

"They are." The Abbess seemed to weigh something that was not the request. "After the midday office. What is loose on that shelf, and nothing bound. You will read with your hands behind you."

"Behind me."

"You are a reeve's daughter, Fenwick. I have seen how you look at a page you want something from."

::: pov emma-la-chapelle

The boat from Kalenjar took most of an hour on a morning like this one, grey, with a low swell out of the west that the crew did not consider weather. Emma did her re-deriving on the water. The scriptorium did not care for the reader, and she did not care to make it.

She read what she had written the night before, at the compound, under the lamp.

The log had grown. The instrument on Krilzat spoke to the relay when the relay passed over, and since the first three returns it had gone on reporting, some weeks most evenings and some weeks not at all, in its flat format. *Optical: standing figure, same position, lamp wavelength, 00:03:02. Thermal nil. Sound nil. Absent thereafter.* There were twenty-six of them now. Twenty-six times a lamp had come to that door, in the hand of someone the log did not describe and nobody had named to her, and for as long as the lamp stayed there had been a figure. Then no lamp, and no figure. Not once otherwise.

Last night she had been tired, and pleased, and had written under the three readings: *Consistent across twenty-six occasions.*

She looked at the sentence for some time. The boat went up a swell and down it.

It was true. That was the trouble with it; it was true, and it was sitting under her readings like a prop under a wall, and a reviewer at the Archive's desk would see *twenty-six* and feel the wall was sounder. She had felt it herself, last night. It was a very comfortable number.

What would have to be true for this to be wrong, and did you look. Strozzi asked it of everyone in the same tone.

So she looked. Twenty-six returns. One instrument, on one floor, against one wall, where a keeper had chosen to put it. One door. One kind of lamp, as far as the wavelength could say, and for all she knew one lamp, in one hand. If the first reading was right and this was an optics of the place, the old stone and a lamp and a room shaped to gather, then it would do the same thing twenty-six times. If the second was right, it would do the same thing twenty-six times. The third the same. The number did not lean toward any of them. She had one observation, and somebody on a rock she had never seen had been kind enough to repeat it for her at intervals.

A thing said twenty-six times by one mouth was not twenty-six people saying it. She had known that since her first year at the school, and had still written the sentence.

She struck it. Under her own name, dated, with the reason: *the returns are not independent of one another; the count of them supports nothing and is withdrawn as support.* She left the count itself in the file, as a count.

Then she went through the rest of the evening's work more coldly and found one more. *The figure is present whenever a lamp is.* She did not have *whenever*. She had *whenever this lamp was brought to that door*. She made the sentence that size.

The three readings stayed where they were, at the bottom. Nothing had moved. She had spent the crossing making her file say less. It was most of what the register was for, and it never felt like work to anyone but her.

Aduine came up out of the grey, terraced to the ridge. She put the reader away before the shore.

::::: scene 3

::: pov oriel-fenwick

She looked for a week, with her hands behind her.

The senior corrector laid the pages out. She did it without being asked and without a word, one loose sheet at a time, on the reading slope under the shutters. When Oriel had finished one, the corrector turned it or took it away and laid the next. So the Abbey knew what a novice with her hands behind her was doing, and did not mind it, and was not going to help.

The Castle's shelf was not only the count. There were the storms. Every season had its line for what Krilzat lost: so many lengths of the outer works, a roof, once a whole gate. The entries were as dry as her father's ledger and went back until the ink was the colour of the sea. There was the watch-post's keeping, set down as *kept*, year on year, that one word, the same word the hand used of a wall. And there were the two sides of the hall, door by door.

She read the left side, which she had not been set to copy. She read every line of the right. Each door had its name, and its two or three words for what the one there did, and she read them without trying to hold them. They were not hers to hold yet, and there were a great many.

No sister crossed.

She looked for it every way she could think of. A boat. A blessing given on Casimor. A sister's death away from the house. A render carried to the watch. There was none of it. The Abbey sent grain to Vellamar every year and wrote down the weight. It had sent nobody to Krilzat, in any line she was shown, ever.

And yet the doors were in order, as you come to them.

On the sixth day she understood that she had been looking for the wrong sort of thing. She had been looking for a line that said *Sister so-and-so went*. She stood at the reading slope with her fingers laced behind her back until they ached. She went back in her head over every page she had been shown that week, and over every practice sheet since her first morning. The island page. The count. Her father's walls.

None of them said who.

The Accounting said who kept his walls. It said who was crowned, and who died, and whose strip lay under which catchment. It was full of names. But there was no name anywhere for the one writing. No line began *I*. No line ended with a mark that meant *this was mine*. Generations of women had sat in this room and written what was so, and not one of them had put down which of them she was.

She had known that. It was the first thing the Abbess had told her. *The correctors cannot tell them apart.* She had taken it for a thing about the shapes of letters.

"That's the last of the loose," said the corrector. It was the longest thing she had ever said to her.

::::: scene 4

::: pov emma-la-chapelle

The novice was on the steps before her, which was new. She was standing halfway down with her arms folded, looking at the tide as if it had kept something back.

Emma sat, as she always sat, a few steps above the catchment stones with the reader shut on her knee. She had watched the girl all week at the shelf she herself had never been let near, with her hands clasped behind her like a child in a glassmaker's. She had not written it down. It was not a reading.

"That looked uncomfortable," she said. "The hands."

"The Abbess says I look at a page as if I'd pick it up and shake it." The novice did not turn round. "Can I ask you something about how you do it? Not what you think. How you do it."

"Go on."

"When you write down what you're told. Do you write who told you?"

Emma had expected the fifth name again, or the room. She took a moment with this one.

"Always," she said. "Who, and the day, and the words as near as I can get them. If I don't have a name, I put what I saw of them. *A steward, at the harbour. A corrector, who has not told me her name.*" She turned the reader over. "It's the first thing they teach us. Before any of the language."

"Why?"

"Because a line with nobody under it can't be told from a guess. If I write *the word means keep*, and don't say who said so, then the next person to open the file can't tell whether a reeve told me, or I worked it out, or I wanted it to be true. So they can't weigh it. They can only believe it or not."

The novice turned round then.

"Ours doesn't," she said. "The Accounting. Not anywhere. I've been a week looking for one sister, and she isn't in it, and then I saw that none of them are. It's every line there is, and not one of them says who."

Emma said nothing. She had been reading those pages for three years. Nearly the first thing she had written about them, at a low band, was that the Accounting carried no scribal attribution at any stratum. She had filed it as a feature of the text. She had never once stood in front of somebody who had just found it out as a fact about her own life.

There was a line in her own file that was the same, and she thought of it now and did not say it. *Placed.* The log's first word. Somebody had carried the delegation's one instrument down a hall and chosen a wall, and the court had said *the keeper decides*, and that was all she had of whoever it was. She did not know their name or their age. She could not say whether it was the same hand that brought the lamp. Every return in her file depended on a person the file could not name.

"So how do you know whether to believe it?" the novice said. "If it were yours."

"I can't tell you that."

"That isn't an answer."

"You said that to me on your first day." Emma nearly smiled. "It still isn't one. I can tell you what I'd be deciding, if it were mine. It wouldn't be whether to believe a line. I couldn't weigh a line. It would be whether to believe the book."

The novice thought about that. Below them the tide came on over the stones.

Emma opened the reader, because the question was worth keeping, and wrote it as given. *A novice, on the steps: when you write down what you're told, do you write who told you.*

"You've put *a novice*."

"I haven't been told your name."

"The Abbess says it down the length of the room six times a day."

"To you. I've heard it. I'd have to put it down as heard, and then it's a different kind of line." She held the pen where it was. "You can tell me, and I'll write it."

The novice opened her mouth, and Emma saw her stop with it open.

"No," she said slowly. "Leave it. A novice."

She went up the steps to the office. Emma did not know what had been decided in front of her. She wrote the date.

::::: scene 5

::: pov oriel-fenwick

At the evening office she stood tideward with the others and said the words she had. The tide came in at its own pace.

The Abbess was at the top of the steps afterwards. Oriel had half known she would be.

"You have finished the loose pages."

"Yes, Abbess."

"And?"

"She isn't there. Nobody crossed. Not that it says." Oriel heard herself going at it straight, and let herself. "But it isn't only her. It doesn't say who wrote any of it. I looked for one sister for six days and then I saw I couldn't have found any of them."

"No."

"Did she cross? The one who counted?"

"The Accounting does not say."

"Do *you* know?"

"I know what the Accounting states," said the Abbess. "It states the count. I have never been told more than you have read this week, and I have kept the book for longer than you have been alive." She looked out at the water. "I looked, at your age. Most of us do, who are set to that page. I gave it a season. I was not as quick as you, and nobody made me put my hands behind me."

Oriel had not thought of the Abbess as having had hands to put anywhere.

"What did you do after?"

"I was let near the book, in time. I wrote my first line in it. And the next morning I went and looked for it, to see my own work, and I could not find which it was." The Abbess said it as she said everything. "I knew the day. I knew the page. I stood there some while. Then the corrector of that time asked me what I had lost, and I found I could not tell her without saying something a sister does not say."

She went in to the book.

Oriel sat down at the practice desk with the shutters open. The light was going. She ruled the column and left it empty, and wrote the ninth door past, and the room, and one standing in it, and the name.

She had been getting it wrong since her first morning. She had thought of that as her letters not being good enough. She had thought the hand was a height, and that she was under it.

She looked at her line beside the loose page. She did not know what was wrong with it; the corrector never said. But she thought she knew now what kind of thing was wrong with it. Anyone in this room could have looked at that sheet and said, *that is Fenwick's*.

The woman who walked the hall could not be found. She had mud on her stick, or she had none, and the page did not say. She had come to this room and written what was so, and she had written it so well that there was no one there. That was the hand. Oriel had spent six days hunting through the Accounting for the one sister who had done, perfectly, the thing Oriel had come up the steps to learn.

The corrector came and looked at the sheet and went away.

Oriel dipped the pen. It would be the same tomorrow. She found she did not mind knowing how long it might take; it was only strange, now, to know what would be gone when she got there.
