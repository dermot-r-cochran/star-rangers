::: pov bram-tollick

Four lengths. That was what Krilzat lost, and Ostrel would be saying the number in the salt-houses by the next calm, shaking its head, since four was a good deal.

The storm came in on the second night of a blow that had already been a blow, and took the outer works on the seaward corner as you would take the crust off a loaf. Bram heard it go. Everybody in the post heard it go. Then the sea was in the lower yard and in the store, and by the time his father had the store door shored, the oil jars on the bottom rack were under a hand's depth of salt water with their bungs started.

In the morning the wind had dropped to what the watch called wind. His mother set the cup out on the table as she did every morning, and he took it, and was greeted. Then they went out to look.

The keep was as it had been. It always was. The sea had come over a wall that three keepers had thickened, and broken it, and had run up against the old stone and gone round. The grey went up into the weather without a streak on it, dead upright. Bram looked at it the way he looked at it perhaps twice a year, when something reminded him to.

His father looked at the wall. Then he went into the store and stayed a while, and came out and said the sentence.

"Two jars."

Bram waited. This season, sometimes, there was more.

"The wall wants light at both ends of the day until it's closed. The hours want a lamp. That is the oil." His father wiped his hands on his coat, though they were dry. "Nothing for the right side until a boat. That one has no hour."

It was not said unkindly, and it was not said as a question. The watch keeps the door, and the hours, because the hours are when the Castle's people go by and somebody ought to be there. A keeper does not have to go down the right side past the notches every evening and hold a lamp up in a doorway. No keeper before him ever had. Bram had been doing it since the first calm, and nobody had told him to or not to. Now there was a reason not to. It was a good one.

"You have the door," his father said. "And the hours. We'll be on the wall."

They were on the wall six days.

::: pov wren-pellam

The court's reciters took a line sitting down, which was the first thing Wren did not like about Vellamar.

She had crossed on a calm to give it to them, as a house's youngest does, and the calm had shut behind her, and now there was a blow on that people said was taking the wall off Krilzat. She was in a long room off the garden quarter with the shutters barred and nine old people on benches. Her own reciter was among them, the one who had taught her the first names and had never seen the Castle. That one gave her a nod with nothing in it to say whether she was doing well.

She stood. She said it slowly, so the shape would hold.

"*A door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time, on the right side past the ninth, and one stands in it.*"

They said it back, one at a time, in the voice that is not a speaking voice. Every one of them went on past the end. Not far. A breath, a half-sound, the lean of somebody reaching for a rail. Nine reciters of the court, and each of them stopped late.

Then they asked. That was the second thing. Old Kell on Ostrel had asked once, and had then put his question away for good. These asked one after another down the bench, as if the one before might have had the question wrong.

"And who stands in it?"

"I have no name for it."

"The keeper gave none?"

"The watch doesn't keep names."

"Then what is it called, there?"

"It isn't. It stands."

She had answered that on Krilzat, at the gate, and in her father's hall. *There isn't one.* She had said it to old Kell without a tremor, because it was so. She noticed that she did not say it now. She said *I have no name for it*, nine times, and each time she heard the difference. Nobody else in the room could have. There was one. It was on Aduine, on a shelf. She was a reciter's pupil and would not say a thing that was not so, even to make a stop sound clean.

Outside, the wind leaned on the shutters. Through the gap in them she could see the edge of the Grove, the great fig with its hundred trunks, and a gardener under it gathering blown scarlet off the processional walk into a basket.

That was where she would say it. Some year, under that tree, to the whole Kingdom, with the stop in it.

"Again," said her own reciter, mildly. "And do not look at us when you stop. It is not our stop."

::::: scene 3

::: pov bram-tollick

He did the hours alone, with a lamp trimmed as low as it would burn.

Morning: the one that goes down, right side, all the way to where the lamp stops. Midday: the one at the second door, for as long as it takes to bar the gate. They did not mind the low lamp. They were made of what there was, and there was less. They went by thinner and went by the same.

It was at the evening hour, on the first day, that he did the thing he had been not doing since the first calm.

It came up the hall on the left side at a walking pace. He had watched it four thousand times with no word for it. Since Pellam's girl he had watched it some sixty times more, with a word for it and nowhere to put the word. The watch does not do names. His father had never once asked what the court's child had told him, and Bram had not offered it. It was not that it was secret. He had only not been able to think what his father would do with it.

But it was in him, like a stone in a boot. Every evening the one on the left came up the hall and he thought *Aldous*, and was annoyed, as he would have been at a tune.

There was nobody behind him. His father was on the wall.

"Aldous," Bram said. Not loud.

It walked as it always walked. It came to the fourth door and turned as if the door were open, and went where the door was, and was not there.

He let his breath out. Nothing had happened. He had not expected anything to happen. He stood there with his ears hot all the same, like someone who has waved at a boat that was not his. They never answer. He had known that longer than he had known his letters. He had not known, until just then, that some part of him had been saving the name up in case it was the knock they were waiting for.

It was not a knock. It was a word she had brought from Vellamar, and it worked when she said it, in a line, in the voice. Said by him, it was a sound he made in a hall.

He thought he would not say it again. It was hers. She had given him a look at it.

Then he barred the door, because it was the hour for that, and put the lamp out to save the oil. He stood in the dark of the passage and thought about the right side.

::::: scene 4

::: pov wren-pellam

She stayed eleven days on Vellamar with the weather shut, and said the Castle's part every morning in the long room, and walked in the garden quarter every afternoon because nobody had told her not to.

Everything on Vellamar leaned. The palms along the walks leaned, and the court's own roofs, and the gardeners leaned without knowing it. Only the fig did not seem to. It had put down so many trunks that it had nowhere left to lean to. She stood under it in the rain-light and thought that it was the nearest thing on four islands to the Castle, and that nobody at court would thank her for saying so.

A boy came and stood by her on the third afternoon. He was a gardener's boy, her age, with a basket of the scarlet blooms and a face the weather had been at. He had heard. All of the court quarter had heard.

"You're the one with the door," he said.

"Pellam's. Yes."

"What does it do? The one in it."

He was the first person on Vellamar to ask her that and not *who*.

"It stands," she said.

"That all?"

It was not all. She had it ready behind her teeth. *It's the colour of the lamp, and there's more of it than the lamp has. Nothing under it. It faces the door. You lower the light and the room is empty, and raise it and it hasn't moved. The others all go somewhere. They have ways. The one called Aldous goes up the left and in at the fourth door, every evening.*

All of it was true, and it was not the chronicle. The chronicle said *who came first*. It did not say *left side, fourth door*. There was no line in it anywhere for the way a figure walks. That was the watch's. It lived on a rock in the mouth of a boy who did not cross water, and she had it folded inside every name now. When she said them in the long room, the nine old people on the benches heard the names and nothing else.

"That's all the line says," she said.

The gardener's boy looked at her as if he knew a short weight when he was handed one. Then he gave her one of the blooms out of the basket, since it was blown and no use for the walks, and went on.

She held it. It was as big as her fist, and wet.

He would be down there now, she thought. It was getting on for evening. The keeper's son would have barred the door after the hour and gone down the right side with the lamp, past his grandmother, to the open door. He would raise the lamp. *Standing.* Somebody was looking at it, at least. The room had been there always, the book said, and the dark in it. Now a boy came every evening and held a light up for as long as it takes to bar a gate.

She found that she had been leaning on that all week.

::::: scene 5

::: pov bram-tollick

You do not need a lamp to walk the right side. He had not known that until he tried it.

The old stone is the same underfoot all the way down, smooth, with the slope so slight that you feel it in the backs of your legs and not your feet. Nothing is ever left in the hall. He put his right hand on the wall and walked. The doors went by under his fingers, each one a seam and a flat and a seam. He counted them, the first and the fourth and the seventh and the ninth, and the names came up at their doors whether he wanted them or not. Past the ninth he counted without them.

It was dark as it is behind your own eyes. There was no wind. He could hear his coat.

Then his hand went into nothing, and that was the door.

He stopped on the sill. He knew where the strangers' case was: to the left, inside, against the wall, a step and a half away. He did not go in. He stood where he stood every evening, and faced where he always faced, and did not lift anything, because he had nothing to lift.

Nothing.

He had known there would be nothing. *Bring no lamp and you will see nothing* was the first thing his father taught him. He was not sure why he had walked this far to have it shown to him. He stood for as long as it takes to bar the gate. Then as long again. The dark did not get any more like a room.

The others had hours. If he slept through the morning, the one that goes down went down all the same, or the watch had always supposed so, and he had only missed it. You could miss it. That was what an hour was.

This one had none. In all the season it had stood when he came and held a lamp up, and at no other time that anybody could speak for. Morning or midnight, it was all one to it. Whatever *when* it had was the keeper's coming. He had been thinking of the evenings as his habit. Standing there, he thought it might be nearer the other way about, and that for as long as the oil was short there was not a keeper in the doorway but only a boy in the dark.

He could not say whether it stood there now. He could not say it did not. The watch keeps which way they go, and he could not keep a way he had not seen. He had nothing for this evening, not even *still*.

He said nothing into the room. He had done his talking for the season.

He went up by the left hand, and did it again the next evening, and the four after. He could not have told his father why, and his father, mortaring by lamplight at the seaward corner, did not ask where he had been without a light.

The calm came on the seventh day, and a boat round to the lee side with it, and oil. That evening he trimmed the lamp full and went down the right side in its little walking room of light. He was past his grandmother before he caught it.

He was not wondering whether the door would be shut.

He had wondered every evening, before the storm. It was a door nobody had seen open in all the watch's telling, and each evening some part of him had come down braced to find it flush and seamed like the rest, and the season's one strange thing taken back. Six evenings of putting his hand into that gap in the dark had worn the wondering off. It was open. That was only how that door was.

He raised the lamp.

Standing. Where it had been. The lamp's colour, and more of it, and no shadow. It faced the door, and him in it.

It told him nothing about the six evenings. He had not thought it would. It had no hour, and he supposed, holding the light up for as long as it takes to bar the gate, that it had whichever hour he brought.

In the morning his father stood back from the seaward corner and looked along it for where it would fail.

"Wall's up," his father said.
