::: pov oriel-fenwick

The girl came up the terrace steps behind the Ranger, off the same boat, and not with her. Oriel saw that from the practice desk, through the open shutters: the Ranger climbing the way she always climbed, at the pace of someone counting something, and ten steps below her a girl in a cloak too heavy for the end of the season, who stopped at the top and waited there until the first office was over, which was right.

She was not a reciter. Reciters are old. She was Oriel's age or a year under, and she stood in the wind off the water as if it were not worth mentioning, which nobody from the terraces did, and when the office was done and the Abbess came out with the book that was not the Accounting, the girl said who she was in the form the court uses when the room requires it.

"Wren, of Knight Pellam."

Oriel's pen stopped. She had been carrying that name about every evening since the reciter came. It was not in the Accounting's entry, which had no Pellam in it and never would. It was in her head, beside the entry, where the Abbess had taught her not to put things: *a door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time.* This was the girl who had carried the one line across the water. Oriel had imagined her taller.

"You are not the court," said the Abbess.

"No, Abbess."

"The court sent a reciter, and the reciter asked what the court wanted asked, and was answered. So you have come up these steps with a question the court did not send."

"The reciter told me what you said. That the book has the room. That it was always there." The girl had a voice that knew how to hold a line, and was holding this one. "And that it has a name, and the Abbey does not offer it."

"The Abbey does not offer," said the Abbess. "It answers. Those are different things, and the court knows the difference, which is why the court did not ask. The Accounting is read to whoever asks what it states. It has been read to the delegation. It will be read to a Knight's youngest who has crossed in the last week of a storm season to hear it. Ask, and Fenwick will read you the line. She has it by heart, if not yet by hand."

Oriel had not known that she had it by heart until it was said in front of somebody.

The girl opened her mouth. Everyone in the long room could see the question in it, whole, brought across two islands and up the steps. Then she closed it again.

"May I see where it is kept, first?" she said.

The Abbess looked at her for as long as it takes to finish a line.

"Fenwick," she said. "The practice desk. Not the shelf."

::::: scene 2

::: pov wren-pellam

Everything in the room was written down.

Wren had grown up in a house that kept its part of the Kingdom by saying it. Pellam had a hall and a hearth and a reciter who came across from Vellamar when there was a child to teach, and what the house knew, it knew aloud. The only writing she had ever been made to care about was the letter her father sent in the form the court requires and nobody reads. She had her letters. They were not these.

The novice's desk faced the sea with the shutters open on it, which no room on Ostrel would have stood for. On the desk was a sheet, and on the sheet the same few lines were written out again and again from the top to the bottom, small and upright and even, each set a little less wrong than the one above it, as far as Wren could judge, which was not far. She could not read a word. She could see that it was one thing said over and over, the way she had said *Aldous, who came first* on Vellamar until the reciter stopped wincing.

"How many times?" she said.

"Every evening since your line came." The Abbess called the novice Fenwick. She had ink to the second knuckle and a terrace farmer's shoulders, and she looked at Wren the way the keeper's son had looked at her at the gate, as something the weather had brought. "It's still wrong. The corrector comes and looks at it and goes away."

"What's wrong with it?"

"I don't know. She doesn't say. If she said, I'd be copying what she told me, and not the hand."

Wren looked down the sheet. In every set of lines, at the same place, near the end, there was one short word and then two more after it. She did not need the letters to know what that was. It was the only one of the Castle's people she had ever stood in front of without a name, and here was the name, more times than she could count at a look, under her hand, in a girl's practice ink, and it might as well have been cut in the old stone for all she could take from it.

She would have to ask. That was the whole of it. The book did not offer.

Beside the lines ran a column, ruled from top to bottom, with nothing in it.

"What goes there?"

"Nothing. I rule it and leave it empty. Every hand has."

"Then what's it for?"

"Answered prayers," the novice said, the way the keeper's son said *the old stone*, flat, a thing that was so.

Wren looked at the empty column for a while. Below the terrace the tide was doing something patient to the stones.

"We have one of those," she said. "A line. It comes after the doors and their names. *And the rooms are more than the names.* Every reciter says it fastest. Mine did. I do."

"Why fastest?"

"Nobody likes it. It's the chronicle saying where it stops."

The novice turned her pen over, and did not write anything, and Wren saw that she would have liked to.

::::: scene 3

::: pov oriel-fenwick

Oriel had been told in her first week that a novice does not ask a guest of the Abbey anything the guest has not begun. She weighed that against the sheet in front of her and found it the lighter of the two.

"You saw it," she said. "The one in the room."

"Yes."

"I've written *one standing in it* every evening, and I've never seen one. Nobody here has. The Accounting names them, and it doesn't say that any sister ever crossed to Krilzat, and I've looked." She had not meant to say that she had looked. "What is it, to see?"

Pellam's girl did not answer at once. She put her hands flat on the bench on either side of her, as if the bench might lean.

"You take a lamp down," she said. "Past where the names stop. It's a long way, and the doors are all shut, and all the same. Then one isn't. The room's dark. You lift the lamp and someone is standing there. It's the colour of the lamp. Not lit by it. Made of it, and more of it than the lamp has. There's no shadow under it. It faces the door." She stopped, and went on more slowly. "The keeper's son lowered the lamp and the room was empty. He raised it and there it was, in the same place, as if it hadn't been anywhere. It doesn't answer. I don't think it looks at you. The others walk, all of them, they have ways. This one doesn't go anywhere."

Oriel found that she had the pen in her hand and nothing to do with it. There was no column for this. The Accounting gave a room, and one standing in it, and a name, and two words more, and she had copied all of that until her hand ached, as a list, the way you copy a list, which is without having to understand it. Now there was a lamp in it.

And the name was in her mouth.

It was simply there, the way the next word of an office is there once you know the office. This girl had stood in the doorway. She had carried a line across the water with a hole at the end of it, and the thing that went in the hole was three words long, and Oriel had it by heart, and nobody had told her that she might not say it. The Abbess had said that the Abbey did not offer. Oriel was not sure, sitting there, whether a novice with ink on her knuckles was the Abbey.

She thought, on the whole, that she was. That was what the hand was.

"If I read it to you," she said instead, "what would you do with it?"

"Carry it." There was no pause at all. "That's what I'm for. A house's youngest carries what she's given to the reciters, and they say it under the Grove."

"Then it would be in both."

"Yes."

"In the same words."

"Yes," said Pellam's girl, and this time there was a pause after it. She looked at the sheet, at the place near the end of each set of lines, and then away from it, out through the shutters, at the water.

Down the room the Ranger had not once looked up from the oldest shelf, which Oriel had learned was how she listened.

::::: scene 4

::: pov wren-pellam

The novices went out to stand at the back of the terrace for the midday office. Wren had no place in it, and went and sat on the steps, and found one of the strangers there already.

They had crossed on the same boat. Wren had spent the crossing not looking at her and had seen a good deal: a deep green-brown coat, plain, with a small barred tab at the collar, worn the way the watch wears its wool, as the thing you are in when you are working. She was younger than Wren had thought the strangers would be. On her knee was a flat grey thing with no mark on it that the Kingdom made, and Wren knew the grey. She had followed a case of it down the long hall.

"The thing the keeper's son put in the room," Wren said. "It's yours."

"The delegation's. Yes." The stranger spoke the Kingdom's tongue slowly and exactly, like someone walking along the top of a terrace wall. "You carried the line."

"It listens, the court said. And keeps what it hears."

"That's the court's word for it. It's a fair one."

"What did it hear?"

The stranger did not answer straight away. She looked at the grey thing on her knee, and Wren had the impression of a person going down a list of the questions she was allowed to answer and finding, to her own surprise, that this one was on it.

"Nothing," she said. "There wasn't a sound. It looks, as well as listens. It saw someone standing in the room for as long as there was a lamp in the room, and nobody when there wasn't. Three times, in what I've been sent so far. That is all it has. I can tell you because you were there, and I'm telling you nothing you didn't carry out of that hall yourself."

"That's what we saw," said Wren.

"I know it's what you saw. I didn't know it until you said so." The stranger opened the grey thing. "May I write that down? Under your name, as you said it. Not beside the other. I don't put things beside each other."

"Why not?"

"Because then it looks as if one of them told the other, and neither did. The keeper put it where he decided. Nobody told it what was in the room, and nobody told you what it would keep. If I write them down apart, they stay two." She wrote. It did not take long. "It's the best thing I've had about that room all year, and I am not going to do anything with it."

Behind them the Abbess's voice went on over the water, and the tide came in under it at the pace it had already chosen.

*Neither did.* Wren thought about the old reciter, wet through at the top of these steps, saying her line in the voice that is not a speaking voice and asking whether the book had the room. The book had. He had come down again with that, and it had gone round Ostrel in a day, and it had been worth his crossing, because the book had not got the room from him. It had been holding it since before anyone could say, in a hand that nobody had ever told what the court would one day carry up the steps.

And if she took the name home. Under the Grove, at the next crowning, she would say *and one stands in it*, and then three words more, and they would be the book's words in the court's voice. Then some later year a line would be new, and a reciter would climb here to have it checked, and one day the thing checked would be this, and the book would be asked about something it had told the court itself. It would say yes. The yes would be worth what it is worth when you ask a person whether she agrees with herself.

She had come to fill a hole at the end of a line. She had not once asked herself what the hole was keeping apart.

"You didn't ask her," the stranger said. She had closed the grey thing. "This morning. I heard you not ask."

"Did you write that down?"

"No. Nobody told it to me."

::::: scene 5

::: pov oriel-fenwick

At the evening office Pellam's girl stood at the back with the novices, because there was nowhere else on the terrace to stand, and faced the water with them, and did not know the words and did not pretend to. Oriel, who had most of them now, said them, and the tide came in over the catchment stones, and nothing anyone said made it come.

Afterwards the Abbess was at the top of the steps, with the boat waiting below and the Ranger already on it.

"You have had the day, Pellam," the Abbess said. "Ask."

"The court did not ask." The girl had her cloak on and her bag on her shoulder. "I thought I had come on my own account. I find I haven't one. If it's read to me I carry it, that's what a house's youngest is, and then it's the court that has it. So I'm not asking."

"That is a longer answer than the court gave."

"The court has had more practice."

The Abbess did not smile. Oriel had been watching her for a season and could say with some confidence that she did not.

"And under the Grove?"

"What I was given. A door, and one standing in it. Then I stop."

"The Kingdom will hear you stop."

"Yes, Abbess."

The Abbess nodded once, the way she nodded at a line the correctors had passed, and stood aside from the steps.

Pellam's girl turned to Oriel before she went down. She seemed to be looking for the form the court uses and not finding one for a novice.

"Thank you for not saying it," she said.

"Thank you for the lamp."

"It's the keeper's son's lamp." She went down the steps to the boat. She did not look back, and Oriel watched her as far as the catchment stones.

"You had it in your mouth before midday," the Abbess said.

"Yes, Abbess."

"And kept it there."

"She hadn't asked."

"No. She had not." The Abbess looked at the water for the length of a wave. "The page, Fenwick. Before the light goes. Leave the column empty."

Oriel went in and sat down at the practice desk with the shutters open and the light going grey. She wrote the ninth door past, and the room, and *one standing in it*, and for the first time there was a lamp held up in the words, and no shadow under them. She wrote the name where it went, as it was written, which was the only place she had been within a breath of not putting it. She ruled the column.

The corrector came and looked at the sheet and went away. It was still wrong.

The column was not. She had got the column right on her first morning and every day since, and this was the first evening she knew what it was she had been getting right.
