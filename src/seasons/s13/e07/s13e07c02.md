The last boat of a storm season comes round to the lee side of Casimor when the weather has stopped meaning it, and what it brings is salt, lamp oil, and whatever Ostrel thinks the watch has run out of. Bram went up on the wall to count the load coming across the rock, since the counting was his now, and counted one more than there should have been, walking.

She had the wind behind her and a bag that was mostly cloak.

"Pellam's," said his father, from the gate, and then, because it had been a season for it, a second sentence. "They learn the names and they go home."

"She went in the middle," said Bram. "With the line. The names weren't learned."

His father considered that the way he considered a repair somebody else had made to the wall, by looking for where it would fail, and did not find the place. He went to see to the oil.

So Bram had the bar of the door in his hands when she came up the last of the path, though there was no storm coming and the bar had no business being anywhere but its brackets. She looked at the Castle first. They all did. Then she looked past him, through the gate, at the door-post, and looked at that for longer.

"It still doesn't lean," she said.

"No."

"I've been on Aduine. At the Abbey. There's a book there."

The watch knew that there was a book the way it knew that there was a court: across water, and somebody else's work.

"The book has the room," she said. "Right side. It's had it since before anyone can say. It says the room was always there."

Bram thought about his grandmother's notch, and the notches above hers, and every keeper who had gone down the right side with a lamp and come back up and cut nothing for a door standing open, because they had seen none.

"It wasn't open," he said.

"The book doesn't say open. It says there. That's as the reciter had it; I can't read the thing." She put the bag down on the path. "And it has a name for the one standing."

::::: scene 2

::: pov wren-pellam

He went still. She had seen him do it before, in the long hall on her first evening, when she said *Mereth, who stood at the door* and he said *midday* and then had to go and find his voice.

"What is it?" he said.

"I don't have it."

"They wouldn't say."

"They'd have said. The Abbess told me to ask and it would be read to me. There's a novice there who writes it out every evening, the whole line, to learn the hand. She had a sheet of it on her desk, more times than I could count, and I sat beside it from the first office to the last. I can't read their hand. I was one question off it all day."

"And?"

"I didn't ask."

Bram looked at her the way the watch looked at the court's children on the first evening, when they found out that it did not look the same from inside. She had worked out on two boats how to say this, and it had come out differently on each.

"If I'd asked, I'd have had to carry it," she said. "I can't hear a name for one of them and not carry it, that's the thing I am. Then it's in the chronicle. The book's name, said under the Grove as if it had been given to us. And the court sends a reciter up to that Abbey whenever a line is new, to find out whether the book has it too. One day the line would be this one. He'd be climbing all those steps to find out whether the book agrees with the book."

Bram said nothing for a time. The wind went over the wall and the Castle stood up out of it and did not lean.

"My father's cut no notch," he said.

"I know."

"He says a keeper who has nothing to add has kept the count honest. I thought he said it because he'd never found a room." He turned the bar over in his hands. "If he'd cut one for a room he'd only been told about, the post would be one more, and wrong, and nobody after him could say which cut it was."

"Yes," said Wren. "That."

"You could have just said that."

"I didn't have it. It's yours."

He nearly smiled, and put the bar back in its brackets to cover it.

"I'd have asked," he said.

"You could. You don't carry anything anywhere. Cross to Aduine and go up the steps and they'd read it to you."

He thought about it honestly; she watched him do it. The keeper's son, on a boat, on another island, asking a room full of women in grey for a name.

"The watch doesn't cross," he said. "And we don't do names. Standing, then."

"Standing."

In the post's own rooms the keeper's wife gave her bread without asking her anything, as she had the first time, and Wren told Bram the other thing while she ate it: that she had met one of the strangers on the Abbey steps, and that their grey case had kept what it saw. Someone standing, for as long as there was a lamp. Nobody when there was not. Three times, in what the stranger had been sent.

"I've been down more than three," said Bram.

"It sends when it sends, I think."

"Nobody told it what was in there."

"No."

"And it has the same."

"The same," said Wren, and saw him put that away somewhere, carefully, the way he put the lamp away.

::::: scene 3

::: pov bram-tollick

They went down the right side in the afternoon, between Mereth's hour and Aldous's, when the hall belonged to nobody.

He carried the lamp. She carried the names, and said them under her breath at their doors as they passed, the first and the fourth and the seventh and the ninth, not to him, the way you touch a wall you know in the dark. Past the ninth she had no names and counted, and he had no notches of anyone's but his own and did not count, because he knew this stretch now the way he knew the path to the lee side. The hall went down. The old stone took no mark. The lamp made its room of light and the dark closed up behind.

The door was open.

It had been open every time. He had stopped expecting otherwise somewhere in the middle of the season and had noticed himself stop, which was a strange thing to catch yourself at: a door nobody had seen open in all the watch's telling, and inside a season it was only how that door was. The strangers' case sat on the floor inside it, against the wall, where he had decided. It did what it did, which from outside was still nothing.

He raised the lamp.

Standing. The lamp's colour, and more of it than the lamp had, no floor-shadow, facing the door. Where it had been. He held the light up for as long as it takes to bar the gate.

"Not like Mereth," said Pellam's girl, beside him, quietly.

"Mereth stands."

"Mereth stands like somebody who has got where they were going. Then goes." She had her head a little on one side. "This one doesn't stand like that. This one stands like—"

She did not finish. He waited, and then asked, because the watch keeps ways and this sounded like one.

"Like what?"

"I don't know. I haven't a word the chronicle would take, and it isn't mine to make one up." She looked a moment longer. "It faces the door, though. I told the novice that. It's the only thing I gave them."

Bram lowered the lamp, and the room was empty. He raised it, and it was not. He had done that more evenings than the strangers' case seemed to have told anyone, and it had never once come out different, and it still told him nothing. But a thing in a grey box had it the same, and a book across the water had a room there. For the first time, standing in that doorway, he did not feel that he was the only one holding it up.

Beyond the door the hall went on, down, the way it always had.

"Further?" she said.

"No."

"No," she agreed, and they went up.

::::: scene 4

::: pov wren-pellam

At the hour she stood at the top of the long hall where she had stood the first evening, with the keeper's son's lamp beside her and the season's last weather doing nothing much to the outer works, and when the light gathered at the far edge of the lamp's reach she did not wait to be told to stand still.

"*Aldous, who came first,*" she said, in the voice that is not a speaking voice, "*who was given the Castle and gave it to the kingdom and went in.*"

He came up the hall on the left side at a walking pace, made of the lamp, with nothing under him, and turned at the fourth door as if it were open, and went in.

She went on. Nobody prompted her. That was what a house was asked for, under the Grove: the chronicle through from the first name without a reciter at your elbow, and she had never done it, and she did it now in the one place where the names could be checked against something. Mereth, who stood at the door. Hallin, who went down and was long. The left side, door by door, and the right, each with the way Bram had given her folded inside the line where no listener under the Grove would ever hear it. *Ceres, who turned back.* And then the one that had not been in the chronicle when she learned it.

"*A door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time, on the right side past the ninth, and one stands in it.*"

She stopped where a name goes.

She let it be as long as a name is. On the boat to Aduine she had thought of that gap as a thing she had failed to bring home. It did not sound like that in the hall. It sounded like the reciter on Vellamar, who had taught her the first names and had never once seen the Castle and had said so, on the first day, before anything else, and had said that this was the ordinary way. She had taken that for something the reciter lacked. It had been the reciter showing her where a reciter stops, and she had been too busy holding the list to see that the stopping was part of what she was being handed.

Then the line that comes after the doors, the one every reciter says fastest.

"*And the rooms are more than the names.*"

She said it at the pace of the others. It took no longer than they did. It had only ever felt long.

The hall was quiet, the one place on Krilzat with no wind in it. Down it, past where the lamp stopped, were the doors with names and the doors without, and one of them open, and someone in it for whom she had a line and the watch had a way and a book had three words she could not read, and a grey case had a count of minutes; and neither the book nor the case had been told anything by anyone who had stood here.

Nobody has finished the Castle. She had lain awake over that in a storm at the start of the season and decided that the keeping was the finishing. She would have put it more plainly now: you kept your part, and you said where it stopped.

"You didn't hurry the last one," said Bram.

"No."

"The one before it's got a hole in."

"Yes," said Wren. "I'm going to say it like that."

::::: scene 5

::: pov bram-tollick

He barred the door after the hour, which was the right time for it, storm or none.

She would be at the post until the ways were learned, she had said, all of them, both sides, and then she had said that she did not think they would be, not all, not by one house's youngest, and had not seemed troubled by it. She had been put in the spare bed without anyone asking how long. His father had said nothing further, having used up two sentences at the gate.

Bram stood with the lamp at the door-post.

Old wood, grey as the keep. Notches from the top down, more than he could count, every one a room that a keeper had stood in with their own feet and cut for with their own hand on the same day. His grandmother's. Under it his own, still paler than the rest, the only one on the post he could have told anyone anything about: right side, past the ninth, open, one in it, standing. The post did not say any of that. The post said one more.

Below his notch there was wood.

He had never looked at that part. You look at the cuts. It was bare grey post, not a great deal of it, between his notch and the floor, and he supposed that his grandmother had stood here on some evening with bare wood under hers and had looked at it the way he was looking. He looked at it for a while and did not cut anything.

Across the water, on an island he would not cross to, a girl he would not meet was writing out a name he would not have, every evening, and getting it wrong, and doing it again. Under a tree on Vellamar, some year, Pellam's girl would say a line with a hole in it and let the Kingdom hear the hole. In a room down the right side a grey case kept its minutes for people who had not come.

And at the bottom of the watch's post there was room.

Nobody has walked to the end of the Castle. He put his hand flat on the bar to be sure of it, as he had every night since he could reach, and the count was what it had been that morning, which his father would have called a day's keeping.
