::::: scene 1

::: pov wren-pellam

From where a Knight's house stands you do not see a crowning. You hear one.

The fig had put down its trunks across a whole garden quarter, and the houses stood among them by tier. The Dukes' people were nearest the old middle trunk, where the Crown would be set. Then came the Earls', and the Barons'. The Knights had the rim. Out there the hanging roots had not yet reached the ground, and the canopy let pieces of sky through. Behind the Knights there was nobody but the four stewards, who governed the Kingdom and stood where they were put. Behind the stewards were the walks, and on the walks was everybody else.

Wren stood beside her father. Knight Pellam was presiding. It was the one morning in a reign when a Knight presided over anything, and what he presided over, as far as she could make out, was a square of leaf-mould and his youngest not forgetting a list. He did it with his whole back.

She had thought she had years. *Some year*, she had told herself, *under that tree*. Then the storm season ended and the old Crown ended with it, in the court quarter, of nothing but age, by the harbour's telling. The first boat of the settled weather came round to the lee side of Casimor with salt and a letter. The keeper read the letter at the gate with his lips not moving.

"Crown's dead," he said. And then, since it had been a year for it, a second sentence. "Pellam's is wanted under the tree."

She had the ways for rather more than half the names by then. The keeper's son carried her bag as far as the gate, which he had not done before, and gave it back to her there.

"With the hole in," he said.

"With the hole in."

That was all the watch had for a crowning. He put his hand on the bar, and she went down the path with the wind behind her.

Now the reciters of the court were going in under the middle trunk: the nine from the long room, and the ones who lived on the other islands and had crossed. Old Kell of Kalenjar was among them, dry for once. Her own reciter did not look for her at the rim. It was not that kind of morning.

A house's reciter says it with the court's, every line, from the first name. That is the cross-check. It is so many mouths and one list, and where a house's mouth goes wrong the others are still going, and everybody under the tree hears who has dropped out. Nobody prompts. There was one line that no mouth but hers would say this morning, since it had not yet been said here. Until it had, it was Pellam's and not the Kingdom's.

A gardener's boy went down the processional walk with the last basket of scarlet. Under the middle trunk, where a Knight's house could not see, somebody drew a breath for a great many people.

::::: scene 2

::: pov osbern-kell

"*Aldous, who came first, who was given the Castle and gave it to the kingdom and went in.*"

It is not loud. People who have never heard the chronicle said under the Grove suppose that it must be, with so many, and it is not. The voice is not a speaking voice and it does not rise. It goes out level under the canopy, and the trunks send it back a little late, so that a man in the middle of it hears each name twice.

Osbern Kell had said it here once before, at the last crowning, and had spent that morning afraid of his own tongue. This morning he was listening. That is the other half of the work for a reciter of the court. You say, and under your own saying you listen outward, tier by tier, for a house's mouth going wrong. He could not have told anyone how it was done. You hear a wrong name as you find a stone in bread.

*Mereth, who stood at the door. Hallin, who went down and was long.* The left side came, door by door. The houses were sound. Rothmar's reciter put a Duke's weight on things. Ashgrove's was a shade quick, as Ashgrove's had been at the last crowning, in an older mouth.

He had his ear on the rim.

He knew that it was not proper. The cross-check is of every house alike, and a Knight's youngest is owed no more of a reciter's ear than a Duke's aunt. But he had taken a line from that child, standing, in a hall that creaked. On his way out of it he had told her not to let them teach her to stop late. He had been wondering since whom he had meant by *them*, having been one of them for more than fifty years.

He had been up the steps on Aduine for this morning too, ten days since. A crowning's line is new, and a new line goes up the steps before it goes under the Grove. He had said it at the top in the reciting voice. A woman in grey had gone to a shelf. He had asked what the court sent him to ask, and had been answered, and had come down with that and nothing over.

Pellam's was sound. He could hear her, or believed he could: a young voice at the rim, level, not hurrying. There was something under each name that he could not account for, a small settledness, as if she were setting each one down at a door. He put it down to nerves, which take people differently.

The right side came on. He had it by the ends, the first and the fourth and the seventh and the ninth. After the ninth, for the first time in his life under that tree, came a line he would not be saying.

::::: scene 3

::: pov wren-pellam

She had expected to be alone in it.

She had the ways folded inside the names, where no listener would hear them. The chronicle's first names are the Castle's people. The court has what they are called. The watch on Krilzat has no names, and has which way each of them goes in the long hall, and at what hour, and calls that its way. *Who came first* was the left side, the fourth door, at evening. *Who stood at the door* was midday, and as long as it takes to bar a gate. She had thought of that as hers. It was a thing she had brought off a rock, and the chronicle had no line for it. She had got ready to stand at the rim and say a list in which every name had a second thing in it that nobody else under the tree could know was there.

At her left shoulder stood the reciter for another Knight's house, a man of fifty with a net-mender's hands. He was saying *Hallin, who went down and was long* in the same level voice as everyone. And he had been his house's youngest once.

She nearly lost the line. She kept it, and looked along the rim while she said the next. There was a woman older than Kell. There was a boy not much above her own age, grey in the face with care. Every house had somebody, and every one of them had been sent. They had come up that path with a bag. They had been told that it looked the same from inside, and had gone down a hall behind some keeper's lamp on a first evening and stood still when they were told to. Whether any keeper's child had ever given them a way she did not know. The watch did not do names, and she thought it had not done ways either, for the court's children, before this year. But they had seen. All of them had.

She had lain in a storm on her first night and thought that the court had the names and had never once watched anyone go. That was so of the nine in the long room. Her own reciter had said as much. It was not so of the rim. Every mouth out here was saying *who went down* over something it had watched go down, forty years ago or four. Not one of them had ever said so to her. Finding out was the point, and there was nowhere in the list to put it.

So that was what a house was kept for. She had no time to turn it over.

"*Ceres, who turned back.*"

Then every mouth under the tree stopped on the one breath, the court's and the houses'. A line new since the last crowning is said here first by the house that carried it. Wren said it alone, slowly, so that the shape would hold.

"*A door was given, on Krilzat, in Pellam's time, on the right side past the ninth, and one stands in it.*"

She stopped where a name goes.

It was the middle of the morning. On Krilzat the door would be barred and the lamp hung on its hook, and nobody would go down the right side until the evening hour was past. The room was dark now. She did not know what was in a dark room. The keeper's son had stood at that sill for six evenings without a light, and he had told her so on the wall in the settled weather, looking at the sea. He did not know either.

She put nothing in the stop. She did not look at the reciters, since it was not their stop. She looked at the nearest trunk of the fig, which was as thick as a house door and went straight down.

She let it be as long as a name is.

::::: scene 4

::: pov osbern-kell

He had stood in that stop before. In Pellam's hall it had been like the last step of a stair in the dark. Here the Kingdom was on the step with him.

Nobody leaned. He listened for it along the court's row, where the nine from the long room stood, each of whom had gone a breath past the end on a bench in the storm season. There was not a sound from any of them. He found that it was easier when the line was in somebody else's mouth. A man cannot run on past the end of a thing he is not saying.

What he had not expected was that the stop would have a size.

Every reciter under the tree had said some hundreds of closes: a name, and what the named one does. They knew to the breath how long one takes. Nobody beat time and nobody looked along the row. The stop ran its length, which was the length of a name that was not there, and at the end of it the whole Grove came in together.

"*And the rooms are more than the names.*"

It was fast. It is always fast. He had said it fastest himself for more than fifty years, and had taught children to. This morning he heard it from outside, because one voice at the rim was not with them. She was not late. She had come in on the breath with everyone. She was only saying the line at the pace of a line. So the Kingdom was done, and standing in the gap before the descent, while Pellam's youngest was still on *the names*, by herself, as if it were an item like any other.

She finished. The descent began.

He did not think the less of fifty years. A line is said as it was given, and that one had been given to him fast. But he had heard how fast, and he did not suppose he would be able to stop hearing it.

The descent is the court's part, and the houses say it with the court: the Crowns, name by name, each closing on what the named one did. The old Crown's close was new this morning. It had gone up the steps for a death at the season's end, in another reciter's mouth. Under the tree it was said by everyone for the first time, and it was the Kingdom's.

Then came the last line, which had not been in the chronicle when he woke.

"*Averil, who stands under the Grove, of the Four Islands.*"

Somewhere past the trunks a thing was done with hands. He was too far in among the reciters to see it, and had been at the last crowning too. He had never minded. The chronicle had a name the more. The rest of the morning belonged to precedence and gardeners.

He did not go out to the rim afterwards. He had said what he had to say to her in her father's hall. He went over it once, standing under the fig with the houses breaking up round him, and found nothing in it that he wished to add or take back.

::::: scene 5

::: pov wren-pellam

The Kingdom went home down the walks saying the name.

It said her line too, here and there, in its own voices and with the words in a different order. Wren walked behind her father through the blown scarlet and listened. *A door on Krilzat, given, in Pellam's time. One standing in it.* Where the stop went, people took a breath and went on to the tide, or the boats, or what the new Crown had said from under the trunk, which a Knight's house had not heard. Nobody on a walk stops where a name goes. She had not thought they would.

Her father said *in Pellam's time* to a Baron's consort at the turn of the walk. He stopped at the end of it and looked pleased. He had looked pleased in the same way at his own table for a season, and she had minded it. This morning she found that she did not.

The gardener's boy was at the edge of the walk with an empty basket. He looked at her, and then at the tree.

"It stands, then," he said.

"It stands."

He seemed to think that was fair weight this time, and went to see what the crowd had done to his borders.

An old man came past her from the Barons' rank, going carefully on the petals. He had been somebody's youngest sixty years ago, by the look of him. He did not stop, and he did not turn his head.

"Fourth door," he said, to the air in front of him.

"Left side," said Wren. "At evening."

He nodded as people nod when a tally comes out, and went on toward the boats.

She stood where she was until her father missed her. There was no line for that either. She had the ways for rather more than half the names, and a hall on Ostrel that would want the whole morning said back to it at supper. The boat for Casimor leaves Ostrel at first light, as anyone from Ostrel will tell you.

Nobody had sent her, this time. She meant to ask whether it had room for a bag that was mostly cloak.
