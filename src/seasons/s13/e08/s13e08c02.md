::: pov oriel-fenwick

The terraces were empty by the first office. Oriel had never seen that.

A reeve does not leave his walls. Her father had not spent a night off his strip in her lifetime that she knew of. But the boats had gone out before light with every freeman who could be spared and a good many who could not, and he was on one of them, in his other coat. From the back of the terrace, where the novices stand, she could see the catchment paths all the way down to the shore, and there was nobody on them. The tide came in over the stones to no one.

The Abbey had not gone. Nobody had said that it would not. The first office was said at its hour, and the Abbess spoke it, and the novices faced the water. Oriel had all the words now but a few. Nothing in them was about a crown.

Then came the long room, with the shutters open and the grey light along the desks. The Ranger was already at the oldest shelf with her page. Oriel had supposed that the strangers would be under the tree with everyone else, and felt obscurely that one of them ought to have told her.

The senior corrector set a practice page in front of her. It was a render list in the charter hand, with the ink gone the colour of the sea. It was the same kind of page as on any morning since her first.

She had thought there would be something. She did not know what. She sat with the ink not open for as long as she dared, and then got up and went down the room to the Abbess.

"Is there nothing for today, Abbess?"

The Abbess finished the line she was on.

"There is a line for today. There is a line for every day."

"Is it written?"

"It is entered after the evening office, Fenwick, like any day's. A day is entered when it has finished being so. This one has barely started."

"Will it say—"

"It will say what is so. I would not know how to tell you more than that before the office, and I have kept the book for longer than you have been alive." She looked down. "Your page."

Oriel went back to her page. It was a list, which is easier to copy than a sentence, and she got it wrong.

::::: scene 2

::: pov emma-la-chapelle

The boat from Kalenjar had carried Emma la Chapelle and a crate of salt, and a crew who had drawn the short lot. Every other hull on the water was going the other way.

She did her re-deriving on the crossing, as she always did. It was not the readings this time. The readings came out three, as they had come out three since the day she filed them. It was the other thing.

The delegation has one instrument on this world outside its own walls. It sits on a floor on Krilzat, where the keeper of the place decided, and it reports when the relay passes over. Late in the storm season she had said in the working room that there was one line in its log she could not put a name under, and that it was the first one. The Chief had told her to write that up, at whatever band it got. She had written it up seven times since and had not handed one in.

Each had been shorter than the one before. She did not trust that. A thing that gets shorter every time you write it may be coming down to what is so, or it may only be coming down. The first had run to four pages. On re-reading she had found a person in it: somebody of a certain age, with reasons, who had carried the delegation's instrument down a hall and chosen a wall. She had never been told of any such person. She had made one, out of a single word in a log, because four pages want somebody in them. She struck the person. The second draft had a paragraph on why that room. She struck it too. By the seventh she had a page, and the page still held a sentence that began *It seems likely that the same hand*.

She looked at that sentence for most of the width of the strait. Then she struck it, under her own name, with the date in the Archive's reckoning. What was left was about half a page.

She was on the boat because of a clerk. He had come to the compound gate a fortnight before with the day of the crowning, given in the form the court uses when the room requires it. He gave the day and nothing after it. That evening the Chief had said that the delegation had been told a day and had not been asked to it, and that those were two things. An officer kept back from her work to stand at a shutter and listen would be the delegation going to the crowning by the back wall. It was one of la Chapelle's three days, and she would take it.

So she had the oldest shelf on the day the Kingdom crowned over four islands, with a charter page in front of her that counted five. She noted it and gave it no band. It was not a reading. It was only where she happened to be sitting.

The scriptorium was as it was on any of her days. That she did write, having eyes: *Day of the crowning, by the court's notice to the compound. The Abbey keeps its ordinary day. First office at its hour. Correctors at the shelves. A novice at the practice desk.*

The novice had gone the length of the room to ask the Abbess something and had come back looking as if the answer had been exact. Emma did not write that down.

::::: scene 3

::: pov oriel-fenwick

The first boats came back on the afternoon tide, and the terraces had it before the sails were down.

Oriel heard it through the open shutters. It came up from the catchment path in the voices of people who had been awake since before light. They had stood on a garden walk behind four stewards and had seen the backs of a great many heads. Crowned, they said. Under the tree. And the name, which everybody on Aduine had known for years would be the name, and which sounded different this afternoon, as a strip does on the day it passes to the son.

Then a woman's voice came from further down. She was pleased with it, as people are with the size of a fish.

"And Pellam's youngest said her door, the one on Casimor, and left the end off it. In front of the whole tree. And they stood there, every one of them, reciters and all. Then on they went."

"Left the end off?"

"Where the name goes. Stopped dead. Well."

Oriel's pen had stopped too.

She had the end. She had it where she had had it since the storm season, three words long. It was on a sheet in her desk more times than she could count, in ink that was her own, and it was the first thing in her mouth whenever anybody said *Krilzat*. A girl of her own age had stood at the top of these steps with her bag on her shoulder and told the Abbess what she would say under the Grove. *A door, and one standing in it. Then I stop.* She had done it. The Kingdom had been made to stand in the place where Oriel's three words went, and it had gone home telling the story as a thing that happened to a line.

She did not say them. Nobody had asked. It was not hard, this afternoon, and she noticed that it was not. In the last week of the storm season she had held the name in her mouth like a hot stone. Now it was only where it was kept.

Down the room the Abbess had not looked up at the voices. Neither had the corrector. The Ranger at the oldest shelf had looked up. Her hand had gone to the grey slate she wrote in, and stayed on it, and she had written nothing.

*She can't*, Oriel thought. *Nobody told her. She only heard.*

She had learned that much of the Ranger's book on the steps. What is only heard goes down as heard, or it does not go down.

::::: scene 4

::: pov emma-la-chapelle

The novice was on the steps before her again. She was sitting this time, which novices did not do, a few steps above the catchment stones with her inky hands hanging off her knees.

"You weren't at it," she said.

"No."

"Weren't you asked?"

"We were told the day. We weren't asked to it." Emma sat down two steps above her, with the reader shut. "And we didn't ask."

"Why not? I'd have asked."

"We've asked the court for one thing in four years. I don't think we've finished understanding it." She could hear the Chief in that, and let it stand. It was also what she thought herself. "The day's line. Is it written yet?"

"After the office. You'll be on the water."

"Then I'll read it when I'm next at the shelf."

"It'll say the same then."

"Yes," Emma said. "That's why it can wait for me."

The novice turned that over. Below them the tide was coming in, at the pace it had chosen.

"What will yours say? For today."

Nobody on Aduine had asked Emma that before. They asked what the delegation thought. Nobody had asked what its record stated, as you would ask it of a book.

"It isn't written either. I write it tonight, at the compound." She turned the reader over on her knee. "It'll say what the instrument had. And what I was told, and who told me."

"What were you told?"

"Today? Nothing." She found that she was nearly laughing, and that it was not unhappiness. "I was told the day, a fortnight ago. Today I've sat at a shelf. I heard some people on a path."

"I could tell you what the boats said."

"I'd have to put it down as heard."

"Then put it down as heard."

Emma opened the reader. "Go on."

"The heir's crowned. Under the tree. This morning." The novice stopped there. There had been more on the path than that, and Emma had heard some of it. The girl looked at the water and did not go on.

Emma wrote it as given. *A novice, on the steps, as she had it from the boats: the heir is crowned, under the tree, this morning.*

"You've put *a novice*."

"I still haven't been told your name."

"No," the novice agreed, comfortably, and got up to go to the office.

Emma sat on for the minute the boat allowed her. It was all the delegation had of the crowning in anyone's words: one sentence from a girl who had it from a reeve who had seen the backs of heads. She would put it in tonight under the instrument's line. Below it would go the half page. That was a list of things she had not been told, and the list was longer than everything above it.

For seven drafts she had been trying to make the list shorter. It came to her on the steps that she might have been filing it under the wrong heading. It was not what was wrong with the write-up. It was a good deal of what the write-up had to say.

She wrote the date, and went down to the boat.

::::: scene 5

::: pov oriel-fenwick

The evening office had the terraces under it again, with smoke from the strips and somebody's dog. The novices faced the water. The tide came in over the catchment stones as it had come in that morning to nobody, and no faster.

Afterwards the Abbess went in to the book.

Oriel sat down at the practice desk with the shutters open and the light going grey, and reached for the Castle's count, which she copied every evening. The senior corrector took it out from under her hand. She laid another page on the slope and went away.

It was one line, with the ruled column beside it. The ink was black.

Oriel had never seen the hand in black ink. Every page she had ever been set was old. The oldest were the colour of the sea, and the newest a brown like a strip in autumn. This one had been made since the office. Somebody she had stood tideward with an hour ago had gone to the book, and somebody had written it out fair for a novice, and the ink still had a shine on the downstrokes.

She read it with her hands behind her, out of habit.

*The crowning. On Vellamar, under the fig: Averil, crowned over the Five Islands. Tide to the third stone at the first office. The walls kept.*

Beside it the column was ruled and empty.

Five. A whole Kingdom had stood under that tree this morning and heard four, her father among them, in his other coat. He would have said, if you asked him, that the Crown holds four. The hand had written five tonight, in black, exactly as it wrote five in the ink that had gone the colour of the sea.

It did not say who said the chronicle. It did not say that a girl left the end off a line, or that there was a line, or a door. It said a crowning, and a tide, and that the walls were kept. That was what had been there.

And it did not say who wrote it.

She knew every face in the house now. She knew the corrector's way of setting a page down, and the Abbess's way of finishing a line. She looked along this one for the turn of a letter that belonged to somebody. There was none, and of course there was none. That was the hand. She had worked that out in the storm season, over six days, with her fingers laced behind her. She had worked it out about women three hundred years dead. This was a woman she had eaten bread beside at midday, and it made no difference at all.

*The book does not get older*, the Abbess had said. Oriel had believed it then as you believe a thing an Abbess says. She was looking at it now.

She dipped the pen. She ruled the column and left it empty. She wrote the crowning, and the fig, and the name, and five, and the tide, and the walls.

The corrector came and looked at the sheet and went away.

Oriel put the two side by side before the light went. The ink on both was the same black, and it was the first time it had been. She could still tell which was hers.
