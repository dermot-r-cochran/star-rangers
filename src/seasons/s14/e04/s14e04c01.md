::: pov nakagawa

*Set it down. Step back. Wait the interval. Whatever happens in it, or does not, come back.*

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there. The order above was not logged. It was given, at the station, on the morning of the first stone, out loud, and written into the file afterwards because the survey lead writes things into the file. It is seventeen words long. Nakagawa had counted, which was a bad sign.

On the day of the long shift Nakagawa had said, at the junction, that an order is not rewritten standing up on the day a thing happens. If it needed rewriting it would look the same in the morning.

It was the morning.

The order looked the same. That was no help. It was the *need* that was supposed to look the same, and the need did not look like anything. Nakagawa read the seventeen words before anyone had eaten and could not find the place in them where shift two-three-four had gone wrong. Then read the entry for shift two-three-four, and could not find the place in that either.

One of them said *come back*. The other said *stayed past the interval*. Both were in the survey lead's hand. Both were signed.

"I've three drafts," said Egede, coming in with the cold, "and you're not going to like any of them. I don't."

"After the knock," said Nakagawa.

::::: scene 2

::: pov aravena

Aravena knocked.

Shift two-three-five. The stone. The squeeze, sideways, the hard square on the breastbone. The silt. The one on the line, in the open, still, grey, large.

It looked the same. Aravena did not know what else it would have looked like. Something had been said from there yesterday, for a long time, to Aravena, and here was the place it had been said from, and it was silt, and two feet, and a rock at the edge of the light.

Kneel. Set it down, a hand from those feet. Step back. Say the thing, going up at the end.

Wait.

The one on the line bent, and put a hand on the rock.

For the length of one breath Aravena did not know. Not afraid. Only not knowing, with the whole body, the way you do not know on a ladder whether there is another rung.

The rock moved. At once. A good one.

So that was that. Yesterday was yesterday, and today the stone came at the beginning, where it had come on every shift for two years, and nobody on the line seemed to think anything was owed. Aravena felt the place in the body where you turn.

And did not turn yet.

There was more interval. There always had been. The rock came early in it and the rest ran on after, and Aravena had stood through the rest on every shift without once thinking of it as standing. It had only been how long the thing took. You set it down, and it was answered, and by the time you had finished being answered it was time to go.

But the order said *wait the interval*. It did not say *wait for the rock*. Yesterday Aravena had worked out, on this silt, what the interval was for. Today the stone had come, and there was still interval left, and for the first time Aravena stood in the leftover part and knew it was there.

It was room. That was what it was. If a hand ever stayed on that rock again, Aravena would not have to decide anything. There would already be an interval's worth of Aravena standing here.

Aravena waited it out, to the end, on purpose. Then the crack, not running.

"Log. Shift two-three-five. Upper junction. Party of three. Knock answered."

The unit hummed.

"Back at the usual time," said Egede, who had been watching the timepiece and trying to look as though not.

"Yes," said Aravena.

It was true. Nothing about it had been usual.

::: pov nakagawa

Egede laid the drafts on the station table that evening in a row, because a thing you are going to argue about should be where everyone can see it. Egede had learned that here, and did it better than the survey lead.

"Failure modes first," said Egede. "A. *Wait the interval, or for as long as the hand of the one who answers remains on the rock.* It says what happened. It fails if the hand stays and nothing is said. Aravena stands on the silt until somebody's legs give."

"B."

"The same, with a limit. *But not longer than.* It fails at the number. I've one figure to put in it, and it's forty-four minutes, and I know what that is a figure of. One shift."

"So on the forty-fifth minute," said Aravena, from the other berth, "I walk off. While somebody's talking to me. Because of how long they talked last time."

Egede looked at draft B for about as long as it takes to say a name, and then turned it face down.

"Withdrawn. I said you wouldn't like them."

"C," said Nakagawa.

"C is the other way." Egede did not pick it up. "*Wait the interval and come back. No exception.* Say it harder. I don't want it. I'm putting it on the table because somebody has to. The file's got an order that says come back, and a shift where nobody did, and your name under both. That isn't a file. One of them's wrong. Either the order goes to meet the shift or the shift gets written up as a departure from it, and I'd sooner it was the order. But it can't be neither."

It was the right objection, and it had the specialist's whole weight in it, and Nakagawa had been reading seventeen words since before breakfast without an answer to it.

"Ferrant."

Ferrant was on the floor at the third shelf with the handset on her knee. She did not look up at once.

:::

::: pov odile-ferrant

She had no page for an order. She said so, first, so that nobody would take what came next for more than it was.

"I'll grade the drafts as instruments. It's the only way I know to grade anything. A. It says a hand on a rock means *wait*. That is a reading. You have written a reading into an instruction, and you have it from one occurrence."

"One occurrence of forty-four minutes," said Egede.

"Length isn't count. I can have an excursion that lasts a week. It is still one." She turned the handset over. "B, you've withdrawn. C is honest, and it throws away the only thing yesterday taught you, which is that the person at the line could see something the order couldn't."

"Then what do you do," said Egede, "when an instrument goes outside what you set for it? Once?"

That she did have a page for.

"An order's a band. Somebody sets it, in advance, on what they know. Mine was set wide, for this station, by people who had never stood on it. When a reading goes outside the band, once, you do not reset the band to fit the reading. You'd be resetting it every week. And you don't pretend the reading was inside. You write the excursion down beside the band, under a name. And you make sure somebody goes on reading." She looked at the survey lead. "You've done the first two. The entry is beside the order and Aravena's name is on the reading. I wouldn't touch either. What I'd want to know, before I left a band alone, is who set it and from what."

"I set it," said Nakagawa.

"All of it?"

Nakagawa opened a mouth to say yes, and did not say it.

::::: scene 4

::: pov nakagawa

It took most of the night, and the wind had gone round to the north by the end of it.

*Set it down.* That was the survey lead's. *Step back.* That too. *Come back.* That most of all. Nakagawa could remember deciding each of them, on the second morning after Aravena had said the thing about a door.

*Wait the interval.*

Nakagawa had not decided that. Nakagawa had only said it. On the morning of the first stone there had been no need to say how long, because all three of them knew how long. They had known for two years.

The first-year file. The second-year. Laid out by arrival, the way Nakagawa had laid it out once before. A shift of rock somewhere below the margin, before the party was in sight of it. Then, after a time, the warm shapes at the margin gone. Every arrival. And in Egede's column, from the third month on, the time between the one and the other, measured, because Egede measures things, and it had come out the same so often that the party had stopped writing the figure and started writing *the interval*.

That was where it came from.

It was not a length the survey had chosen. It was a length the survey had *found*. It was how long the ones below the margin took, after a rock was moved, before they went. The party had timed it a hundred times from the far end of a passage without knowing what it was watching, and then on the morning of the first stone the survey lead had needed a length of time for one small person to stand at a door, and had reached for the only one on the planet that everybody already knew.

Their own. Handed back to them, in an order, by somebody who had not noticed doing it.

Nakagawa sat with the two files open and the seventeen words between them.

So that was why it could not be made longer, and why it could not be given a limit. You do not correct a thing you measured off somebody else by what would suit you. Egede would say that of a test card. Ferrant had more or less just said it of a band. And on shift two-three-four, the one on the line had put a hand on a rock and not moved it for a very long time, and whatever else that was, it was the people the interval had been measured from doing something else with it.

Aravena was awake. Aravena was generally awake when the file was open.

"That's how long they waited," said Aravena, looking at the column upside down. "Before. After their knock. Before they gave up on us and left us the place."

"That is a reading."

"It's mine."

"It is. And it is not going in." Nakagawa said it gently. "Not that one. The file says a rock moved and then the margin was empty, and the time between. It does not say anyone was waiting for anything. I should like them to have been waiting for us. I should like it very much. That is exactly why I am not the person to write it down."

Aravena thought about that for a while.

"All right," said Aravena. "It's still mine."

"I never said it wasn't."

::::: scene 5

::: pov nakagawa

Shift two-three-eight. Four mornings. The order looked the same on all of them, and now so did the need, which was none.

Nakagawa told Egede at the junction, before the knock, standing up, since it was not a rewriting.

"The order stands. All seventeen. I am not lengthening the interval and I am not capping it, because it was never mine. You measured it. Third month of the first year. Off them."

Egede's face did several things. The last of them was the instrument specialist finding a figure in somebody else's document and knowing it for one of Egede's own.

"You put my interval in a standing order."

"I put theirs in. In your hand."

"And nobody said."

"Nobody had to. We all knew how long it was. Nobody asked whose." Nakagawa let that be what it was. "Your objection stands too. An order and a shift, and my name under both. It is answered in the column, and you may not like the answer. The order was followed. Aravena waited the interval. On that shift the interval was a long one. That is my reading and it goes in as mine, not as a fact."

"That's an elastic order."

"It is an order that says what we will do. It never once said how long they would take."

Egede was quiet. Then, because Egede is honest about instruments, including drafts: "And if Aravena's on the silt and it's quiet? No voice. Nothing coming up the crack. For an hour."

"Then that is overdue, and overdue is the party's own rule, and older than the knock. It is for a person nobody can hear. On two-three-four we could hear that Aravena was being spoken to. Nobody is overdue who can be heard being spoken to." A pause. "If it is ever the other thing, I will decide it then, at the junction, and I will not have written it down in advance, and you may put that in the file as a failure mode."

"I will," said Egede. And then: "The drafts. Keep them. Not adopted, with the reasons. Ferrant said the struck ones were the best thing in the file."

"You are building a shelf of those."

"I'm aware," said Egede.

So the survey lead's own column, that shift, said: *Standing order of the knock read against shift 234 on four mornings. Not rewritten. The interval in it was measured, not set: years one and two, rock shift below margin to withdrawal from margin. It is not the survey lead's to lengthen or to cap. Reading of shift 234 stands under the name it was entered under. Three drafts considered and not adopted; kept.*

Then Nakagawa knelt to the unit, and told it the channel was off, and the time. Aravena picked a stone and went down the third passage with the light held low.

The rise. The wait. The rock, up through the boots, at once.

And then nothing, for a while, which Nakagawa had stood through on more than a hundred and seventy shifts and never timed, because the rock had come and the rest was only Aravena walking back. It was not walking back. Nakagawa looked at the timepiece and saw that now. There was the rock, and then there was most of the interval still to run, and somebody was standing on the silt running it.

Aravena had always come back at the same minute. The column had said so on every shift since it began to carry the time, but one. The column could not say that on this shift, and the three before it, the minute had been on purpose.

That would have to be asked about. Nakagawa decided not to ask. It was the kind of thing a person tells you, or does not, and it was not a planetary survey's to know.

The light came back up the third passage, low, not running.
