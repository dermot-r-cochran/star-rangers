::: pov toma-egede

The part came in a box the size of two hands.

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there. On this shift the someone was sent a correction by people who were not, and had to write back.

The supply run had come down in the usual wind, in the afternoon, with the party just up from the junction and the knock answered and the cold still in everybody's knees. Crates for the station. Fuel. Food that was not the food they had. Aravena went out to carry, because Aravena always did. The run's crew said the wind would be up again by dusk and they meant to be gone before it.

And one small box, with Egede's name on it, from the Corps' instrument desk.

Egede had not asked the instrument desk for anything. Egede knew that for certain, because Egede kept a list.

There was a docket on top.

*Fault query. Relay unit, upper junction. Sound channel. Recurring dropout at start of shift: twenty-two shifts, 220 to 241. One dropout of forty-four minutes, shift 234, marked for attention. Pattern consistent with module fault under load. Replacement module enclosed. Fit, test, and return the faulted part with classification.*

Egede read it standing in the door with the wind coming in.

Then read it again, indoors.

Somebody at a desk a very long way from Fliade had been reading the junction unit's channels as they came up the relay. Properly. Every shift. They had seen the sound channel go quiet at the same point in every shift for twenty-two shifts, and come back. They had seen it go quiet once for forty-four minutes. And they had done exactly what Egede would have done: decided the part was failing, and sent a new one on the next run, without being asked, so that a three-person party on a cold world would not be left with a deaf unit.

It was a kindness. It was good work. Egede could not find one thing wrong with it.

"It's the one instrument on this station," said Egede, to nobody, "that has never once been wrong."

"You said that the day I switched it off," said Nakagawa, from the table.

"I know I did."

"What's in the box?"

"A new one."

Nakagawa looked up. Then, carefully, did not smile.

::: pov odile-ferrant

Hers was only a page.

It had come down in the same bag, folded once, with the boundary side's mark on it. She read it standing up, with her back to the rack, because a thing read before you have settled is a thing you still see.

*Review of attendance. Section Lead O. Ferrant, boundary monitoring. Fliade station. In attendance past one year. The officer will state continuing need, at grade, or return with this run.*

That was all. It was a fair page. She would have written it herself, about somebody else, and sooner.

She had come for seven weeks of an optical return with nothing behind it. She had read it. She had filed her reading at its grade, in her own file, and seen the thing once with an instrument of her own, and filed that. Since then the handheld had shown one shape on the line every shift. The gravimeter on the third shelf said every evening what it had said for a long run of evenings: the offset, where it had been since before the first knock, and nothing else.

She read the quiet one every night all the same. She was not sorry. But *I read it and it says the same* is not a need. It is a habit, and she knew the difference, because telling other people the difference was most of her trade.

She could reset the band. She had said so the week she came: when she left, *nominal* would mean again what it ought to. It was an afternoon's work. There was an afternoon.

Her pack was under the spare berth. It had been one pack when she came and it was one pack now.

She sat down on the floor by the third shelf, where she thought best, and tried to write one line that was true.

*Boundary instrument: within band. Offset stable.*

True. And an argument for going.

She looked at it for a while.

There was something else. She knew there was, the way you know there is a reading in a log before you have found the line. Every shift she came down to the junction, or waited at the table, and wrote *Countersigned. Interval as stated. Nothing to grade.* She had written it forty-two times. This morning made forty-three.

That was not boundary data. Nothing of it was in her file, and she had said so, more than once, so that nobody would ask her twice.

A patch says a Ranger is trusted to be right about one narrow thing. She had spent a year on this roof being careful not to be right about anything else.

Outside, somebody dropped a crate, and Aravena said sorry to it.

::::: scene 3

::: pov toma-egede

You do not answer a fault query with a feeling. You answer it with a test.

Egede brought the junction unit up onto the bench, which was the end of the station table, and ran the sound channel, in test, where it files nothing, through everything the test card had. Low. High. Rising. Falling. A knock on the table leg. It classed what it was built to class, and called the rest *nothing*, and did not miss one.

"Serviceable," said Egede.

"You knew that," said Aravena, coming in with a crate.

"I knew it this morning. Now I've got it at a time, on a card. That's different." Egede wrote the time on the card. "They'll want the difference."

Then the docket. It had classes, the way a channel does. Somebody decides in advance what kinds of thing can be wrong with a part, and prints them down the side with a box each.

*Component. Power. Firmware. Environmental. Operator. Unresolved.*

Egede went down them the way Egede goes down anything. Failure modes first.

Component. No. On the card.

Power. No.

Firmware. No, and Egede would have liked to see somebody try to say so.

Environmental. Egede thought about that one longer than was comfortable. There was certainly something in the environment. It was large and grey and stood on a line. It was not a *cause*.

Operator.

That was the box the desk would expect. A channel that goes off because somebody switched it off is an operator matter. In every manual Egede had ever read, the word printed after *operator* was *error*.

"It isn't an error," said Egede.

"No," said Nakagawa.

"It's the least accidental thing in the whole file. There's a column for it. It's got your name on it every shift, and a time, and hers under that."

"Yes."

"And none of that goes up to the instrument desk. They get the channels. They don't get your column. That goes to a different desk." Egede sat back. "So from where they sit there's just a hole. Same place, every shift. Nobody's name on it."

It had been said in this room, on the night of the argument, by the person now sitting on the floor at the third shelf. *A gap with no name on it looks, in eleven years, exactly like a fault.*

It had not taken eleven years. It had taken twenty-two shifts and one careful stranger.

There was one more box, at the bottom, where there always is. *Other (state).* An instrument specialist is trained from the first week never to use it. *Other* is where you put what you could not be bothered to find out.

Egede pulled the order of shift two-two-oh, to get the words exactly, since they were going in a document.

*Off from the moment the one who knocks enters the third passage until they are back at the junction.*

Egede read it as an instrument. It had a start. It had an end. It had no length.

No minutes anywhere. Every dark Egede had ever logged, on any station, said how long, in advance, on the order. This one said *until they are back*. And Aravena came back when the interval was out, and the interval, it had turned out not long ago, was not even the party's. And on two-three-four Aravena had come back when the one on the line was finished, and not before.

So the length of the gap in Egede's sound channel was set, every shift, down at the line.

Once, for forty-four minutes, it had been set by somebody large and grey who did not know there was a channel. Or a unit. Or a desk.

Egede had a word for an instrument that files a people who were never asked about it. Egede had given it to the survey lead twice. This was that, with the ends changed over: an instrument that goes deaf for exactly as long as somebody who has never heard of it takes.

There was not a box for that either. Egede did not look for one.

Egede wrote, small, in *Other (state)*:

*Not a fault. Channel off by standing order of the survey lead from shift 220, for the knock. Each interval entered in the survey lead's column and countersigned. Interval is set by event, not by time. Longest: 44 min, shift 234. Module serviceable on test, card attached. Replacement returned unopened.*

Under the boxes there was a line Egede had never once seen filled in on a docket. *Certified officer's countersign, where applicable.*

Egede looked at the floor by the third shelf.

"Is it applicable?"

::::: scene 4

::: pov odile-ferrant

She read the docket twice. Then the card. Then the box, which she turned over once in her hands and did not open.

"*Pattern consistent with module fault*," she said. "They graded it."

"Wrongly."

"Correctly. That's what it is consistent with. From there." She gave the box back. "It's a good query. I'd want whoever wrote it on my station. They were handed a gap with no name on it and they did the only thing you can honestly do with one."

"I'm sending their part back."

"So you should. With the name." She took the pen. "It's applicable."

*Countersigned. Not a fault. Dark by order; owned.* Her name. The patch number under it, which was what the line was for.

And then she sat with the pen still in her hand, because she had just read her own continuing need in somebody else's document.

She had said it herself. An instrument that is off is entitled to nothing. What it has instead is a person. A dark is owned by the name on it and the countersign under it, and the countersign has to come from somebody whose patch is not the survey lead's to command, or it is only a party agreeing with itself.

There was one such patch on Fliade. It was on her shell, on the hook by the door.

She wrote it fast, then, the way you write a reading once you have found the line.

*1. Boundary instrument, third shelf: within band, offset stable. Does not of itself require an officer in attendance. Band to be reset on this officer's departure.*

*2. Survey instrument, upper junction: sound channel dark each shift by standing order of the survey lead, interval by event. An ordered dark is owned by its countersign. This officer is the only certified officer on the planet. The instrument desk has on this run queried the dark as a fault (docket attached), which is what such a gap looks like from outside when the countersign is not where the reader is looking. Need continues while the order stands, or until another certified officer attends.*

*3. The second ground is instrument discipline. It is not boundary data. The boundary side may find it is not theirs to staff. It is stated so that they can.*

She read the third line again. It was the one that could send her home. It was also the only one that made the other two worth signing, and she left it in.

"Survey lead."

Nakagawa came and read it, standing, all three lines, and took rather longer over the third than the others.

"You have written them a reason to recall you."

"I've written them what it is. If I leave that out, it's a need with a hole in it and nobody's name on the hole."

"Yes," said Nakagawa. "I see that." And then: "I wrote for a patch, last year, to be right about one narrow thing. It has been. This is another request. I shall make it as one."

Under her three lines, in the survey lead's hand: *Countersigning officer requested by the survey lead. This is a request distinct from that of the third year, and is made as such.*

There was a fourth reason. She did not write it. On the last line of the entry for shift two-three-four there were four names, and one of them was hers, because she had asked. She had no page for what that line was about and had never wanted one. She found she did not want to read the next line like it from some other station, in somebody else's hand.

It had no grade. It was not a need. It stayed where it was.

::::: scene 5

::: pov toma-egede

The run lifted before dusk, as it had said it would, with the wind already getting up under it.

It took the empties. It took a small box, unopened, with a docket on top and a test card under the docket. It took one folded page with three lines and a fourth in another hand.

It did not take Ferrant. She stood on the roof of the world in her shell, with the patch on it, and watched it go, the way you watch a thing you have signed for.

"You're still here," said Aravena.

"Until somebody reads line three."

"They might not send anyone."

"Then I'm here on my own statement. It's got my name on it. It can be asked about."

Egede was thinking about the desk.

Somebody there would open the bag, and find their own part come back with the seal on it, and a card, and a box marked *Other*. They would not be pleased. Egede would not have been. Then, if they were any good, and they were, they would read what was written in it. *Interval is set by event.* And they would sit there, a long way from any cave, wondering what kind of event takes forty-four minutes and gets a certified officer's countersign.

Egede could not tell them. It was not in the box. It was not in anything.

"They'll write back," said Egede. "They'll want to know what the event is."

"What will you say?" said Nakagawa.

Egede thought about it properly. Failure modes first.

"That the channel was off," said Egede. "So I've no reading."

Ferrant made a sound that was very nearly a laugh, and went in out of the wind.

That evening the survey lead read the unit's channels at the station table, as on every evening. Thermal. Imager: one upright shape on the line, one return. Sound channel.

*Anstolik, passing.* Four.

And the gap. A little way into the shift, where it always was. It had a start and an end and a name over it and another name under that, and as of today it had a docket as well, on its way to a desk, to say so to people who had not been there.

Egede looked at it over the survey lead's shoulder, which was a habit now.

It was the same hole. It was the best-kept thing in the file, and it had just been taken for a fault by the first careful person to see it from outside.

"I'm going to put a note in the summary," said Egede. "At the turn of the year. So the next one who looks knows where to look."

"What class?" said Nakagawa.

"I'll think of one," said Egede. "I've been getting practice."
