::: pov aravena

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there. It is about an entry two years old, and the someone was there for that one too, and had their back to it.

Aravena had knocked on every shift since the long one, and the year was most of the way through its file.

A stone, a step back, the thing said, the wait. Every shift the one on the line bent and put a hand on the rock, and every shift Aravena did not know, for the length of a breath, whether the hand would stay. Then the rock, a good one, and the interval, which was now a thing Aravena waited on purpose, and the crack. At the junction: *knock answered*. The unit hummed.

Nothing was different. That was what Aravena kept turning over, on the way up the long passage with the unit's weight where it always sat. Something had been said for forty-four minutes to a person who could not read a word of it, and on the very next shift the one who said it had answered a stone with a stone as if nothing were owed. Nobody had asked Aravena for anything back. Nobody had looked to see whether it had landed.

The other thing Aravena kept turning over was the standing.

For two years Aravena had come out onto the silt and found the one on the line standing with the whole body still, and had thought of it as something the one on the line did, the way the unit hums. Then, for forty-four minutes, Aravena had done it. It was not hard, and it was not nothing. It was a thing you did with all of yourself so that none of you got into what was being said. Aravena had not known that until doing it, and had not been able to stop knowing it since.

So at the station that evening, with the wind on the wall and the channels not yet read, Aravena asked.

"Four. In the second year. How long did Four stand?"

Nakagawa did not need the file for that. Nobody on the roof did.

"Eleven minutes."

"What were we doing?"

Egede looked up from the imager's case. Ferrant, on the floor by the third shelf with the gravimeter's handset on her knee, did not look up, which was how you knew she was listening.

"Working," said Nakagawa. "With our backs to it. That is in the file."

"What were we *saying*?"

Nakagawa looked at Aravena for about as long as it takes to say a name.

"That," said the survey lead, "is not in the file. Or I have never looked." A pause, the kind Nakagawa left when a thing was about to be the survey lead's own. "I have never looked. Sit down. This one is the station's."

::: pov nakagawa

Shift forty-one came up out of the file the way a thing comes up that has been read too often in one direction.

Nakagawa knew the thermal channel of that shift better than any page in four years. A profile, warm, upright, at the edge of the working light where the third passage comes in. Arrived between one sweep and the next. Eleven minutes. Gone. Nakagawa had read it the evening it happened and called the other two over and said the thing the party still said, and had struck a word out of Aravena's entry, and had not slept.

What Nakagawa had not done, that evening or on any evening after, was read the rest of the shift beside it.

There had been no reason to. The log of shift forty-one was a log: the party's own voice, saying what the party had done. It was what the survey already knew. You do not go looking in your own entry for what happened behind you.

But the unit marks a time against every sweep of its thermal channel, and it marks a time against every line it is told. It always had. Egede had specified it. Nobody had ever needed the second set of marks for anything but putting the lines in order.

Nakagawa put the two columns on the table, side by side, and ran a finger down them.

*Log. Shift forty-one. Upper junction. Party of three. Nothing so far.* That was before. Four minutes before the profile arrived, by the marks. Nothing so far.

Then the profile. The first sweep that had it.

Then, inside it, a line in Aravena's voice: floor temperature at the junction, taken at the unit's base, and the figure. The unit's acknowledgement.

Then nothing the unit had kept, for a while.

Then a second line, in the same voice: floor temperature, repeat, and the same figure. The unit's acknowledgement.

Then the last sweep that had the profile, and the first that did not.

Egede's tracks came after. The time came after. The eleven minutes held two lines of the log and both of them were the same temperature.

"I took it twice," said Aravena, very quietly. "The first one looked wrong."

"It was not wrong."

"No. I took it again to be sure."

Egede had come round the table to read over Nakagawa's shoulder, which was becoming a habit. "That's all of it? In eleven minutes?"

"That is all the unit was told. It keeps what it is told. It does not keep us talking; there is no class for that, and I am not sorry." Nakagawa looked at the gap between the two lines, which by the marks was most of the eleven minutes. "You were at the far wall with the imager."

"Then I was saying something," said Egede. "I'd have been saying something the whole time. Lens fog, probably." A pause, and then, because Egede is honest about instruments, including that one: "I'd like it noted that none of it was kept."

"Noted," said Nakagawa, and did not write it down.

It should have been funny, and it very nearly was. The party had spent a season arguing about what a survey may keep of a thing said in its hearing. It had turned a channel off. It had written the thinnest entry of its life, on purpose, and countersigned the gap. And the whole time there had been a shift in the file on which somebody had stood in the open at the edge of the party's light for eleven minutes while the party said things, and the party had every word of what it had said that the unit was ever given, and it was a temperature. Twice. To be sure.

"*Overheard*," said Egede, in the voice Egede uses for a word that is going to end up in a document.

"No," said Aravena.

Egede waited. It was a real waiting; Egede had learned it at the junction, and kept it.

"You overhear from behind something. There's nothing at the edge of the light. It's open floor. I've knelt on it." Aravena was looking at the two columns, not at anyone. "Eleven minutes, in the open, where any of us only had to turn round. That isn't overhearing. I don't know what it is. But I stood like that for forty-four minutes on two-three-four and I wasn't overhearing either."

"We do not know what it stood for," said Nakagawa.

"No."

"We know what was being said while it stood. That is a different thing to know, and it is the only one of the two that is ours."

"I know," said Aravena. "I'm not asking you to file the other one. I'm saying I've been on the inside of it now. From inside it's the same thing to do. You go still, all of you, so none of you gets into it. And you don't know a word. I don't know if that's what Four was doing. I know that's what it is like."

"Then that is a reading, and it is yours."

"It's mine."

"It goes in under your name," said Nakagawa, "beside the fact, and not instead of it."

::: pov odile-ferrant

They asked her, in the end. They always did, on this roof, and she had stopped minding it.

"I'll grade what there is to grade," Ferrant said. "It is not much, and it is good. Two sets of time marks, from one instrument, on one shift, made by a specialist who is sitting here. One set says when something warm stood at the edge of the light. The other says when two lines were spoken into the unit. The second falls inside the first. That is not *consistent with*. That is simply so, as far as one clock can agree with itself, and I would sign it."

"And the rest," said Nakagawa.

"There is no rest. Nothing on that table says why anyone stood anywhere. An interval tells you how long. It has never once told anybody what for." She turned the handset over on her knee. "It isn't boundary data. Nothing of it goes in my file. I'm saying that so nobody asks me twice."

Nobody did.

She went back to the gravimeter's log, which said what it had said every evening since she had narrowed its band: a small, stable offset, and nothing else. She read it properly. She was not going to be the officer who stopped reading the quiet one because something more interesting was on the table.

But she had kept forty-four minutes on her own timepiece, on shift two-three-four, and it had agreed, and she found the two numbers had gone and sat down next to each other without being asked.

Forty-four. The roof held the interval and not one word. There had been no instrument to take the words and there never would be, by an order she had countersigned and would countersign again.

Eleven. The roof held the interval, and it held the words too, every one the unit was given, in a clear voice, with the time against each. It could do that because the words were its own.

She thought about that for as long as she allowed herself to think about things that were not hers.

A survey can always write down what it said. It is the easiest record there is, and nobody had thought it worth reading, because what you said is the one thing you believe you already know. And it had taken a person standing still for forty-four minutes in front of something unreadable to make anyone on this roof wonder what the roof itself had sounded like, to someone standing still.

It had sounded like a temperature, taken twice.

She did not think that was a small thing to have been. She had spent a working life among people who, asked what they would say if they were certain someone was listening, would have prepared something. This had not been prepared. It was what the party said when it thought nobody was there, and it was exact, and it had been checked.

Ferrant put that nowhere. It had no grade. She only noticed, closing the handset, that it was the second thing on this planet she had nothing to file about, and that she had stopped counting them as losses.

Nakagawa wrote it under that evening's date. Not on shift forty-one. Shift forty-one stood as it had been written, strike and all; you do not go back and make an old entry wiser than the people who made it.

*Thermal profile, shift 41, eleven minutes, as filed. Log lines taken inside the interval, by the unit's time marks: two. Floor temperature, upper junction; floor temperature, repeat. Coincidence in time. No reading.*

Under it, in Aravena's name, Aravena's sentence, as Aravena gave it, which was shorter than it had been at the table: *From inside, it is the same thing to do.*

Under that, Ferrant's line. *Marks agree. Nothing to grade as to cause.*

Then the others slept, and Nakagawa read the unit's channels, late. Thermal. Imager: one upright shape on the line, one return. Sound channel: *Anstolik, passing*, one, and the gap that was the knock, owned and countersigned.

And then Nakagawa sat with the thing the survey lead had said in the second year, which was in no log, because Nakagawa had said it and not filed it.

*Nobody looked up.*

It had been a charge. Nakagawa had meant it as one, against all three of them and first against the one saying it: somebody stood behind us, close enough to touch the light, and we did not know. For two years it had been the party's shorthand for the whole of what the party had got wrong on Fliade. Walking in without knocking. Hearing a people and filing a rock. Not looking up.

Aravena had said something else at the table, late, when Egede had gone to bed and it was only the three of them and the wind. Nakagawa had asked for it, because it had been visibly on its way.

"I didn't look up either. On the silt. The whole forty-four."

"The order says the light stays low."

"The order says where the light points. It doesn't say eyes. I could have looked." Aravena had thought about it. "It wasn't for looking at. I don't know how I knew that. If I'd looked up I think I'd have been checking. Whether it meant me. Whether I was doing it right. And then some of me would have been in it."

Nakagawa had written that down too, under Aravena's name, and had not said what the survey lead was thinking, which was this.

Suppose a head had turned. On shift forty-one, in the second minute. Any head; Nakagawa's own. A light swung round, a shout, three people on their feet. The file would have had a sighting in it, the first ever made in the moment, and Nakagawa would have been glad of it for the rest of a career.

And it would have been eleven seconds long.

Nakagawa did not know that. It was not a finding. It was only that the survey lead could not make the other version come out any longer than that, however it was run.

The party had not looked up, and had got eleven minutes of somebody's standing, and two years later a stone on the line every shift, and this year a voice. Nakagawa was not going to file the first as the cause of the rest. That would be a guess wearing a count's clothes. But the thing the survey lead had carried for two years as the party's first failure had turned over on the table tonight and shown its other side, and the other side was not a failure. It was only what people look like when they are saying what they have to say, properly, to the one who is meant to keep it, and do not stop to see who else is there.

It had been an accident. Nakagawa was clear about that. The party had not been courteous on shift forty-one. The party had been busy.

And Nakagawa had said, in this room, on the night of the argument: a thing kept by accident is a thing kept on purpose from the day you know what it is.

There would be no order. Nakagawa turned that over once and was sure of it. You cannot order where three people look, and a survey lead who tried would deserve the log that came of it. The party would work at the junction tomorrow with its back to the third passage, as it had on every shift for four years, because that is where the work is.

It would only know, now, which way its back was turned. Nakagawa put the light out, and slept.

Shift two-eight-eight.

The stone. The squeeze. The silt, and the one on the line, in the open, still. Kneel. A hand from those feet. Step back. The thing said. The hand on the rock, and the rock, at once, a good one. The interval, waited on purpose. The crack, not running.

The junction, and the channel on again, and the unit humming to say so.

Aravena set down the light, and knelt to the unit, with the third passage behind.

It had always been behind. The unit sat at the middle of the junction, facing the way the party faced it, and you knelt in front of it, and the place where the third passage came in was over your left shoulder and a little back, at the edge of the light. Aravena had knelt here on two hundred and eighty-eight shifts and had known that on perhaps three of them.

"Log," said Aravena. "Shift two-eight-eight. Upper junction. Party of three. Knock answered."

The unit hummed.

Egede was at the far wall with the imager, saying things. Nakagawa was reading the ground, which looked like standing still. It was an ordinary shift, and the first reading of an ordinary shift is the floor.

Aravena took the temperature at the unit's base, and read the figure, and said it.

Not slowly. Aravena noticed the wanting to say it slowly, and clearly, and a little toward the left shoulder, the way you would say a thing to someone you thought might be there. And did not. A reading said for somebody is not a reading. You say it to the unit, at the pace it goes, in the voice it is used to, because that is how a log is told, and if it is ever anything else to anyone, that is not the one who is saying it's to arrange.

The unit hummed. It had heard. Aravena liked that, still. Four years, and it was still nice to be answered.

The figure was the figure. It did not look wrong. Aravena did not take it twice.

Behind, over the left shoulder and a little back, the third passage came in at the edge of the light, the way it does. There was the fungus, amber on one side and blue-green on the other. There was the dark past it. There might have been nothing there at all. There had been nothing there, as far as any channel said, on almost every shift the party had ever worked.

Aravena did not look round.

The unit's thermal channel would say, this evening, at the station, and that was soon enough to know, and it was the survey lead's to read. Until then it was only a junction, with three people in it saying what they had to say, and a floor at the temperature the floor was.

"Tracks," said Egede, from the far wall. "When you're ready."

"Ready," said Aravena, and told the unit the tracks.
