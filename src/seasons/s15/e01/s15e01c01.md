::: pov toval

The man from Dock Seven came up to the boundary desk in person, in the middle of the afternoon watch, and stood at the door of the Boundary Operations Room until Toval had finished the line he was on.

Toval knew the voice before he had placed the face. He had heard it on the desk's line a good many times, always at night, and once across two seats in a hall.

"You're not on until ten," Toval said.

"I'm not. They've had me in on my own time." The man was in work blues all the same. "The dock office is going through the maintenance returns. All of them, as far back as they go. There's a line for the exchanger behind the inner bulkhead. It shows nine changes and then it shows nothing, and somebody down there has noticed the nothing and wants a reason beside it."

"What reason have you given?"

"None yet. I want what the desk has. Written, with a time on it. I'll put it beside the line and they can make of it what they like."

Toval considered whether the desk could give that. It could. What the desk had was on the sheet, and the sheet was the station's.

The Dock Seven line was one line among the others. It was taken at each change of shift, in two columns. The first was the desk's own, from the certified instruments, and it read the failure point, where an officer had held the boundary and had not been recovered. The second was the Field Analyst's, from arrays nobody had been able to certify, and it carried a signal the certified instruments filed as noise. Toval had taken both at 1400, in order, when he reached them.

He took a slip and wrote the desk column as it stood. The failure point warm. Not decayed to the schedule a failure decays on. The figure, and the band it sat inside. He added the time and his name.

He did not copy the second column. It was carried under an assumption written at its head, and a slip had no head.

The man read it standing.

"That's what you told me the first night I got you on the line."

"It is what the desk had the first night."

"And every night since."

"At every change of shift," Toval said. "The desk doesn't take it in between."

"No." The man folded the slip once, along the middle. "You never did tell me it wasn't the exchanger."

"I couldn't support that it wasn't. I still can't."

The man nodded. He appeared to have expected nothing else, and to be content with it. Toval noted that, and then asked the thing he had meant to leave alone.

"Then why did you stop changing it?"

"Nobody told me to. I'd like that understood up here, because they may ask you." He looked at the slip and not at Toval. "It came up warm one night, same as ever. I had the exchanger half out of its cradle, and I thought, this would be the tenth of these. I put it back. I logged it warm. In the morning it was warm and nothing had come of it. The next time, I logged it warm and didn't get the tools out." He put the slip in his breast pocket. "I never decided it wasn't the exchanger. I ran out of ways to go on thinking it was."

Toval had no entry for that, and did not look for one.

"I've a new hand on nights," the man said, at the door. "A good one. He asked me last week which it was, with her. Dead, or not. He'd read the Council's piece, and he'd read what the High Captain said in the hall, and he wanted to know which of them the dock went by."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I didn't know. He thought I was keeping something back." The man very nearly smiled. "I used to ring up here and think the same of you."

He went down. Toval heard him as far as the turn of the stair.

The watch log took a visit in the same form as a call: the time, who had come, what was asked, what the desk gave. Toval wrote the time. He wrote *Dock Seven, night hand, in person*. Under what was given he wrote *copy of the desk column, Dock Seven line, as taken 1400*.

That left what was asked, and he stopped there with the stylus up.

He had had the answer ready from the moment he knew the voice. *Is that ours, or is that yours?* The desk reads the failure point. It doesn't read your bulkhead. He had given that answer on a good many nights, and it had not once satisfied anyone. Today nobody had asked for it.

He went back up the watch log to find the last time somebody had. He remembered most things he had written, and he was fairly sure of this one, but he had learned to check whether he was remembering or preferring. It was not in this book. He fetched the one before from the shelf, and then the one before that. The entry was where he had thought, in his own hand. *Dock Seven, night hand. Asked whether the warmth was the dock's or the desk's. Given: failure point warm, within band; exchanger not read.* It looked like every entry above it. Nothing about it said that it was the last.

He put the books back in their order.

There was a second thing, and it was in no book. He had come on at 1400 and taken the desk from a human Officer of the day watch. She had handed him the sheet and the state of two gauges and had gone to her dinner. In the first winter the humans coming off had usually said something at the handover about the Dock Seven line. It had been phrased as a question, and it had wanted the answer no. He did not know when that had stopped. He knew only that she had not said it today, and that he had not noticed until a man stood in the door with no question to put.

He tried to say, in order, what he could support.

The dock had stopped asking whether it was the exchanger. Nobody had told the dock that it was not. He had been on the desk the whole of that time and would have known. He could not support that the dock now held some other view. He had one man and a slip. He could support that a question had stopped coming, and that the desk had not answered it.

He would have liked to write that down as a result. He knew what he was. He had sat beside that line through a great many changes of shift wanting it to settle, and here was something that had settled without consulting him, on the wrong side of the desk. But the watch log had a place for what a caller asked and what the desk gave. It had no place for a question that had ceased to arrive, and he thought on the whole that this was correct. A desk that logged what it was no longer asked would soon be logging what it was pleased about.

Under *asked* he wrote *a copy*. On the line beneath, where the desk recorded a query on a reading, he wrote *none put*.

Nothing from the Dock Seven line had left the desk before to be set beside something else. He considered, because he was Toval, whether that was at last a use. A man had taken a slip away to account for a thing he had stopped doing. But the man had stopped some time ago, on his own, with an exchanger half out of its cradle. No decision had depended on the slip.

So it was not a use. He found he could leave that where it was. He took up the sheet at the next line.

::: pov syra

She came in at ten to ten and read the sheet before she spoke to him.

Every line was taken and entered. The Dock Seven line at 1400 was in his hand: a figure a little above the middle of its band, the band beside it, and the Field Analyst's four phrases. They were the four she had written at her own last turn.

Then the watch log. One visit. She read it once as an entry and once for the last two words, which were in the right place and which she had not seen on that line before.

"He asked for a copy and nothing else," she said.

"Yes. I had the other answer ready."

"I know which answer." She read the entry again. "This is accurate. You gave him the desk column only."

"The slip had nowhere to put the heading."

"Good."

He was collecting his things with less than his usual economy. She waited. It was generally quicker.

"The last time the dock asked whether it was theirs is two books back," he said. "I went and looked. I wrote the entry myself. I didn't know it was the last one."

"A last instance has no mark on it. It is identified by reading back."

"Did the desk do that? Stop them asking."

Syra considered what she could support.

"The desk gave the same sentences each time it was asked. I cannot support that this ended the question. I can support that it never answered it. You have also reached some view about the hall, and the Council's document, and what the station now holds. I can see it from here."

"I have one man and a slip."

"Then that is what you have."

He accepted it. He had become better at accepting a short inventory, and she had not told him so, since he would have wanted a figure for how much.

"He isn't waiting on it any more," Toval said. "The exchanger. I suppose that's all I meant."

"He was not waiting. He was changing an exchanger. It is a different occupation, and more tiring."

"Are we?" It was out before he had weighed it, as usual. "Waiting."

"No. Waiting is taking the reading between the changes of shift. You did it for one night. I did it for three weeks, at the end of my watches, and we have each written down what it was. The line is taken when the sheet is taken. Between times the desk has forty other things on it."

He thought about that for the time it took to close his bag, and seemed to find it sufficient, or at any rate checkable.

"I'll take it at six, then," he said. It was his turn. He went.

The Field Analyst came in at the change, as it had at every change, and stood at the end of the desk with one hand flat on the wall. It did not speak until she reached the line. She began at line one. She reached Dock Seven at fourteen minutes past ten.

The desk column first. Warm. The figure sat two divisions under his, and well inside the band. She wrote the figure and the band.

"Arrays' column," said Rook-7. "Variation continuing. Invariant held. Nothing addressed. None detected."

She wrote the four phrases under his four.

The preference was where she had left it. She looked for it, as she looked for it at each of her turns, because an analyst who has stopped checking for a known lean has only stopped knowing about it. She could not have said that it was smaller than on the morning she entered it, or larger. She had never had a measure. It pointed the way it had pointed. She would have preferred that the second column say something else tonight. It did not, and what was on the sheet was what Rook-7 had given her.

She finished the sheet. Rook-7 took its hand from the wall and went, and the room was hers.

It was also the day for the drift log. She took the book from the low drawer under the instruments, where the schedule was kept inside its cover.

An open entry was looked at again each quarter. The instrument was either recalibrated, and the entry closed against the new figures, or it was not, and the entry was carried forward with the date. Most entries were closed within the year. Three of the certified gauges were due tonight. She did them in the order the book had them, and closed two, and carried the third, which leaned low in cold weather and would be replaced when the stores had its like.

Hers was after it. It was the oldest open entry in the book. It had never been closed, since nobody had yet proposed a reference against which to recalibrate an analyst.

She read down it. The first entry was the one she had written on a spring morning: the direction, the magnitude that could not be measured, the second reading that had found it, the correction. Six words added at the foot, saying that she did not wish it gone. Under that, the quarters. Each was a date and a direction and one word.

She had asked for nothing in any of them.

She wrote the date. She wrote the direction, which had not changed. Under it she wrote the word the book allowed for an entry that was neither corrected nor withdrawn.

*Carried.*

The next reading was at six, and it was Toval's. She closed the drawer and took up the first of the night's gauges.
