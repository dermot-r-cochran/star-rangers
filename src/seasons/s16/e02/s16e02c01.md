::: pov toma-egede

Egede kept a list of everything the station was waiting to hear.

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there. On this shift three answers came down in one bag, from people who were not, and the someone had been waiting for them with a pen.

It was a short list. A station on a cold world does not ask for much, and most of what it asks for comes. Cells. Rations. A lens. Those went on a different list and were ticked on the day.

This one was for questions. It had been pinned inside the lid of the instrument case since the run of the year before, and it had three lines.

*1. Instrument desk. Docket, sound channel. Their part returned unopened. They will ask what the event is.*

*2. Boundary side. Ferrant's statement. Three lines.*

*3. Survey lead's request. A countersigning officer.*

None of them was ticked. Egede had looked at that list on most evenings for the better part of a year. A list with nothing ticked is not really a list. It is three things somebody is worried about, written down neatly.

The supply run came down in the usual wind, in the afternoon, with the party just up from the junction and the knock answered and the cold still in everybody's knees. Shift three-one-eight. Crates for the station. Fuel. Aravena went out to carry, because Aravena always did.

And one bag.

Egede turned it out on the end of the table. A folded sheet, with Egede's name on it, from the instrument desk. A page with the boundary side's mark, for Ferrant. And a page for the survey lead, with a mark Egede had seen on the cover of every year's summary, going the other way.

Egede counted them. Then counted the list.

"Three," said Egede.

"Three what?" said Aravena, coming in backwards with a crate.

"Three of three. Everything on the list. In one bag."

"Is that good?"

"It's never happened," said Egede, which was not the same thing, and opened the folded sheet first, because it was Egede's.

*Docket, relay unit, upper junction, sound channel. Class as returned: Other. Part received, seal whole. Test card received. Not a fault: accepted as stated. Dropout continues each shift and is read here as owned. No further parts will be sent.*

Egede liked them for that. They had been told there was nothing wrong, by a stranger on a cold world, in a box marked *Other*. And they had believed it, and then gone on reading the channel every shift anyway. It was exactly what Egede would have done.

Then the last line.

*Query. Interval "set by event, not by time". State the event.*

There was a space under it for the answer. It was one line long.

Egede had had the answer ready since the evening the last run lifted, and had said it aloud then, with the survey lead for a witness. But an answer you have had ready for a year is exactly the kind you stop checking. So Egede went down it once more, failure modes first.

Was it true? Yes. The channel had been off for every one of them. Ninety-nine now, from shift two-two-oh to this morning. Egede had been at the junction for all ninety-nine, and had felt the rock come up through the floor at every one. On one of them Egede had stood for forty-four minutes while something that was not a rock came up the crack.

But that was Egede. Ferrant had said it on the night of the argument: a boot keeps nothing. The query was not addressed to Egede's boots. It was addressed to the instrument specialist, about an instrument, and the instrument had been off.

Was it all there was? No. The rest was in the survey lead's column, which went to another desk, and in four people.

Would they like it?

"No," said Egede, aloud.

"No what?" said Aravena.

"They won't like it. I wouldn't. They'll know it's true, though, and that's the worst kind to be sent."

Egede wrote it small, to fit the line.

*Channel off for the event, by order. No reading is held by this specialist or by any instrument on the station. Interval entered each shift in the survey lead's column, countersigned.*

"You could tell them more," said Aravena. "You were there."

"I was there. The channel wasn't. They asked the channel."

"They might want to ask you."

"Then they'll need a different form." Egede folded the sheet along its own fold. "I looked. There isn't one."

Then Egede opened the instrument case and put a tick against line one. It was a small square tick, and it was the first on the list.

::: pov odile-ferrant

Hers was a page again.

She read it standing up, with her back to the rack, because a thing read before you have settled is a thing you still see. She had read the last one in the same place. That one had asked her a question and given her until the wind got up. This one was the answer to what she had said, and it had taken most of a year to come.

*Statement of continuing need. Section Lead O. Ferrant, boundary monitoring. Fliade station. Read.*

*Line 1. Accepted.*

*Line 3. Read, and agreed. The second ground is instrument discipline. It is not this side's to staff. It is referred to the planetary desk, with the survey lead's request.*

*This officer remains in attendance on her statement until that desk has answered.*

She read it twice.

They had agreed with her. It was an odd thing to be agreed with by a page. She had written line three so that her own side could find the thing was not theirs, and they had found it, in her words, and said so.

It had been the line that could send her home. It had not sent her home. It had sent the question down a corridor to another desk, and she had to admit that was where the question lived.

She noticed what the page did not say, because that was her trade. There was nothing about line two. Nobody on her side had written that the need was real, or that it was not. They had written whose it was to weigh. That was careful, and she marked it the way you mark good work by somebody you will never meet.

Then she turned the bag out again, properly, corners and all.

There should have been a fourth thing. It was not on Egede's list. It had gone up under seal in the third year, before Egede kept that list, and it was hers alone. She had asked whether a station like this one gets a reader for the instrument on its third shelf. She had asked to stay and be that reader until somebody said.

That was two years ago. The bag was empty. She folded it flat and put it with the empties.

She found she did not mind as much as she had expected. A side that takes two years over a question is at least not guessing. And the log was read. It had been read last night and it would be read tonight, by someone sitting on the floor in front of it, whatever anybody ruled or did not.

"Anything else in there?" said Egede, from the instrument case, with the pen still out.

"No."

"Then it's three of three."

She did not correct that. It was three of three on Egede's list, and it was a very good list.

"What does yours say?" said Aravena.

"That I was right about line three," said Ferrant. "I'm told I may stay until someone else decides what that means."

"That's good. Isn't it?"

She thought about how to say it truly.

"It's fair," she said. "I'll tell you whether it's good when I've seen the other page."

::::: scene 3

::: pov toma-egede

The other page was the survey lead's.

Nakagawa read it at the window, standing, with the wind working at the wall. Then read it again. Then came and put it on the station table, face up, where everybody could see it.

"This one is the station's," said Nakagawa.

*Review of attendance. Fliade station, surface party. In attendance past four years.*

*Request for a countersigning officer: received, with the boundary side's referral. An officer is not posted under a standing order until the attendance the order belongs to has been reviewed.*

*The survey lead will state continuing need, at grade, or the station will return with the last run of the year.*

*The survey window is not under review. It stands.*

Egede read it the way Egede reads anything. Where does it start. Where does it end. What is it built to notice.

Then Egede saw it, and pointed with the pen.

"That's her page."

Nobody said anything.

"It is. Last year's. *State continuing need, at grade.* It's the same page. They've changed the name on it."

Ferrant came and read it over the table, upside down, which she could do.

"It's our form," she said. "The boundary side's. Somebody has borrowed it." She read to the end. "They've borrowed it properly. I'd have changed the same words."

"Which desk?" said Aravena.

"The planetary desk," said Nakagawa. "The one my column goes to."

Egede knew that desk. Not to speak to. It was the *different desk*, the one Egede had complained about on the day the box came. The instrument desk got the channels and saw a hole in them. The planetary desk got the survey lead's column, with every dark in it, and a name, and a time, and Ferrant's line under that. It had never once taken the gap for a fault. It had had the name all along.

It had not mistaken anything now, either. It had read the request carefully and asked the next question.

"You asked them for one more person," said Egede slowly. "And they've asked about all of us."

"Yes."

"That isn't what you asked for."

"No. It is what I should have asked, at that desk." Nakagawa looked at the page as if it were ground. "I wrote to them for somebody to stand under an order of mine for as long as the order stands. It is fair to want to know first what the order is standing on. It is a fair page."

It was. That was the trouble with this run. Three answers had come down in one bag, and Egede could not find a thing wrong with any of them.

So Egede looked for a difference, and found one.

"Hers said *this run*. Ours says the last run of the year."

"I had one pack," said Ferrant.

Egede looked round the station. The rack, three shelves of it. The imager's case, and the spare's. The bench, which was the end of the table. Four berths. Down the long passage, the unit on its base and the imager on its stand.

Egede had a list of that too. It was the oldest list on the station, begun on the first day, because of the rule for this world: nothing is brought here that cannot be taken away again. Everything the party had ever carried onto Fliade was on it, with the day it came.

It was a good deal longer than one pack.

"What happens if you can't?" said Aravena. "State one."

"Then it says," said Egede. "It's the last thing on the line."

"All of us?"

"The station," said Nakagawa. "It says the station."

Aravena looked at the page, and then at the door, and did not say anything else. Egede saw that, and did not know what to do with it, and went back to the words.

"*At grade*," said Egede. "She has a grade. It goes with the patch. What's yours?"

Nakagawa looked at Ferrant.

::::: scene 4

::: pov odile-ferrant

"It isn't a number," said Ferrant. "Not for you."

She had not expected to be asked. She had expected, a little, to be blamed, and had been getting ready for that instead.

"On my side it's a form. You couldn't read it and you don't need to. On a page like this one it only means three things. Write what somebody who was never here could stand on. Say which parts you saw and which parts you think. And leave out what you only want."

"I have a column for that," said Nakagawa.

"I know. I've read it. It's the reason I'm not worried about your grade." She made herself go on. "That is my line three, on your table."

"It is my request on my table. Your line told them which desk."

"I'd write it again."

"I should think less of the file if you would not," said Nakagawa.

That was all. She had got ready for more than that, and now had nowhere to put it.

Outside, the run's crew were calling the wind. It would be up by dusk.

"When does it have to go?" said Egede. "There's no day on it. Hers had *this run*."

"Not on this run," said Nakagawa. "I am not going to answer that page standing up, on the day it came. If it wants a different answer it will look different in the morning. The relay can take it when it is ready."

"How many mornings?"

"As many as it needs. The last time it was four."

So the run lifted before dusk with the empties, and one folded sheet with one line added in a small square hand, and nothing at all from the survey lead.

Ferrant watched it go from the lee of the station, in her shell, with the patch on it. A year ago she had stood here and watched a run leave without her, on her own statement. Now the statement had been answered, and she was still here, and the question she had been asked had moved one place along the table.

She knew what it was like to be asked it. You sit on the floor and try to write one line that is true, and the first true line you find is an argument for going.

That evening the survey lead read the unit's channels at the table. Thermal. Imager: one upright shape on the line, one return. Sound channel: *Anstolik, passing*, two, and the gap that was the knock.

Ferrant sat on the floor at the third shelf with the handset on her knee, and read. The offset was where it had been since before the first knock. There was nothing else tonight.

She caught herself, closing the handset, hoping the survey lead would find something to write.

She put the hope down where she put all of those. An officer who is hoping sees things. And it was not her statement. Hers had been answered.

The countersign would be hers, though. Tomorrow morning, and the morning after.

::::: scene 5

::: pov toma-egede

Egede ticked the list last thing, when the others were in their berths and the station had gone quiet round the wind.

Line two. Line three.

Three ticks, small and square, one under another. It had never happened on any list of Egede's, in any posting. It ought to have been the best evening of the year. Egede looked for the feeling and found a smaller one in its place.

Under the ticks Egede wrote a fourth line. A thing that has been asked goes on the list, whoever it was asked of.

*4. Planetary desk. Station to state continuing need, at grade. The survey lead's.*

"That one is not yours."

It was Nakagawa, behind, reading over Egede's shoulder. That was the wrong way round. Egede had been doing it to the survey lead for a year and had never once thought what it was like.

"No," said Egede. "It's on my list, though. I'm in the station."

Nakagawa did not argue with that.

"I'm going to write you a case," said Egede. "For staying. Failure modes first."

"I know you are."

"It'll be a good one."

"Yes," said Nakagawa. "That is what I am afraid of."

The survey lead went to bed. Egede stayed a while longer with the lid of the instrument case open and the pen out.

Line four had no tick. A line with no tick was not new. What was new was that Egede could not tell which way a tick on that one would be good news.

There was no box beside it. Egede drew one, small and square, and left it empty, and went to find a clean sheet.
