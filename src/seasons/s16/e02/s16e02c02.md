*The survey lead will state continuing need, at grade, or the station will return with the last run of the year.*

This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there. This time the someone had been there for four years, and was asked by a page to say what for.

Nakagawa had slept on it. In the morning it looked the same.

That was the first morning, and it proved nothing. A thing has to look the same on more mornings than one. But Nakagawa got up before anyone had eaten, and stood at the table, and tried to write a line that was true.

*Upper levels, to the junction: surveyed. Filed to date.*

It was true. It was also an argument for going.

Ferrant had sat on the floor by the third shelf a year ago and done exactly this, with the wind getting up and a run waiting. Nakagawa had watched her from the table and thought it looked hard. It was harder than it looked.

"I've a case," said Egede, coming in with the cold and a sheet. "Three grounds. You're going to like it, and that's the fault in it."

"After the knock," said Nakagawa.

Shift three-one-nine. The long passage. The junction, in the fungus-light, with the third passage coming in at the edge of it.

Nakagawa knelt to the unit and told it the channel was off, and the time. The unit hummed once and was quiet. Aravena picked a stone, the size of two hands, and went down the third passage with the light held low.

The rise came up through the crack, a shape with nothing in it.

Then the wait.

Then the rock, up through the floor and the boots, at once. Then the rest of the interval, which Aravena stood through on the silt, on purpose. Then the light coming back up the passage, low, not running.

"Log," said Aravena, kneeling. "Shift three-one-nine. Upper junction. Party of three. Knock answered."

The unit hummed.

Nakagawa wrote the column, standing. *Sound channel at the junction off for the knock, by order. Interval stated.*

There was a page on the station table that asked what all of this was for. Nakagawa had just watched all of it done, exactly as on every shift, by people who had read the page. Nobody had hurried and nobody had been slow.

Nakagawa did not write that down. It was not an answer. It was only what the party was like.

Ferrant put her line under the column that evening, at the table. *Countersigned. Interval as stated. Nothing to grade.*

"That's a hundred," said Egede, who had the number.

"It's one," said Ferrant. "I've done it a hundred times."

::: pov nakagawa

Egede laid the case on the station table that evening, three sheets in a row.

"Failure modes first," said Egede. "One. The ground. We've four years of the upper levels. The Anstolik going through. The fungus. The floor at the junction, every shift. Four years is nothing for ground. You say so yourself. And you can't make a long record afterwards. Somebody has to have been there."

"How does it fail?"

"On your own rule. I can't leave the instruments to keep it without us. Nothing stays here that the planet would keep. So it's three people or no record. And the page doesn't ask whether there's a record. It asks whether there's a need. They'll say it's a good file, and thank us for it."

"Two."

"The count. Every shift since the first stone, we've knocked and been answered. Two hundred and fifty-six this morning. It's the longest unbroken series in the file. I don't know of another like it anywhere. Stop it, and nobody can start it again as the same one."

"And?"

"And it's a count of us." Egede looked at the sheet with dislike. "We'd be staying to count ourselves knocking."

Egede did not pick up the third sheet.

"Three is the line. Somebody stands on that line every shift, in the open. We know that. We've a frame of it for every day." Egede stopped, and it was a real stop, the kind Egede had learned at the junction. "If the station goes, then on the first shift after, they're there. And nothing comes. I've been down every failure mode I own. I haven't a class for that one. It's still the worst thing on the table. I'd stay for it. I want it in writing that I'd stay for it."

It was a good case. Nakagawa had known it would be. It had the specialist's whole weight in it. It had something else too, that the person who once proposed to put collars on a herd would not have thought to write.

And Nakagawa liked it. That was the fault in it, as Egede had said. Nakagawa could feel the liking, the way you feel a slope under snow.

"Ferrant."

Ferrant was on the floor at the third shelf with the handset on her knee. She did not look up at once.

"I've no page for a station's need," she said. "I'll say that first. I can grade them as instruments. It's the only way I know."

"Then do."

"One is my own first line. Within band, and stable. It's true, and it says you could go and lose only length. Two is an instrument reading itself. I wouldn't sign that on a card, and Egede wouldn't either."

"No," said Egede.

"Three." She turned the handset over. "Three is the only one on the table that is a need. And it isn't yours."

Nobody spoke.

"It's somebody's who has never stated it. You would be stating it for them, from up here, at your grade. And you have no reading of what your stone is to them. None. You switched a channel off so that you wouldn't have one. I can't grade a need that's stated for a person nobody asked. I've no page for that. I don't think anyone has."

Egede was quiet for about as long as it takes to say a name.

"Unasked," said Egede.

It was not said in the voice Egede uses for a word that is going into a document. It was quieter.

"I didn't say it," said Ferrant.

"No. It's my word. I know where it goes." Egede put a hand flat on the third sheet, and did not turn it over. "I'm not withdrawing it. It's still the worst thing on the table. I just can't make it ours."

"Keep all three," said Nakagawa. "Considered."

"And not adopted?"

"I have not said. There are three mornings yet."

Shift three-two-one. The third morning.

The stone. The squeeze, sideways, with the handheld's hard square against the breastbone and its sound off. The silt. The one on the line, in the open, still, grey, large.

Aravena had carried the page down the long passage in the head for three days. Each morning Aravena tried to leave it at the junction with the unit. It was not a thing to take through the squeeze.

Kneel. A hand from those feet. Step back. The thing said, going up at the end.

The hand on the rock. For the length of one breath, not knowing. Then the rock, at once, a good one.

Aravena waited out the rest of the interval, on purpose, and looked at what the low light showed. Two feet. A rock. The row of stones going off along the line to the side, each one a hand from the last, further than the light went.

Nobody down here had heard of a page.

That evening Nakagawa asked, the way Nakagawa asked things: plainly, at the station, after the others had eaten.

"This is the station's, so it is asked here. You have been at the line for every knock there has been. Is there a need there? One that I could state."

Aravena had known for three days that it was coming. Aravena had slept on it, which was the survey lead's way, and it worked.

"You're asking me because of the line."

"Yes."

"Then no."

Egede looked up from the far end of the table. Ferrant did not, which was how you knew she was listening.

"How many times is it? I don't count. You do."

"Two hundred and fifty-eight."

"Two hundred and fifty-eight. And nobody down there has asked me for anything. Not once. Not to come. Not to bring the stone. Not to come back the next day. I put it down and I'm answered. That's all it has ever been." Aravena had this in order, and was a little surprised to find it so. "Answering isn't asking."

"The long shift," said Egede. "Something was said to you for forty-four minutes."

"And I don't know a word of it. It might have been asking. It might have been something else. I stood there for all of it and I can't tell you which. You can't put that on a page as a need. I can't. I was the one there."

Nakagawa waited, because there was more.

"And you ruled on the rest yourself. What happens at the line is given to whoever knocks. It isn't the survey's. You switched a channel off for that. So if it isn't the survey's to keep, it isn't the survey's to stay for. You can't have it one way in the log and the other way on that page."

It was very quiet in the station. The wind had gone round.

"That is the answer I had," said Nakagawa.

"I know. You wanted to see if I'd say it."

"I wanted to know whether it was only mine."

Then the other thing came out, before Aravena had decided to let it.

"I'd stay. If it's wanting. I'd stay until there wasn't a stone left in the upper levels."

"That has a number," said Egede.

"Don't tell me it."

"I wasn't going to."

"It's a want," said Aravena, to the survey lead. "You've a rule for those."

"I have," said Nakagawa. "It is being used a great deal this week."

Later Aravena lay in the berth and listened. The wind. The third shelf, humming very slightly, read an hour ago by somebody on the floor in front of it.

Aravena thought about two grey feet at the edge of a low light. Once, a long time ago, Aravena had thought the one on the line was waiting for the party. Later Aravena had thought it was only where it stood.

Aravena did not know which. That had not changed. A page had come, and that had not changed at all.

Shift three-two-two. Four mornings. It had looked the same on every one.

Nakagawa got up before the others and stood at the table in the cold.

The rule for this world was half Nakagawa's own. *Longest windows. Lightest presence.* For four years the survey lead had taken *lightest* for something a party got better at. Fewer lamps. A light held low. One person at a door, and not three. A channel switched off. Each time there had been a little less of the survey on Fliade than the day before, and each time it had been right.

Nakagawa had never once followed the word along to see where it stopped.

And then the want.

Nakagawa looked at it properly, standing still, the way you look at a reading before you decide where it goes. It was large. It had been in the room for four mornings, behind every line the survey lead tried to write. Every ground Egede had put on the table, Nakagawa had wanted to be good. Every time Ferrant had opened her mouth to grade one, Nakagawa had hoped.

A reading its reader wants is entered last, or not at all.

Nakagawa had said that to Aravena a year ago, gently, about somebody else's reading. It would be a poor rule that was only for other people.

So the survey lead wrote, standing, before anyone had eaten.

*1. Upper levels, to the junction: surveyed, and filed to date. The party's conduct is as it has been since the third year. It knocks, works, and does not go down. Nothing in the file requires three people on the ground to keep it true.*

*2. At the line the party announces itself each shift and is answered. What is given there is given to the one who knocks. By the survey lead's rule of shift 220 it is not the survey's, and it is not stated here as the survey's need. The survey holds no request from the people below, to come or to remain, and has no means to ask for one.*

*3. The survey lead wants the station to stay. That is entered last, as a reading its reader wants. It is not a need. No continuing need is stated at grade.*

Nakagawa read the third line again.

It was the one that would send everybody home. Without it the other two were only a survey lead being careful. With it they were true. Nakagawa left it in.

Ferrant was up. She was generally up when something was being written. She read it standing, all three lines, and took longer over the third.

"That's at grade," she said.

"Yes."

"I'd sign it. I'd sooner not have to say so." She put it down squarely on the table. "They only asked you for a need. You could have left the third line off."

"Then it would be a statement with a hole in it," said Nakagawa, "and nobody's name on the hole. I had that from a boundary officer."

Ferrant very nearly smiled, and then did not, and went to put the water on.

Egede read it last, over the survey lead's shoulder, and it took Egede a long time.

"You've put that you want to."

"Yes."

"In a document."

"It was the only place I could put it where it would do no harm."

Egede thought about that. Then, because Egede is honest about instruments, including cases: "Mine. Keep it. Considered and not adopted, with the reasons. All three sheets."

"It is on the shelf already."

"I'm aware," said Egede.

The relay took the statement. It went the way Ferrant's letter had gone in the third year, up and out, to a desk a long way from any cave.

In the survey lead's own column, that shift: *Review of attendance read on four mornings. Statement made, three lines. No continuing need stated at grade. Specialist's case to remain: three grounds, considered and not adopted; kept. Standing orders unchanged.*

Then Nakagawa said the rest at the door, with a hand on it, because it needed saying once.

"We knock. We work. We do not go down. Today is the same as yesterday."

"Until when?" said Aravena.

"Until the last run of the year. And on every shift before it."

Aravena nodded, and picked up the unit, and settled its weight where it always sat.

Ferrant came down that shift, to countersign at the junction.

She walked in front of Aravena on the long passage, because Aravena was always last. For a while there was only the sound of four people going down into the dark.

Everybody on the roof knew about Ferrant's letter. It was a small roof.

"The old one," said Aravena. "From the third year. About whether the third shelf gets a reader. It wasn't in the bag."

"No."

"Will it come?"

"I don't think so. Not before the last run. They've had two years."

"So you never get an answer."

"It gets outlived." Ferrant said it over her shoulder, without stopping. "That's what happens to a question when the thing it's about goes first. There won't be a station for it to be about."

"Is that an answer?"

"No. I looked for a grade for it. I haven't one." A few steps. "I asked to read the third shelf until somebody ruled. Nobody has ruled. So I read it."

Aravena thought that was the most cheerful thing Ferrant had ever said, and did not tell her so.

The junction. Nakagawa knelt to the unit, and told it the channel was off, and the time. Aravena picked a stone, the size of two hands, without looking, the way you pick up a thing your hand knows.

The third passage. The squeeze, sideways. The silt.

The one on the line, in the open, still, grey, large.

Aravena had wondered, coming down, whether the thing said would come out different today. There was a date on the roof now. There had never been one before.

It did not come out different. Kneel. Set the stone down, a hand from those feet, beside yesterday's. Step back. Say the thing, going up at the end. It was the same thing it had always been, said the same way. Aravena had not brought the date through the squeeze. It would not have fitted.

The hand on the rock. One breath of not knowing.

The rock, at once. A good one.

Aravena stood through the rest of the interval, on purpose, with the light held low. Then the crack, not running.

At the junction the channel was on again, and Ferrant was at the edge of the light with her hands empty.

"Log," said Aravena, kneeling to the unit. "Shift three-two-two. Upper junction. Party of three and one. Knock answered."

The unit hummed. It was the fifth year, and it was still nice to be answered.

Egede was at the far wall with the imager, saying things. Nakagawa was reading the ground, which looked like standing still. It was an ordinary shift, and the first reading of an ordinary shift is the floor.

Aravena took the temperature at the unit's base, and read the figure, and said it.

The unit hummed.
