This is logged the way things are logged on Fliade: by someone who was there. On this shift somebody else was there as well, and the log can say how many and cannot say who.

Aravena knocked.

Shift three-four-seven. At the junction Nakagawa knelt to the unit, and told it the channel was off, and the time. The unit hummed once and was quiet. Aravena picked a stone, the size of two hands, without looking.

The third passage. The squeeze, sideways, the stone turned lengthwise and the handheld's hard square against the breastbone, with its sound off. Out onto the silt with the light held low and the breath not back.

The one on the line, in the open, at the place where the row of stones ends. Aravena came out looking at the light, as you do with the breath not back, and the light was on two grey feet.

It found them the way it does. Then Aravena's hand took the light to the side, an arm's length along the line. The hand had done that at every knock since the shift there were two. Aravena had never decided it and had never filed it. On every one of those shifts it had taken about a breath: silt, lit flat, and the light coming back.

This time it was not silt.

It was feet.

The breath had not come back from the squeeze. Now it stopped where it was.

There had been a shape in that place once. The light had gone round it, a thin bright line with nothing inside. Behind it the silt had been lit as if nobody stood there. Aravena had not forgotten what that looked like.

This was not that. The light lay on these feet the way it lay on the others. It was on fur, grey, with silt in it. Behind them a shadow went back across the ground, long and black, as it does from anything a low light is on.

Somebody was standing there.

The rest came in at the edges, the way it does when you have been looking at a lamp. Grey. Upright. In the open, beside the one who answers.

The light went up, slowly. Not far. A light held low goes as far as it goes: the feet, and the fur above them, and a hand hanging, and then grey going up into the edge of the lamp. Aravena did not lift it past that. It had not been lifted past that for the one who answers, on any shift. It was not going to be for anyone else.

But it stayed.

The hand had always known what came next, because next had always been silt. It did not know what to do about somebody. So the light stayed where it was, and Aravena stood behind it with the stone held to the front, and looked.

Whoever it was stood on the line. Not past it, and not below it. Facing the crack. Still, with the whole body, the way the one who answers is still. It did not turn. An arm's length away the other feet did not turn either.

*Who are you?*

It was not a thing to say. There was no way to say it, and a knock is not for that. Aravena thought it once. Then Aravena thought of a profile in the file, the smallest one, and of what the log calls it. Aravena did not say that either.

Then the next thought, which was plainer. You are holding a light on a person.

The breath came. The hand remembered itself. The light went down the fur and along the silt, low, to the feet it knew.

After that it was a knock. Aravena made sure of it.

Kneel. The stone set down at the end of the row, a hand from those feet, beside yesterday's. There was nothing to choose. The row was there, and the stone went at the end of it. Stand. One step back toward the crack. The thing said, facing the one who answers, going up at the end.

It came out the way it always came out. Aravena noticed that, and was a little surprised.

The hand on the rock. One breath of not knowing.

The rock, at once. A good one.

It came through the silt and the boots. From the other side there was nothing. Nobody had set a stone there. Nobody answered from there.

Aravena stood out the rest of the interval, on purpose, as on every shift. The usual one, and no more. The light lay where it lies: two feet, a rock, the new stone. Aravena did not take it to the side again. It had been there once, for a long time. Once was looking. Twice would have been checking.

Then the crack, not running.

You go in sideways, and for a moment the line is at the edge of what you can see, in the light that has no lamp behind it. Two of them. Both on the line. Neither had moved.

::: pov nakagawa

The rise was late.

Nakagawa stood by the unit with the timepiece running, because the column needs the interval. Egede was at the far wall with the imager. The rise comes up the third passage at the same point on every shift, a shape with nothing in it. Everybody at the junction knows the point. It is in the boots.

The point went by.

Egede looked up, and did not say anything.

The rock had been late once, by forty-four minutes. Nakagawa could not remember the rise being late. The rise was the party's own.

Most of a minute. Then it came up through the crack, going up at the end, the same as on any shift. The unit on its base had no channel open for it.

Then the wait. Then the rock, up through the floor and the boots, at once.

"That part's ordinary," said Egede quietly.

Then the rest of the interval. Then the light coming back up the passage, low, not running, most of a minute after its time.

Aravena came out with a face Nakagawa had seen at this passage mouth before, two years ago, and did not say anything yet.

Nakagawa knelt and told the unit the channel was on, and the time. Aravena knelt after, because there was a shift to log.

"Log. Shift three-four-seven. Upper junction. Party of three. Knock answered."

The unit hummed.

Aravena stayed kneeling.

"Two on the line. One answered."

The unit hummed again, once, low. It was all it had.

"Tell me," said Nakagawa. "In order."

Aravena told it in order. The squeeze. The feet of the one who answers. The light going to the side, as it does, and finding feet. The light going onto them, and up, as far as it goes. Fur. A hand. A shadow behind on the silt.

"Onto," said Egede. "Not round."

"Onto. It was somebody."

"How big?"

"I don't know."

"You were stood in front of it."

"I had a lamp on its feet, and I was looking where the lamp was. It was upright. It was grey. I didn't look to see how much of it there was."

Egede began to say something. Nakagawa looked across, and Egede did not.

On the line, facing the crack, still. How long the light stayed, which Aravena could not say and did not guess at. The light back. The kneeling. The stone at the end of the row. The step back. The thing said. The rock, at once, from the one who answers. Nothing from the other. The interval. The going. Both still there.

"Which one answered?" said Egede.

"The one who answers."

"There were two warm ones. How do you know which was which?"

It was a fair question. Ferrant had asked one very like it at this junction, about a different two.

"The one at the end of the row," said Aravena. "Where the stone goes. I've looked at those feet on every shift there's been. The other one was on the other side. There aren't any stones on that side."

Egede had the handheld by then. Aravena had unclipped it and held it out. Egede ran the frames back on the junction screen, standing, with the survey lead reading past one shoulder for once.

Optical: two. Thermal: two.

"Two returns," said Egede. "Last time it was two shapes and one return. This is that the other way round." Egede counted, without being asked. "Two hundred and six shifts that handheld's gone down with the stone. One with two and one. Two hundred and four with one and one. And today."

"Yes."

"I can't head that row."

"No."

Egede looked at the screen a moment longer, and then asked the other thing.

"What did you say down there?"

It had been asked once before on this roof. That was in the third year, by Ferrant, who was told it was not in the log, and said *good*.

"Not what, then," said Egede, seeing the survey lead's face. "Who to. There were two of them."

Aravena thought about it honestly.

"I said it the way I say it. Facing the one who answers."

"And the other one?"

"Was an arm's length off. I don't whisper."

"Then it heard."

"I'd think so. I don't know what I sound like from there."

"That is which way the one who knocked was facing," said Nakagawa, "and how far off the other stood. Both are in the frames. Neither is what was said. That is not in the log. It is not going to be asked for standing up at a junction."

Egede took that, and did not make the face.

Nakagawa wrote the log there, standing, in the survey lead's own hand. Aravena's line to the unit was Aravena's. This one never had been.

*Party announced itself at the line: one member, one stone of the upper levels, set down on the line and left; handheld carried, pointed low, sound channel off. Two were present on the line, in the open, upright, at arm's length of each other. The one who answers was in the place where the stone is set on every shift, and answered: a shift of rock at the edge of the silt. The second did not answer, did not move, and was present when the light withdrew. Handheld: two upright shapes, two thermal returns, a shadow thrown by each. Nothing said is recorded. The line was not crossed by any party.*

Egede read it over the shoulder, which was the right way round again.

"*The second.* Second what?"

"The second of two."

"You wrote *shape*, last time."

"Last time a shape was all the handheld had. This has a return, and fur, and a shadow. I am not going to call it a shape. I am not going to call it by a name either." Nakagawa capped the pen. "*Second* is as far as the frames go."

In the survey lead's own column: *Sound channel at the junction off for the knock, by order. Interval stated.* It was longer than yesterday's by most of a minute.

There is another number in that column. It is the survey lead's own and belongs to no instrument. It counts the shifts on which the log says one on the line. Nakagawa looked at where it had stood yesterday, and did not add to it.

They told her at the station door, coming in out of the wind.

"Two," said Egede, before it was shut. "Two returns."

Ferrant put down what she was holding.

She did not ask for the handheld. She noticed that she wanted to, and that was why she went first to the third shelf. She sat down on the floor in front of it and opened the gravimeter's log at the morning.

There had been two on that line once before. On that shift her instrument had had something at the same minute. It was in her file, at its grade, with the frames. So there was one question on the roof tonight that was hers. Nobody had asked it yet. She meant to have the answer before anybody did, and before she had seen a frame. An officer who goes to the interesting picture first reads the quiet instrument afterwards with the picture still in her eyes.

Egede gave her the minute, off the handheld's marks. She read it. Then the hour on either side. Then the minute again.

"Before anyone asks," she said, to the table. "The offset is where it has been since before your first knock. There is nothing at that minute. There is nothing in the hour round it."

Nobody had asked. Egede had been about to.

"So it isn't what it was last time."

"I've told you what my instrument has. I haven't told you what anything isn't. I don't do that from one log." She closed the handset. "You don't need me for it. Your lamp went onto it."

Then she took the handheld.

There were more frames than usual. Silt. Two feet at the row's end, which she had for every shift and did not count. The slide to the side.

Feet.

She held the frame. Optical: fur, lit, with the grain of it showing, and a shadow going back across the silt. Thermal, at the same mark: a return. Warm, and upright as far as the frame went.

She read one page for a living. For two years she had had a frame of that arm's length on every shift, with nothing on it. What her page is about does not take a lamp. This took it like anybody. A child could have seen that, and she would have said so in any file.

So it was not hers. That was easy to say and odd to feel. There were two on the line, and nothing of it was boundary data.

She went on through the frames. The lamp went up a little, as a lamp does that is held low. A hand, hanging. Grey to the edge of the light. Then it stopped going up, and stayed.

She read the marks, because marks were a thing she could give.

"Your look to the side takes three or four seconds," she said to Aravena. "Today it took fifty-two."

Aravena was on the other berth with a strap and a needle.

"It felt longer."

"It usually does."

Egede had come to stand behind her, which she allowed.

"Size," said Egede. "Is it the size of the other one? Is it smaller? You've got both."

"I've one, and then the other. Never both in a frame, until the end."

She ran it on. The lamp coming back. The stone going down. A hand on the rock, and the rock moving. Then the interval, with the frame quite still on two feet and a stone. Then the turn, and the last frames before the crack: two upright greys, at the bottom of what the optical could do, with the frame swinging.

"There. I can count from that. Two. I wouldn't measure a table from it."

"The feet, then."

"The first one's feet have a stone beside them, which is a scale of a sort. The other's have silt. And the handheld was on somebody's chest who had just come through a squeeze." She looked up. "I could give you a figure. It would have a band on it so wide that you could do nothing with it. And if I gave it to you narrow, what would you lay it against?"

Egede did not say. She thought Egede knew.

She filed the frames as frames, one optical and one thermal to a mark, at what a frame is entitled to. They went in her file because the handheld was hers. No reading went with them.

Then she got up and wrote her line under the survey lead's column, at the table.

*Countersigned. Interval as stated. Nothing to grade.*

She had written it a hundred and twenty-seven times before. Tonight it covered rather more than usual, and it was still true.

Egede had pulled shift forty-one before anyone had eaten.

Nakagawa knew what it was before it reached the table. Thermal channel, the unit on its base, the second year. A profile, warm, upright, at the edge of the working light where the third passage comes in. Eleven minutes. Against it in the file was the thing the log has for a name. *Individual Four.*

Egede laid a frame from the handheld beside it. Today's, thermal, from the mark where the lamp stopped.

"Failure modes first," said Egede. Nobody had asked for them. "They're different instruments. That's the unit's channel, across the junction, the whole of him, head to floor. This is a handheld pointed low, from a few steps. I've all of one and the bottom of the other. They're three years apart. And we've no method for what three years does to one of them. We never have had."

"No."

"So I can't lay one against the other. I know I can't. I wanted them on the same table."

"And now that they are?"

Egede looked at the two of them for a while.

"They're both warm. That's all I've got. I've never had so little off two readings."

It was honestly said, and it had cost something. Nakagawa remembered a specialist who once wrote *artefact* seven times sooner than leave a line empty.

"He was small," said Egede. "Four. That's how we told that one from the rest. *Smaller.* You let me keep that word."

"In the second year. It was the measurement, in the second year."

"And I haven't one tonight. Ferrant won't give me a height off those frames, and she's right. And if she did, I wouldn't know what to do with it. Say it's big. Then it's any of them, or it's that one three years on. Say it's small. Then it's that one, or it's another small one we never told apart." Egede turned the frame face down. "It comes out the same whichever way I run it."

"Yes."

"It might have been."

"It might," said Nakagawa. "It might have been anyone below that margin, and we do not know how many there are. That is a guess. I should like it as much as you would, which is the reason it is not going in."

Nakagawa opened the file at the name, and read what was under it to the end. Then asked the question a survey lead has to ask of a file. Did it want anything added?

The party had seen a second one at the line. It could not say it was that one. It could not say it was not. Either would be a line about something nobody had seen.

Nakagawa wrote nothing under the name, and shut the file.

"Tomorrow, then," said Egede, to the other berth. "If it's there again. Lift the lamp. One frame, feet to head, with the other one in it. Then I'd have something."

"The light is held low," said Nakagawa. "That order is older than the handheld."

"Then we don't find out."

"A knock is not changed to find out. You heard that in the second year. There was a stone from the roof on this table at the time."

Egede had. Egede made the face that meant the point was taken.

Then Nakagawa looked across at the other berth, and waited.

It was Aravena's to ask, if it was anybody's. Not for the lamp. For something smaller. To take the light to the side a second time, on the way out. To stand a little longer tomorrow. Nakagawa would have refused each of them, and had the refusals ready, and would not have thought the less of the asking.

Aravena pulled a thread through.

"You'd want to know," said Egede. "You more than any of us."

"Yes."

"So?"

"I looked," said Aravena. "Once. Fifty-two seconds, apparently. That was already longer than I meant."

Nobody said anything after that. Nakagawa found the refusals were not needed, and put them away.

Later, with the others in their berths, the survey lead read the unit's channels, as on every evening. Thermal. Imager: two upright shapes on the line, two returns. Sound channel: *Anstolik, passing*, three, and the gap that was the knock.

It had taken a second shape on that line, two years ago, to make anyone on the roof write down how many were there to answer. The number had been one ever since. Tonight it was two, and both were warm, and the survey could count them and could not tell one from another.

Nobody had told the party who. Nobody had told it anything. The log said so, as on every shift.

Nakagawa signed it. There was nothing in it that sleeping on would change.

Aravena lay in the berth and listened to the wind, and to the third shelf, and thought about feet.

In the second year Aravena had asked the dark a thing, sixteen times, at the bottom of the long passage. It was for the one who carried the unit. Then there was a stone, and somebody on a line to answer it. After that the asking went down in the mornings, because that was where anybody was.

The one on the line was not Four. Aravena had known that since the first stone. It was somewhere to send a thing, and it was not quite where the thing had been sent. Aravena had gone on for three years without knowing whether anybody down there told anybody anything.

Today somebody else had stood an arm's length off, for the whole of it.

It might have been Four. Then it had been said in Four's hearing, once, out loud. Or it might have been anybody, and then nothing was different. Aravena lay and looked for some way that the two would look unlike from up here. There was none. Either way it was the same two feet in a lamp.

It had not been said to that one. Aravena was clear about that. The stone went where the stone goes, and the face went with it. You do not turn round in the middle of a knock and say a thing at somebody because you hope you know who they are. That would be some other thing to do. Aravena did not know what it would say.

Whoever it was had been there. Being there is not the same as being told. Aravena had said that once, about a unit.

But it had stood the way Aravena stood on the long shift, with all of itself. Aravena knew what that was like from inside. It did not say who.

Aravena slept.

Shift three-four-eight.

The stone, without looking. The squeeze. The silt.

Two feet, at the end of the row.

The light found them. Then the hand took the light to the side, an arm's length along the line, the way it did.

Silt. Lit flat, the way silt is when nobody stands on it.

The light came back.

Kneel. The stone, a hand from those feet, beside yesterday's. Step back. The thing said, going up at the end. The hand on the rock, and the rock, at once, a good one. The interval. The crack, not running.

"Log," said Aravena, kneeling to the unit. "Shift three-four-eight. Upper junction. Party of three. Knock answered."

The unit hummed.

"One?" said Nakagawa, reading the ground, not looking up.

"One," said Aravena.

Nakagawa wrote something, standing. There was a count in that column. Aravena had never asked what it stood at.

Aravena did not ask now.
