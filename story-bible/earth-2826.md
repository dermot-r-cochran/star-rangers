## Purpose and rules

### Why this file exists

The record has written Earth's history and none of its geography. The Crisis
Centuries are dated and their losses named; the repair has its institutions,
its discipline and its doctrine; Venice floats; New London went up because the
British Isles were drowning. No page says how warm the planet is, how high the
sea stands, what is left of the ice, or what a coast looks like now. Threshold
Station has been on Earth since PR #1405, and the record's two boundaries that
can be stood on share a tidal shore with Irish names. A chapter set there will
need a tide, a wind, a season and a horizon, and an image of that coast will
need a prompt. Without a note, the generator supplies the drowned-world
cliché, and the writer has nothing to set against it.

### What the record already holds (derived)

### How the figures were chosen

**Conservative, and honest about it.** Every figure comes from the IPCC Sixth
Assessment Report (AR6, Working Group I, 2021) and the literature it assesses,
read for the low-to-middle pathways (SSP1-2.6 to SSP2-4.5) and the
multi-century commitments they leave. AR6 projects in detail to 2100 CE, more
loosely to 2300, and for sea level gives a 2,000-year commitment; 2626 CE is an
interpolation across the slow, committed processes, so ranges are kept and a
choice inside one is marked proposed with its reason.

**The repair changes the air faster than the ice** (derived, real physics).
Carbon drawdown can lower the atmosphere's carbon dioxide within decades to a
century, and surface temperature follows. Ice sheets, deep-ocean heat and deep
oxygen answer over centuries to millennia, and they do not run backwards when
the air cools: an ice sheet lost in two centuries takes tens of millennia to
regrow. This one asymmetry reconciles most of the record. The air was
repaired. The coastline was not, and could not have been.

## The physical figures

Each figure is **proposed**: a value chosen inside the published range, his to
overrule. The line beneath each gives its basis.

### Warming: a peak near 2 °C, held below it

- **Proposed.** Global surface temperature peaked at about **2 °C** above
  pre-industrial during the Crisis Centuries, overshooting it for some decades
  (perhaps to 2.2 or 2.5 °C at the worst), and the drawdown has held it since
  in a band of about **1.5 to 2 °C**. In 2826 UCSD the planet is warmer than
  our own time, by roughly half a degree to a degree, and steady.
- *Basis:* AR6 SPM Table SPM.1 gives 1.3–2.4 °C (SSP1-2.6) and 2.1–3.5 °C
  (SSP2-4.5) for 2081–2100 CE; AR6 Chapter 4 finds that sustained net-negative
  carbon dioxide emissions bring temperature down slowly. The crisis's *"heat
  and drought events compressed into decades"* reads as an overshoot.
- Whether the drawdown has lowered the plateau, or only held it, is an open
  item below.

### Sea level: about 3 m, still rising slowly

- **Proposed.** Global mean sea level in 2626 CE stands about **3 m** above
  its early twenty-first-century level, inside a band of roughly **1.5 to
  4.5 m**, and is **still rising**, at a few millimetres a year: perhaps 20 to
  50 cm a century, about the rate of the early twenty-first century.
- *Basis:* AR6 SPM section B.5 and Chapter 9: by 2300 CE, 0.3–3.1 m under SSP1-2.6
  and higher under middle pathways; over 2,000 years, 2–3 m for 1.5 °C and
  2–6 m for 2 °C, rising for millennia after. Summing the parts for six
  centuries at about 2 °C gives thermal expansion of roughly half a metre to a
  metre, mountain glaciers about 0.3 m, Greenland 0.5–1.5 m and Antarctica
  0.3–1.5 m.
- **The honest note.** 3 m sits in the upper half of the band for a 2 °C
  world. It is chosen because the record's crisis overshot (above) and
  because an ice sheet that has started to lose mass keeps losing it after the
  air cools. A figure of 2 m would also be defensible and would change no
  page; 4 m would start to reach cities the record has not lost.
- **The tail, named.** If West Antarctica's marine ice had proved unstable in
  the way AR6 rates *low likelihood, high impact*, the figure would be 5 m or
  more. This note does not take the tail.
- *Derived:* Venice's *"slow, continuing rise"* (`venice.md`), on a sinking
  lagoon in a semi-enclosed sea, is this rate seen locally.

### The ice

- **Arctic sea ice in summer: gone** (proposed). Late-summer ice is absent in
  most years and the Arctic Ocean is open water from August to October; winter
  ice still forms, thinner and younger than ours, every year. *Basis:* AR6 SPM
  section B.2: practically ice-free in September at least once before 2050 CE in
  every scenario assessed, and more often with more warming. At the lower end
  of the plateau, a summer with some ice left is possible, and unremarkable.
- **Greenland: reduced, not gone** (proposed). The ice sheet has lost perhaps
  a tenth to a fifth of its mass. Its margins have retreated tens of
  kilometres in the south and west, leaving new fjords and a wider ice-free
  fringe of bare rock, meltwater rivers and young tundra; the interior dome
  stands. *Basis:* AR6 Chapter 9: the threshold for eventual near-complete
  loss lies somewhere around 1.5–2 °C (low confidence), and loss at that
  level takes many millennia. Six centuries is the opening of that process,
  not its end.
- **West Antarctic Ice Sheet: retreating, not collapsed** (proposed). The
  Amundsen Sea glaciers have pulled back their grounding lines and contributed
  some tens of centimetres to a metre; the sheet as a whole stands. East
  Antarctica is near balance. *Basis:* AR6 Chapter 9: sustained retreat in the
  Amundsen sector is possible at 1.5–2 °C; collapse of the whole marine sheet
  is a multi-millennial and uncertain outcome.
- **Mountain glaciers: mostly gone** (proposed). The Alps, the Pyrenees, the
  tropical Andes, East Africa and New Guinea have none, or a few shaded
  remnants. The Himalaya and Karakoram keep their highest ice, much reduced.
  The large high-latitude fields (Alaska, the Canadian Arctic, Svalbard,
  Patagonia) persist at a fraction of their old size. *Basis:* AR6 Chapter 9
  and the global glacier projections it assesses: about a third to a half of
  glacier mass lost by 2100 CE even near 2 °C, with small and low-latitude
  glaciers going first and committed loss continuing for centuries.
- *Derived:* **peak water has passed.** Rivers once fed by glacier melt in the
  dry season (the Indus, the Andean rivers, the Rhône) ran high while the ice
  wasted, during the crisis, and have run lower in late summer since.

### Permafrost

- **Proposed.** The southern and discontinuous permafrost of Siberia, Alaska
  and Canada has thawed to depth across a broad band; continuous permafrost
  survives further north, shrunken and thawing slowly from the top. The
  thawed land is thermokarst country: lakes in collapsed ground, leaning
  forest, river banks that slump each summer, and Arctic coasts of ice-rich
  soil cut back by kilometres where open water now meets them in autumn
  storms. Boreal forest has moved north into former tundra, unevenly.
- *Basis:* AR6 Chapter 9 and the IPCC ocean and cryosphere report (SROCC, 2019): near-surface permafrost volume falls
  by about a quarter for each degree of warming, and the carbon it releases is
  committed for centuries.
- *Derived:* part of why the repair *"is not finished"* is that the thawing
  north is still releasing carbon the drawdown must answer.

### Monsoons, drought belts and storms

- **Proposed, from AR6's regional assessments (Chapters 8 and 11, and the
  Atlas).**
  - **Wet gets wetter and dry gets drier, broadly.** The subtropical dry
    belts have widened toward the poles. The Mediterranean basin, southern
    Africa, south-western North America, south-western Australia and parts
    of Chile and Central America are drier in their soils and their summers
    than ours, and their farming has moved with the water.
  - **The monsoons are heavier and less regular.** South Asian and East Asian
    monsoon rainfall is greater in total, falls in harder bursts, and varies
    more from year to year. The West African monsoon is wetter in the east of
    the Sahel and less reliable in the west.
  - **Tropical cyclones are not more numerous**, but more of them are
    intense, they carry more rain, and their surges ride a higher sea.
- *Derived:* **a storm surge on a 3 m higher sea is a different animal.** AR6
  and the IPCC ocean report (SROCC, 2019) find that half a metre of rise turns
  many coasts' once-a-century high water into an annual one. At 3 m, the old
  extremes are the ordinary winter tide. This, not mean sea level alone, is
  what made the crisis a *fragmentation*: coasts that would have been wet were
  broken by the storms that rode the new water.

### Ocean circulation (the AMOC)

- **Proposed.** The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, the deep
  overturning that carries warm surface water north and returns it cold at
  depth, is **weakened by roughly a quarter to a half** against its
  pre-industrial strength, and **has not collapsed**. It has been slowly
  steadying since the drawdown cooled the air, and may be recovering a
  little.
- *Basis:* AR6 SPM section C.3 and Chapter 9: very likely to decline over the
  twenty-first century in every scenario; medium confidence of no abrupt
  collapse before 2100 CE; longer-range model runs at low-to-middle forcing
  show declines of about 25–50 % over centuries, with partial recovery as
  forcing falls. Collapse is the named uncertain tail, and the record's
  choice about it is an open item below.

#### The Gulf Stream and Ireland

- **The Gulf Stream is not the AMOC, and it persists** (derived, real
  oceanography). The Gulf Stream is a western boundary current, driven mostly
  by the wind over the subtropical North Atlantic and held against the
  American coast by the Earth's rotation. It runs in 2826 UCSD as it runs now.
  What has weakened is the overturning that pulls part of its warm water on
  past Ireland as the North Atlantic Drift and sinks it in the Nordic and
  Labrador Seas. So the current still flows *towards Ireland*; less of its
  heat arrives.
- **Ireland has warmed less than the world** (derived). AR6 and the models it
  assesses show a *warming hole* over the subpolar North Atlantic, south of
  Greenland and west of Ireland, where the weakened overturning delivers less
  heat and the sea surface has warmed little or not at all. Ireland, downwind,
  has warmed less than the global average: **proposed**, by about 1.5 °C
  above pre-industrial in a world near 1.5–2 °C, half a degree more than
  Ireland had warmed by our own time.
- **Winters wetter and stormier** (derived, proposed in degree). AR6 Chapter 8
  and the Atlas project wetter winters for north-west Europe, with heavier
  rain in single events, and a winter storm track that reaches further into
  Europe (medium confidence). The steeper temperature contrast between a
  cool North Atlantic and a warmer continent feeds those storms. For the
  coast, the consequence is the surge point above: on a higher sea, the
  winter storm decides where the water goes, and mean sea level only decides
  where it starts.
- **Heavier rain in the west, drier summers in the east** (proposed, from the
  regional projections for Ireland that build on AR6). The Atlantic west takes
  the winter rain; the east and south-east have drier, warmer summers, with
  dry spells that a twenty-first-century farmer would call droughts and a
  twenty-ninth-century one calls July.
- **Still temperate and maritime, not cold** (derived). With the overturning
  weakened and not collapsed, the regional cooling it causes is smaller than
  the warming around it. Ireland is mild, wet and windy, as ours is: a little
  warmer, wetter in winter, rougher at sea. The frozen Europe of film and
  headline is the collapse case, which this note does not take.
- *Derived, for the reconciliation below:* a temperate maritime coast with a
  modest warming, a below-average sea-level rise (see *Ireland and the
  British Isles*) and hard rock under most of it is a coast a repair can
  rebuild. Its threat is the winter storm, which can be engineered against
  harbour by harbour. The English lowlands faced the same storms on soft
  ground that was sinking.

### Ocean chemistry: held by the repair

- **Acidity: held** (derived from canon; the figure proposed). The repair
  holds *"ocean alkalinity ... within bounds"*, and SSDC *"still hold[s] the
  ocean chemistry where the repair left it"*. Proposed: surface pH is held
  near **8.05**, about our own time's value, against the 8.2 of the
  pre-industrial ocean. It was lower during the crisis. Holding it is
  continuous work, by alkalinity added at sea, and it would drift down within
  decades if it stopped.
- **Oxygen: held at the surface, not at depth** (derived, real
  oceanography). AR6 SPM section B.5 finds deep-ocean deoxygenation irreversible on
  centennial to millennial scales. The repair can hold oxygen in shelf seas
  and the surface layer; the deep ocean ventilates over centuries and is still
  carrying crisis-era water. The oxygen minimum zones are larger than the
  pre-industrial ocean's. This is the physical ground under the Crisis
  Centuries page's *"smaller, faster-maturing fish assemblages"* in deep and
  open water.

## What the figures do to coasts, deltas and cities

### In general (derived, from the figures and real geography)

- **The three metres are not evenly spread.** Local rise differs from the
  global mean by land movement and by the sea's own gravity. Ground still
  sinking after the last ice age (southern England, the Netherlands, the US
  mid-Atlantic coast) or by compaction (every great delta) has seen more;
  ground still rising (Scotland, the north of Ireland, Scandinavia) has seen
  less. A shrinking ice sheet also pulls less water towards itself, so
  Greenland's loss raises the sea less near Greenland, and less around the
  North Atlantic's European shore, than far away; Antarctica's loss raises it
  most in the northern hemisphere.
- **The deltas went first and furthest.** The great river deltas lie within a
  few metres of the sea and are compacting under their own weight: the
  Ganges–Brahmaputra, the Nile, the Mekong, the Mississippi, the Rhine–Meuse,
  the Yangtze, the Niger. Three metres, with surges on top, puts most of their
  seaward land in the tide. *Proposed:* each became a working wetland, managed
  for sediment, fish and storm defence, with its people moved inland or onto
  mounds and platforms; none is a dead sea.
- **Cities rode, moved, or let go** (proposed, from canon's *"coastlines
  rebuilt to ride water rather than resist it"*). The repair's doctrine was
  not the dike. A city whose old core mattered enough, and whose sea floor
  could hold moorings, floated its core as Venice did; a city with high ground
  behind it moved its living districts uphill and let the water have its
  waterfront; and low ground that could not be floated or defended for a
  cost that would still make sense in a century was given back. A few great
  barriers were kept where they already stood and could be held for centuries,
  and the doctrine counts them as the exception.
- **Low coasts are wider and wetter; rocky coasts look much as they did.**
  Where land slopes gently the sea has moved inland by kilometres, over salt
  marsh, mudflat and lagoon, and the tide runs further up every river. Cliffs
  and steep shores have lost little ground; their beaches have narrowed, their
  harbours been raised, and their low headlands become islands.
- **What is not under water.** Three metres drowns perhaps one per cent or so
  of the land, but it is the one per cent where a large share of humanity
  lived and most of its old record was kept. That is why the crisis reads as a
  catastrophe of archives as much as of acreage, and why the record keeps
  mentioning the archive: the *"heavily archived heart"*, the 2130s data-loss
  gap.

### The British lowlands (keeping exactly to the record)

- The record says the British Isles were *drowning*, that *large parts* came
  out of the crisis *uninhabitable*, and that the fragmentation took *"the
  low-lying, heavily archived heart"* (`the-commonwealth.md`,
  `new-london-space-habitat.md`). It names no county, river or city of
  England.
- **Derived reading.** In the 2130s and 2140s CE the global sea stood perhaps
  half a metre to a metre above ours (AR6's projections for the twenty-second
  century), not three. What made large parts of a country uninhabitable at
  that level was the surge on top of it, the failure of defences built for a
  lower sea, salt in the ground water and the loss of everything that made
  low ground insurable. *Uninhabitable* is the right word for land given up,
  not only for land under water. By 2626 CE, at three metres, much of that
  given-up land is permanently tidal.
- **Proposed, as the content of *the low-lying heart*:** the Thames estuary
  and the low parts of old London; the Fens and the Wash; the Somerset Levels
  and the Severn shore; the Humber lowlands; the Lancashire and Lincolnshire
  coastal plains. Southern and eastern England is subsiding slowly as the
  land rebounds in the north, so it took more than the global rise. These are
  names a chapter may use, and none is needed until one does.

## The two reconciliations

### Recovered, and still marked (proposed reading)

- **The tension.** New London: *"Earth recovered."* The repair page: *"not the
  planet that had existed before"*. The Crisis Centuries page: *"permanent
  marks that stewardship never reversed"*. The Commonwealth: *"most of the
  coastline its members came from"* is gone.
- **The reading.** **Earth recovered as a biosphere and as a place to live;
  its coastline was never restored, and was never going to be.** New London's
  sentence is about its own hedge: the catastrophe it insured against was
  Earth's *failure*, and that did not come. The physics in this note says why
  both can be true without strain. The air and the surface ocean answer
  repair within a century; ice sheets and the deep ocean do not. The repair
  could bring the temperature down and hold the chemistry; it could not put
  the ice back, so it could not lower the sea, so it rebuilt the coasts to
  live with the water.
- **New London's *"three centuries after they stopped"*.** The British Isles
  stopped *drowning*, in the page's sense of losing ground to the sea, when
  the fragmentation decades ended and the coasts were rebuilt to ride the
  water. The sea's slow rise since has been ridden, not suffered: a harbour
  re-set, not a town lost. Read that way the sentence stands beside Venice's
  *"continuing rise"*. No page needs to change.
- **No conflict found.** No page says the coastline was restored or the sea
  went down, and no page gives a figure that disagrees with this note's.

### Ireland and the British Isles (proposed reading)

- **The tension.** The record's British Isles came out of the crisis largely
  uninhabitable, and its only Earth shore in the present, shared by Threshold
  Station and the Marsh Causeway, carries Irish names: Carraig Bán, the Sceir
  Light, Dún Rí, Brother Daire's river.
- **The reading.** **The loss fell mainly on the English lowlands; Ireland's
  coasts were rebuilt to ride the water.** Every physical ground points the
  same way (derived): Ireland's coast is mostly rock, high, and indented, with
  few large areas within three metres of the sea; the north of the island is
  still rising slightly after the ice age, and the south is close to stable,
  where south-east England sinks; Greenland's loss raised the sea least on the
  coasts nearest the North Atlantic's top; and the weakened overturning left
  Ireland temperate and wet rather than hot or cold (*The Gulf Stream and
  Ireland*, above). Ireland's losses were real and local: estuary flats,
  reclaimed polders, coastal bog margins, low harbour towns. They were the
  kind of loss a rebuilt harbour and a moved village can answer.
- **Wording checked; no conflict.** `the-commonwealth.md` says *"Large parts
  of the British Isles"*, *"largely gone"* of the place the association was
  *of*, and *"most of the coastline its members came from"*;
  `new-london-space-habitat.md` says *"the British Isles were drowning"*, and
  its residents are *"descended from the British evacuation programmes"*.
  *Large parts* is not all; the members' coastline is that of the communities
  who joined a British association; an archipelago part of which drowned *was
  drowning*. The closest strain is *"largely gone"*, read here as the place
  the association was centred on, the low-lying heart, not every shore of the
  archipelago. The Celtic Union's absence from the Commonwealth is cultural
  and stated as such, so it neither needs nor contradicts a surviving Irish
  shore.
- **What is not established.** The record does not say the coast is in
  Ireland. Its names are Irish, and Dermot ruled that the coast stays unnamed.
  This reading explains why an Irish-named shore can be lived on in 2826; it
  does not place the station.

## The Irish coast that Threshold and the Causeway share

**Nothing here names the coast.** It describes what a few metres of rise and
a weakened overturning do to the kind of coast the record's names describe,
and checks the names against it.

- **The estuaries are longer, and the tide runs further** (derived). On a
  drowned river valley, three metres moves the head of the tide inland,
  sometimes by kilometres. Brother Daire's river, up which the tide runs
  twice a day below high ground at Dún Rí, is that kind of river. *Proposed:*
  the old river abbey and Dún Rí's high ground are where they are because
  high ground above a tidal river is what lasted.
- **The bog and marsh margins went under, or went salt** (derived). Low
  coastal bog, fen and grazing marsh just behind the shore takes the sea
  first: brackish, then salt marsh, then tidal flat. A causeway across such
  ground is built when the marsh is passable and broken when the sea comes
  into it. **The Marsh Causeway fits.** *Proposed:* the causeway was built
  across a coastal marsh after the crisis, by people crossing ground that was
  still becoming sea, and its ruin is that ground's later history. Its grove,
  three centuries older than the causeway (`dryadic-trees.md`), stands on a
  rise in the marsh that the water never reached: dryadic trees live eight
  centuries and more, so the oldest of the seven may predate the crisis.
- **Harbours moved, headlands became skerries** (derived). Three metres puts
  the old quays under, and a harbour is rebuilt higher or moved to deeper,
  steeper water. A low headland whose neck is drowned at high water becomes an
  island at every tide, then a rock: *sceir* is a skerry, a sea rock. **The
  Sceir Light fits**, as does Carraig Bán, a rock *"cut off from the mainland
  on a schedule a child can learn"*, of *"knife-edge rock and drowned coves"*:
  a tidal island at a higher sea, the coves drowned by name.
- **The light postdates the crisis** (derived from the dates). The Sceir
  Light's register is about two centuries old, so the light was lit around
  the 2620s UCSD (2420s CE), nearly three centuries after the fragmentation.
  *Proposed:* it was built for a coast that had finished rearranging itself
  enough to be charted again, at the end of a spit of shingle (`s01e01c03`)
  that is itself a product of the higher sea working the shore.
- **The Board's tide gauge holds the rise** (proposed). A gauge kept for two
  centuries on a sea rising a few millimetres a year has recorded perhaps half
  a metre of it. Nobody on the shore remarks on it. It is the same kind of
  record the clock corrections are: a slow number kept by people who were
  never asked to look for anything, and a chapter may let the two sit side by
  side without comment.
- **The weather is the Gulf Stream's** (derived from *The Gulf Stream and
  Ireland*). Mild, wet, windy; westerly gales in winter on a sea that runs
  higher at every surge; dry spells in a summer half a degree warmer than
  ours. The dock crews' *falling tide* and *the wind had not eased at all*
  (`s01e01c03`) are this coast's ordinary weather, not a crisis's.
- **What a picture shows** (proposed, for prompts). Grey rock and shingle;
  salt marsh and tidal flats in the low ground; a raised modern sea wall with
  older, lower stonework drowned beside it at high water; small islands that
  were headlands; green fields above the marsh line. Never a submerged town,
  never a tower in the sea, never a ruined city skyline.

## Traps

- **The drowned world.** Half-submerged skyscrapers, rowing boats among
  rooftops. Three metres does this to waterfronts, marshes and deltas, not to
  cities on high ground, and the repair has had five centuries to clear what it
  did. The record's word is *rebuilt*.
- **Dystopia.** A ruined, rationed, hostile Earth with its people huddled under
  domes. The record's Earth is *"healthier than at any point since the early
  industrial era"* in its unmanaged biomes, holds preserves, runs a working
  repair, and keeps Venice. The tone rule applies: the dark fact is the
  coastline that is gone and the amphibians that did not come back, hinted,
  never shown as a wasteland (`CLAUDE.md`, *Tone*).
- **The pristine Earth restored.** The opposite error: ice back, sea down,
  reefs wild, the twentieth-century planet returned. The record refuses it
  (*"not the planet that had existed before"*, *"The reefs are scaffolded, not
  wild"*), and the physics refuses it more firmly.
- **An ideology for the repair.** No page names one, and none may
  (`CLAUDE.md`, *no named political ideology*). The repair was *"coalitions of
  surviving governments and cooperatives"*, consolidated through the USC and
  now SSDC's protocols; it is described by what it did, never by a doctrine's
  name, and never as the triumph or failure of a system.
- **The frozen Europe.** The AMOC-collapse case of film and headline. This note
  does not take it (open item below), and the Gulf Stream does not stop either
  way.
- **The weather as plot, or precision beyond the science.** No superstorm of
  the week, no tipping point reached on the page, and no sea level to the
  centimetre. Earth's climate in 2826 is a steady, slightly changed one, and

## Where this goes

**Nothing is published from this file.** An Earth lore page, or a section of
an existing one, is a later step, taken only when a chapter set on Earth
needs a figure or a coast, and then by extension of the pages cited above,
never by rewriting them. The smallest such step would be one paragraph on
`climate-repair-and-terraforming.md` giving the plateau and the sea, marked as
an extension.

**For image prompts.** Any prompt for an Earth coast, Threshold's shore, the
Causeway, Carraig Bán, Venice's lagoon or a delta starts from this file. Earth
coasts are photographable places in our own time, so the image rule applies
first (`CLAUDE.md`, *Images*): Dermot's own frames of an Irish coast before
any generator, and a generated image only for what no camera can show, such as
a raised sea wall with drowned stonework beside it, or a platform city.
Vocabulary to start from: *grey rock and shingle, salt marsh, tidal flat, a
low skerry with a small light, a raised sea wall with older stonework drowned

skyline, rooftops in water, apocalyptic, post-apocalyptic, dystopian, domes,
wasteland, ice age, frozen sea, 3D render, CGI*.

**Both ruled 20 October 2026, as recommended** (Dermot, verbatim: *"Yes, agreed"*): the plateau was drawn down slowly to about 1.5–2 °C (item 1, B), and the AMOC weakened by a quarter to a half and never collapsed (item 2, A). The options below are kept as the record of what was weighed.

Only genuine dilemmas are put here. Each is also listed in

### 1. Has the drawdown lowered the plateau, or only held it?

The record says the repair works *"toward a target the world had already
demonstrated it could hold"* and holds the chemistry *"where the repair left
it"*. Both readings below stand beside every page; they differ in what a
chapter may say about the weather and the ice.

- **A. Held at about 2 °C.** The plateau is where the crisis left it. Summer
  Arctic ice never returns; glaciers keep shrinking; the restoration doctrine's
  target is chemistry, not temperature.
- **B. Drawn down slowly, now about 1.5–2 °C (recommended).** The drawdown has
  taken off part of the overshoot and is still working. It fits *repair* and
  the doctrine's target, it keeps *"not finished"* true, and it changes nothing
  that the coastline answers to: the sea still rises, because the ice lags.
- **C. Back to pre-industrial.** Refused, as the pristine-Earth trap and by
  the physics while permafrost still thaws. Listed so it is refused on purpose.

### 2. The AMOC: weakened, or collapsed?

The one genuine fork in the ocean. Collapse is AR6's named uncertain tail, and
a story could take it.

- **A. Weakened by a quarter to a half, never collapsed (recommended).** The
  conservative reading his direction asks for; Ireland stays temperate and
  maritime; the reconciliation above stands on it.
- **B. Collapsed during the crisis, partly recovered since.** Possible in
  models with an overshoot. A harsher crisis in north-west Europe and a sharp
  sea-level jump on the American coast; Ireland's survival would need more
  explaining.
- **C. Collapsed and not recovered.** North-west Europe colder than ours in a
  warmer world. It breaks the *rebuildable* reading and leans toward the
  frozen-Europe trap. Not recommended.
