## The orbital ring of *The Long White Trail*

Dermot, verbatim, numbered *4* in his message:

> The orbital ring is a chain of thousands of individual habitat stations, not a smooth band. From the terrace at dawn it appears as a fine beaded arc of pinpoint lights low across the southern sky, like a string of pearls catching the first sun, always in the same position. Seen from orbit and the docking approach, it is a vast necklace of distinct stations receding into the curve of the planet, with Station Lúnasa as one bead among thousands.

**What it answers.** The ring is one of the trailer's *new particulars*, the fourth of the five departures listed in `src/journal/the-long-white-trail.md` (*a ring of habitats circling the whole world and visible from the ground*), and the Director briefs in `video-prompts.md` were written because the generator invented it with nothing to go on. This says what the ring is and how it looks. The list his *4* belongs to is not in this repository; the session that recorded this read it as that fourth departure.

**The reading, in four points.**

1. **A chain, not a band.** The ring is thousands of separate free-flying habitat stations sharing one orbit, not a continuous structure. Nothing joins it to the ground, so it sits inside the no-towers ruling of 10 October 2026, and nothing joins the stations to each other either.
2. **The orbit is geostationary, or close to it.** *Always in the same position* settles it. Any ring in the equatorial plane draws a fixed arc on the sky, but its stations crawl along that arc unless their period is one day. So the stations hang still only at geostationary height, about 35,800 km up on an Earth-sized world. That also fits *low*. From a terrace at 52–54° N, Ireland's latitudes and the obvious reading of Carrigbawn's bay if the world is Earth, the arc stands about 28–30° above the southern horizon at its highest, about 38,600 km away, and falls to the horizon east and west. A ring below about 4,200 km could not be seen from that latitude at all, and a lower ring visible from there would sit lower still and crawl.
3. **Beads, not a thread, and that is arithmetic, not art.** The geostationary circle is about 265,000 km round. Two thousand stations would stand about 130 km apart, about 12 minutes of arc as seen from the terrace, and five thousand about 50 km apart, about 5 minutes. The eye resolves about 1 minute, so the stations read as separate points. Toward the horizon, where the distance grows and the arc foreshortens, they crowd together and the beads run into a fine line. A brief or prompt may use that.
4. **Lit before the ground.** At that height the stations stand in sunlight through the whole night except for a short eclipse near midnight around the equinoxes. Before dawn the terrace is still dark and the ring is already lit. *Catching the first sun* is right as the observer sees it, because the pearls light up before the bay does.

From the docking approach the necklace curves away on both sides and passes behind the planet's limb. Lúnasa is one bead among thousands, so it is a habitat station of the ring and not a stand-alone station.
