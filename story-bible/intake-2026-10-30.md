## The orbital ring of *The Long White Trail*

Dermot, verbatim, numbered *4* in his message:

> The orbital ring is a chain of thousands of individual habitat stations, not a smooth band. From the terrace at dawn it appears as a fine beaded arc of pinpoint lights low across the southern sky, like a string of pearls catching the first sun, always in the same position. Seen from orbit and the docking approach, it is a vast necklace of distinct stations receding into the curve of the planet, with Station Lúnasa as one bead among thousands.

**What it answers.** The ring is one of the trailer's *new particulars*, the fourth of the five departures listed in `src/journal/the-long-white-trail.md` (*a ring of habitats circling the whole world and visible from the ground*), and the Director briefs in `video-prompts.md` were written because the generator invented it with nothing to go on. This says what the ring is and how it looks. The list his *4* belongs to is not in this repository; the session that recorded this read it as that fourth departure.

**The reading, in four points.**

1. **A chain, not a band.** The ring is thousands of separate free-flying habitat stations sharing one orbit, not a continuous structure. Nothing joins it to the ground, so it sits inside the no-towers ruling of 10 October 2026, and nothing joins the stations to each other either.
2. **The orbit is geostationary, or close to it.** *Always in the same position* settles it. Any ring in the equatorial plane draws a fixed arc on the sky, but its stations crawl along that arc unless their period is one day. So the stations hang still only at geostationary height, about 35,800 km up on an Earth-sized world. That also fits *low*. From a terrace at 52–54° N, Ireland's latitudes and the obvious reading of Carrigbawn's bay if the world is Earth, the arc stands about 28–30° above the southern horizon at its highest, about 38,600 km away, and falls to the horizon east and west. A ring below about 4,200 km could not be seen from that latitude at all, and a lower ring visible from there would sit lower still and crawl.
3. **Beads, not a thread, and that is arithmetic, not art.** The geostationary circle is about 265,000 km round. Two thousand stations would stand about 130 km apart, about 12 minutes of arc as seen from the terrace, and five thousand about 50 km apart, about 5 minutes. The eye resolves about 1 minute, so the stations read as separate points. Toward the horizon, where the distance grows and the arc foreshortens, they crowd together and the beads run into a fine line. A brief or prompt may use that.
4. **Lit before the ground.** At that height the stations stand in sunlight through the whole night except for a short eclipse near midnight around the equinoxes. Before dawn the terrace is still dark and the ring is already lit. *Catching the first sun* is right as the observer sees it, because the pearls light up before the bay does.

From the docking approach the necklace curves away on both sides and passes behind the planet's limb. Lúnasa is one bead among thousands, so it is a habitat station of the ring and not a stand-alone station.

**What it does not do.** No page under `src/` changes. The Journal entry still lists the ring as an invention of the film, and that stays true until Dermot says otherwise. Three things are left open and are in `open-questions.md`. Is this canon, or the settled look of the ring for a remake of the film and future briefs? Which world does the ring circle? And how does Lúnasa stand toward Threshold's forty seconds, which the Journal names as the reason a second station with them would unsettle the record?

## The ring's layout, for films

Dermot asked for *"The ideal number, size and role individual stations inbetween the major space habitats"*, was given the layout below (Opus, in the session), and answered, verbatim:

> Yes, add it to D7

**The layout.** It stays under ruling 1, so it is how the ring looks on screen and not canon.

- **Number.** There are 3,600 slots of 0.1° each, which is today's geostationary slot width, about 74 km. One major habitat sits every 1°, 360 in all. Nine smaller stations sit between each pair, about 3,240 in all, with a few slots left empty for clearance.
- **Size.** A major habitat is 1.5–2 km across and spins for 1 g at about 1 rpm, a rim about 900 m from the axis, for 10,000–50,000 people. A smaller station is 100–300 m, mostly not spinning, with tens to a few hundred people aboard.
- **Roles of the smaller stations.** Transfer and docking hubs, since a javelin's job ends at a parking orbit; orbital compute complexes (`src/lore/orbital-compute-complexes.md` already places them about Earth), mostly radiator wing turned edge-on to the sun; depots and yards; station-keeping and traffic posts.
- **What the terrace sees.** A degree of the ring is about 1.1° of sky, so the majors are about two moon-widths apart. At dawn, with the sun side-on, a major is about magnitude −1 and a 200 m station about +4, so as the sky pales the smaller beads go out and the majors stay. Lúnasa is one of the majors.

D7 carries it in its instruction, its first and fifth shots, its judging line and its grounding.

## A rail launch, and Orbital Five-O's page

Dermot asked to *"consider if the spaceplanre with rail is a valid option for villages"*, and then *"Does Five-O need a rename or a rethink?"*. Opus put options to him in the session, and he answered, verbatim:

> Option 1 for Five-O, option 1 for the rail

**The rail (option 1, a lore extension).** `src/lore/ascent-javelins.md` gains *The Rail*. A few small coastal sites throw a standard javelin along a level track two or three kilometres long, laid beside the landing runway and running out over water. The craft leaves the sled at a little under the speed of sound, about 270 m/s, which takes 1.2–2.5 km at 1.5–3 g. The physics behind it: that speed is about 3% of what orbit needs, so the rail saves only the ballistic boost off the cradle, and its real gain is a smaller site. Faster is not on, since 600 m/s needs 6 km of track and a boom along the ground, and 2 km/s needs 68 km. A rail site is a regional port that a coast's villages use, never one per village, because propellant, certified crews and weather holds need a region's launch rate. The track is never elevated or on a slope, under the no-towers ruling. The craft keeps its name. D7 keeps the trailer's rail across the bay, which the rail ruling had been offered to allow.

**Five-O (option 1, a clarification).** The name and the concept stand. The concept already fits the peaceable present and the shadow-shaped rule. The name is the StarQuest editions' site name, so a rename would change a live domain's identity. The one change: `src/lore/orbital-habitats-compact.md` dropped its clause naming the old Earth series the name nods at. That clause cited a non-public-domain fiction in the Archive's voice, against the 17 October rule. The real-world echo stays where it is allowed, in `story-bible/` and the Journal. No in-world origin for the name is given (option 2, not taken).

**Why rails stay rare.** Asked *"Is the rail a better design that the vertical javelin or just different?"*, Opus answered that it is different. It is the better passenger launcher where a site can have one: smaller engines, an abort on the track, and seconds of acceleration rather than a minute and a half. The cradle is better for any heading, heavy cargo, heavy worlds, awkward sites and more launches a day. Dermot, verbatim, to adding that to the page: *"Yes, add the sentence"*. The Rail now says why rails stay rare: one heading, a lower payload ceiling, few suitable coasts.
